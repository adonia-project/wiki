# Guaji Society Notes

Internal working notes on Guajicutea's social hierarchy, ruler list, and political structure.

## Social hierarchy (post-unification, mature form)

The hierarchy has a dual-track structure at the top — secular and sacred authority mirror each other — then transitions to economic specializations below.

| Tier | Secular track | Sacred track |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | **Cacique Má** (elected king) | **Bohíque Má / Warí** (high priestess) |
| 2 | **Nitainos** (warriors, trade chiefs, enforcers) | **Bohíques** (priests, healers, scribes, calendar-keepers) |
| 3 | **Craftsmen** (goldsmiths, potters, canoe builders, stone carvers) | **Merchants** (trade specialists, canoe captains) |
| 4 | **Fishermen** (coastal, reef, deep water) | **Farmers** (conuco tenders, harvesters) |
| 5 | **Naborías** (unskilled peasantry — general labor, clearing, hauling, building) | |
| 6 | **Slaves** (captured foreigners — skilled rank higher, children born free) | |

### Labor evolution

1. **Originally communal** — everyone farms, everyone fishes, everyone builds. No permanent specialization.
2. **Agricultural intensification** — as root-crop farming matures, surplus allows some people to specialize full-time (craftsmen, religious specialists).
3. **Wealth accumulation** — trade (gold, ornaments, volcanic stone) and mining create concentrated wealth. Chiefs can support permanent warriors (nitainos).
4. **Military emergence** — wealth allows building war canoe fleets. Warriors enable raiding other islands for slaves and goods.
5. **Hierarchy hardens** — by peak period (1300s–1400s), full six-tier system with hereditary classes, specialized craftsmen, merchant fleets, and slave labor.

### Slavery

- **Source**: captured foreigners from raided islands and conquered/coerced territories. Not domestic — Guaji commoners are not enslaved.
- **Non-hereditary**: children of slaves are born free. Slavery is a one-generation status. This makes it a population absorption mechanism as much as a labor system — captured people's children become Guaji.
- **Skilled vs. unskilled**: a captured canoe builder, goldsmith, or weaver has higher status and better treatment than an unskilled captive. Skilled slaves work at their craft; unskilled slaves do field labor, hauling, and mining.
- **Post-disaster role**: after the yukubara (516 CE) and the great hurricane (1143 CE), the island lost population. Raiding for slaves was a way to replenish labor and people.
- **Not chattel slavery**: slaves are not property in the same way as livestock or gold. They are obligated laborers with defined rights (food, shelter, eventually freedom for their children). Closer to debt-bondage or war-captive servitude.

### Priestess naming convention

Every **Bohíque Má** (high priestess) takes the name **Warí** ("moon") when she assumes office. Her personal name is set aside — she becomes the moon's voice on earth. She IS the office, not the person.

- The name connects to her domain: moon, calendar, tides, cycles, hidden knowledge
- Every reference to "Warí" in documents could mean the current priestess — the name is timeless
- This creates a contrast with the Cacique Má: each king takes a **unique symbolic name** reflecting his reign. The king is change and action; the priestess is continuity and memory. The king acts; the moon endures.
- The personal names of priestesses are not recorded in Bikumaku — only the title "Warí" appears. This is intentional: the office transcends the individual.

## Governance and election system

### Village level
- Each village is headed by a **village cacique** (a man)
- Village caciques are **chosen by the eldest women** of the village — the matriarchs who trace land and kinship
- In practice, the eldest woman usually selects the **eldest son** of the current chief — succession is normally hereditary within the chiefly family
- Exception: if the chiefly family is **cursed** (see below), the eldest woman may choose a different man from outside the family
- The man governs daily affairs (labor organization, dispute resolution, trade, defense), but the women who selected him can remove him
- This is the surviving matriarchal foundation: men hold the visible position, women control who gets it

### The priestess obligation
- Every chief is **expected to bear a daughter who will become a priestess** (a lower bohíque, trained in Bikumaku, calendar, and ritual)
- This is the price of chiefly power — a chief gives a daughter to the sacred track in exchange for holding the secular position
- If a chief fails to produce a daughter for the priestesshood, his family is considered **cursed** — the three powers are offended by a chief who takes without giving
- A cursed family is unlikely to continue ruling — the eldest women of the village will select a new chief from a different family
- This creates a structural link between the secular and sacred tracks: chiefly families feed daughters into the priestesshood, and the priestesshood validates chiefly authority

### Island level — election of the Cacique Má
- **Lower priestesses** (regional religious authorities) elect the **Bohíque Má (Warí)** — the high priestess is chosen by the priestly class from within their own ranks
- The **Warí herself** selects the **lower priestesses** — she appoints the regional religious authorities, often from the daughters given by chiefly families
- **Village caciques** (lower chiefs) vote to elect the **Cacique Má** (king/head chief) — the secular leader is chosen by the secular leaders
- The **Bohíque Má (Warí)** confirms the king's election — her blessing is required for legitimacy
- A king cannot rule without the priestess's approval. The election gives him the secular mandate; the priestess gives him the sacred one.

### Summary of the power structure
1. Eldest women of each village → choose village cacique (normally eldest son, unless family is cursed)
2. Village caciques → vote for Cacique Má (king)
3. Chiefly families → expected to give daughters to the priestesshood
4. Warí → appoints lower priestesses (often from chiefly daughters)
5. Lower priestesses → elect the next Warí
6. Warí → confirms the Cacique Má's election with her blessing

The system is circular and self-reinforcing: chiefly families provide daughters to the priestesshood, the priestesshood validates chiefly authority, and the eldest women (who may themselves be matriarchs of chiefly families) select the chiefs. Women control entry at every level — through the eldest women at village level and through the priestesshood at island level.

### When king and priestess disagree
- **The priestess generally has the upper hand.** The sacred authority outranks the secular.
- The king commands people; the priestess commands the system that made him king — the calendar, the records, the genealogies, the election process
- If the priestess withdraws her blessing, the king's legitimacy is questioned. Village caciques may withdraw support.
- In practice, most reigns operate through cooperation — the king handles trade, war, and daily governance; the priestess handles calendar, ritual, records, and succession disputes

### The usurpation — Guani Waku (Gold Cycle, ~930 CE)

**[Documented in calendar corridor + oral tradition]**

During the reign of **Guani Waku** (Gold Cycle, 912–938 CE), the king attempted to break the priestess's authority by ordering her assassination. Guani Waku was the wealthiest ruler of his era — gold from Mina Yaruma was flowing, trade was strong, and he commanded a loyal corps of nitainos. He believed his wealth and military power made the priestess unnecessary.

**The plot**: Guani Waku sent trusted warriors to kill Warí in her ceremonial chambers near the catacombs. The assassination succeeded — Warí was killed.

**The aftermath**: the plot was discovered within days. The bohíques, who controlled the catacombs and the calendar, refused to recognize the king's authority. Village caciques — who had been chosen by the eldest women — withdrew their support. The king's own nitainos, horrified by the sacrilege (killing the moon's voice was an offense against all three powers), turned against him.

**The king's death**: Guani Waku was killed — the oral tradition says he was "judged by the three powers" and executed as a sacrifice. The exact method is disputed: some accounts say the bohíques performed a ritual execution; others say his own warriors killed him; still others say he was left in a cave to face the underworld alone. What is certain is that both the king and the priestess died.

**The restoration**: a new Warí was installed — the name passed to the next priestess, as it always does. A new Cacique Má was elected. The event was carved into the calendar corridor as a warning: a glyph showing a fallen king and a fallen priestess, with the priestess glyph reborn (a new moon rising beside the fallen one). The message: the king is mortal and replaceable; the moon always returns.

**Legacy**: the usurpation attempt was the only time a king tried to seize absolute power. It failed catastrophically and reinforced the dual-authority system for the remaining centuries. No later king attempted the same. The story became a cautionary tale told to every new Cacique Má at his installation: "Remember Guani Waku. The moon cannot be killed."

### Ruler list adjustment
- Guani Waku's reign is shorter than originally listed: ~912–930 CE (the assassination plot cuts his reign short)
- A brief interregnum follows (~930–932 CE) — no king while the priestess is restored and the system stabilizes
- The next ruler (Wirá Kuru, Storm Rest) takes office ~932 CE, beginning the recovery from the crisis

## Land ownership

### Early period (pre-1143 CE) — matrilineal
- Land passes through **women**. Women are the agricultural managers; they control who farms which plots.
- Land is not privately owned — it is **held by matrilineal kin groups**. A woman's daughters inherit the right to farm specific plots.
- Village caciques manage land allocation on behalf of the community, but the eldest women decide which families get which fields.
- This is the foundation of women's economic power: they don't hold political office, but they control the food supply.

### After the great hurricane (1143 CE) — consolidation
- The hurricane destroyed villages, displaced populations, and disrupted the matrilineal land system
- Rebuilding required centralized organization — village caciques who coordinated reconstruction gained control of land allocation
- Over the following generations, land shifted from **matrilineal kin groups** to **chieftain family ownership**
- Chieftain families (patrilineal) began treating land as their property rather than community-held trust
- Women's control of land eroded — they retained ceremonial and ritual authority but lost economic power
- The priestess retained sacred authority (calendar, catacombs, Bikumaku) but the secular domain (land, agriculture, trade) shifted to male chiefs
- This is the key moment in the matriarchal-to-patriarchal transition: not a sudden overthrow, but a disaster-enabled consolidation

### Late period (1300s–1500 CE) — chieftain land
- Land is owned by chieftain families, inherited patrilineally
- Naborías work chieftain land as tenant laborers, not as kin-group members with rights
- The old matrilineal system survives only in ritual contexts — certain sacred groves and ceremonial plots are still "women's land"
- The priestess's authority over sacred sites persists, but her authority over economic land is gone

## Marriage customs

### Early period (pre-1000 CE) — polyandry
- Women could have **multiple husbands**. This made sense in a society where men were away at sea or on trade voyages for long periods — the household needed multiple men to contribute labor, protection, and children
- Polyandry gave women significant household power — she chose which men to marry and which to release
- Children belonged to the mother's lineage (matrilineal), not to a specific father
- Husbands could be fishermen, traders, warriors, or craftsmen — the household was a flexible economic unit

### Transition period (1000s–1300s CE) — shift to monogamy
- As trade routes stabilized and men were more present (not away at sea for months), the need for multiple husbands decreased
- Wealth accumulation made paternity matter — chieftain families wanted clear inheritance lines, which required exclusive marriage
- The growth of the tributary system also contributed: marriage partners from tributary islands became a form of tribute, and these marriages were 1:1 diplomatic alliances
- Polyandry gradually gave way to **monogamy** over several generations, not through a law but through cultural shift

### Late period (1300s–1500 CE) — monogamy
- 1:1 marriages are the norm
- Marriage is both a domestic arrangement and a diplomatic tool — tributary islands send marriage partners as part of tribute
- Chieftain families arrange marriages for political and economic alliances
- Women's household power is reduced from the polyandry era — one husband, patrilineal inheritance, chieftain land ownership
- The priestess's naming tradition (Warí) is the last surviving expression of the old matriarchal order.

## Education and apprenticeship

Bikumaku is specialist priestly writing — not mass literacy. Most Guaji learn through apprenticeship and oral transmission. There is no formal schooling; children learn by embedding into households and crews.

### Apprenticeship system

When a family gives a child to apprenticeship, they **lose the child**. The child moves into the master's household, eats the master's food, sleeps in the master's house, and works the master's tasks. Their loyalty shifts — the master becomes a second parent. The birth family might see them at festivals, but the child's daily life is now with the trade.

This is a significant social transaction, not casual lending. The family gives away a child to secure their future and fulfill a social obligation. The child is "lost" to the birth family but "gained" by the trade. The master receives free labor for years and eventually a successor.

**Gender segregation:** Boys apprentice to male masters, girls to female masters. No mixed apprenticeships.

### Apprenticeship tracks

**Male tracks:**
- **Goldsmith** — fire, metal, trade connection. High-status craft.
- **Canoe-builder** — large woodwork, sea connection. Essential for trade.
- **Stone-carver** — quarry work, heavy labor. Bikumaku carving is specialized training within this.
- **Merchant/sailor** — joins a canoe crew or merchant household. Learns navigation, trade routes, negotiation, other languages.
- **Fisherman** — usually learned from father, but can apprentice to a boat captain.
- **Warrior (nitaino)** — training in combat, canoe warfare, leadership. Usually for chiefly families, but commoners can rise through skill.
- **Male bohíque** — healer, ritual assistant, works under priestess authority. Lower status than priestesses.

**Female tracks:**
- **Weaver** — cotton and fiber work. Hammocks, nets, clothing, trade goods.
- **Basket-maker** — carrying baskets, storage containers, fish traps.
- **Potter** — hand-built vessels (coiled, not wheel-thrown). Storage jars, cooking pots.
- **Priestess (bohíque)** — formal training in Bikumaku, calendar, ritual, healing, cosmology. Most formalized education — years of memorization, initiation rituals, sacred knowledge. Lower priestesses train initiates; Warí appoints lower priestesses.

**Learned at home (no formal apprenticeship):**
- **Farming** — children learn conuco work from parents. Girls manage plots; boys clear land.
- **Household management** — girls learn cooking, child-rearing, household organization from mothers.
- **Basic fishing** — boys learn net-mending, shore fishing from fathers.

### Age and lifecycle

- **Childhood (0-10)** — children stay with parents, learn basic skills, participate in daily work.
- **Apprenticeship placement (10-14)** — family decides child's track. Child moves to master's household.
- **Apprenticeship (10-20)** — child learns trade through work and observation. No formal tests — master judges readiness.
- **Recognition (18-25)** — master declares apprentice ready. No universal ritual — some crafts have markings or ceremonies, others just informal release.
- **Practice (25+)** — former apprentice works independently, may take own apprentices, may return to birth family or stay with trade.

For the religious track, there ARE formal stages:
- **First marking (puberty)** — initiate receives first body paint or scarification, enters training
- **Learning (10-20)** — memorization, Bikumaku instruction, ritual practice
- **Ordination (20-25)** — recognized as bohíque, can perform rituals
- **Seniority (30+)** — may become regional religious authority, train new initiates

## Settlement organization

### Village types

**Small villages (most settlements):**
- 10-30 bohíos (round thatched houses)
- Population 50-200 people
- Cluster around a central plaza
- Cacique's house larger, faces the plaza
- Storage pits near houses
- Agricultural plots surrounding
- No fortifications — open layout
- Canoe landing on nearest river or coast

**Larger villages (regional centers):**
- 50-100 bohíos
- Population 300-800 people
- Larger central plaza — used for ball games, markets, ceremonies
- Cacique's house on raised platform, more elaborate
- Guest houses for visiting traders
- Resident bohíque (priest)
- Wooden palisade and watchtower
- Controlled entrance
- Warehouse district for trade goods

**Main ports (Yukubá, Waribá):**
- Significant permanent population (2,000-3,500 at peak)
- Fortified — stone walls, watchtowers, controlled gates
- Warehouse district for trade goods and tribute storage
- Guest house compound for foreign traders
- Cacique Má's residence (large stone and wood complex)
- Religious structures (shrines, temple platforms)
- Canoe harbor with controlled access
- Permanent garrison of nitainos (warriors)

### Layout principles

**Central plaza:**
- Heart of every settlement
- Open packed-earth space
- Used for ceremonies, ball games, markets, public gatherings
- Cacique's house faces the plaza, largest structure
- Religious structures nearby (shrines, bohíque's house)

**House cluster:**
- Bohíos arranged in rough circles or rows around the plaza
- Each bohío houses an extended family (parents, children, maybe grandparents)
- Smaller structures for storage, cooking, menstruation seclusion

**Perimeter:**
- Agricultural plots outside the settlement
- Canoe storage near water
- Defensive structures (where present) at the edge
- Controlled entrance/gate

### Fortifications

**Threat model:**
- Never invaded by foreign powers
- Main threat: tributary rebellions — islands refusing to pay, killing tax collectors
- Occasional counter-raids from angry tributaries
- Defense against punitive expeditions, not conquest

This means fortifications are deterrents and symbols of power, not desperate military infrastructure.

**Materials:**

**Wood:**
- Palisades — vertical logs set in a trench, lashed together
- Watchtowers — elevated platforms for sentries
- Gates — controlled entry points
- Problem: rots quickly in tropics (5-10 years), needs regular replacement
- Used in all periods, but especially early

**Earth:**
- Banks and ditches — earthen ramparts with a ditch in front
- More durable than wood, but erodes in heavy rain
- Used in combination with wooden palisades

**Volcanic stone (andesite, basalt):**
- Available locally from quarries
- Hard, durable, but difficult to work without metal tools
- Used for foundations, platforms, some walls
- Religious structures (catacomb calendar corridor)

**Limestone:**
- Available from uplifted coral reefs and marine deposits
- Softer than volcanic stone, easier to carve
- Used increasingly in late period for fortifications and elite structures
- More durable than wood, less labor than volcanic stone

**Fortification evolution:**

**Early period (500s-800s CE):**
- Wooden palisades at larger villages
- Earth banks and ditches
- Simple watchtowers
- Minimal permanent fortifications

**Middle period (800s-1100s CE):**
- Stone foundations for cacique's houses
- Some stone watchtowers at main ports
- More elaborate wooden palisades
- Religious structures in stone (catacombs)

**Late period (1100s-1500s CE):**
- Limestone fortification walls at Yukubá and Waribá
- Stone watchtowers with wooden platforms
- Permanent garrison structures
- Fortified warehouses for trade goods and tribute
- Elite residences in stone and wood

By peak period (1400s), the two main ports have:
- Limestone perimeter walls (2-3m high, 1m thick)
- Stone watchtowers at corners and gate
- Controlled gate with guard house
- Fortified warehouse district
- Garrison barracks for nitainos

These are enough to deter tributary raids and project power, but not siege fortifications — Guajicutea was never under sustained attack.

### Tributary conflicts

**Pattern:**
1. Tax collectors from Guajicutea visit tributary island to collect tribute
2. Tributary community refuses, kills the collectors
3. Word reaches Guajicutea — cacique and Warí decide on response
4. War canoe fleet dispatched — punitive raid, not conquest
5. Raid burns village, takes captives, installs cooperative chief or demands higher tribute
6. Return to Guajicutea

**Frequency:** Occasional — most years tribute flows smoothly. Rebellion maybe once every 10-20 years.

**Fortifications role:**
- Protect main ports from counter-raids
- Secure collected tribute before redistribution
- Project power — a fortified port says "we are organized, we are strong"
- Deter rebellion by making raids costly

**Counter-raids from tributaries:**
- Small-scale — a few war canoes
- Target coastal villages, not main ports
- Steal goods, take captives, assert independence
- Met with punitive response from Guajicutea

The fortifications at Yukubá and Waribá are primarily about controlling the tributary system, not defending against foreign invasion.

### Payment and obligation

The child's labor IS the payment. The family pays nothing; the master receives free work for years. The master provides food, shelter, training, and eventually the child's future. It balances out: the master invests resources in a child who works for free, and eventually produces a skilled adult who can continue the trade.

If the master sends the child back early (failed apprenticeship), the family receives a child with no skills and lost years. This is a risk. Alternatively, the failed apprentice might stay as a permanent servant — not skilled enough to practice, but useful for labor.

### Social mobility

Apprenticeship is a path upward. A naboría's child can become a craftsman or merchant through apprenticeship. But it requires:
- A family willing to give up a child
- A master willing to take them (usually requires some connection — kinship, friendship, debt)
- The child's aptitude

Most apprenticeships probably stay within the same class — craftsmen's children become craftsmen — but the path exists.

For the religious track, chiefly families are expected to give daughters (the priestess obligation). But a commoner girl showing spirit-sensitivity or healing aptitude might also be given to the priestesshood. This is a more significant jump — a naboría's daughter becoming a bohíque.

### What happens when the master dies

If the master dies before the apprentice is ready:
- Apprentice might transfer to another master (if one can be found)
- Or be released early, partially trained
- Or stay with the master's household as a servant

For the religious track, the priestesshood is continuous — if a lower priestess dies, her initiates transfer to another. Warí oversees all religious training.

## Warfare

### Character
Guaji warfare is **defensive and extractive**, not conquest-based:

- **Defensive**: protecting coasts from raiders, fortifying settlements, watching for approaching fleets. Guajicutea is the largest island in the eastern archipelago — it is both a target and a defender.
- **Extractive**: raiding other islands for slaves, goods, and resources. Not territorial conquest — the Guaji don't hold land on other islands. They raid, take, and leave.
- **Naval, not territorial**: power projection is through war canoes, not land armies. The fleet is the military.

### Evolution
- **Early period (pre-700s)**: occasional raiding, mostly small-scale. Canoe fleets used for trade and fishing, not dedicated warfare.
- **Middle period (700s–1000s)**: increasing raiding as wealth grows. War canoe fleets become permanent. Nitainos emerge as a warrior class.
- **Tributary period (1300s onward)**: instead of raiding and taking, Guajicutea threatens to raid and receives tribute. More efficient and more stable. Some eastern islands become tributaries.

### Tributary system (1300s onward)

By the 1300s, Guajicutea has established **tributary relationships** with smaller islands in the **Greater Capuyaquiran** (eastern archipelago):

- **Tribute goods**: cotton, shells, pearls, specialty woods, food, marriage partners, raw materials
- **In exchange**: protection from other raiders, access to Guajicutea's trade network (including Tapuya/mainland routes), religious authority (Bikumaku-trained bohíques may serve tributary communities)
- **Maintenance**: regular visits by war canoes, trade partnerships (tribute = price of access), threat of raiding if tribute stops
- **Thalassocracy**: sea-based power network, not territorial empire. Guajicutea controls sea lanes and trade access, not land.
- **Scale**: probably 3–8 smaller islands as tributaries. Not the entire archipelago — the furthest islands are too distant to coerce.

### Notes on the ruler list

- **Reign lengths** average ~26 years. Some longer (30+), some shorter (20–22). No reigns under 20 years — the succession seems relatively stable.
- **Repeated names**: some names recur (Guani Waku, Yabí Waku, Warí Waku) — later rulers may take the name of an earlier notable one, a common dynastic practice.
- **The priestess line**: every reign has a "Warí." We don't list individual priestesses because the name is always the same and personal names are not recorded. Notable priestesses would be identified by events — e.g., "the Warí who recorded the great hurricane" or "the Warí who standardized Bikumaku."
- **Pre-unification rulers**: the Yukurí and Caracurí each had their own caciques before 516 CE. These are not listed — their names are lost or preserved only in oral tradition.
- **[Oral tradition]**: oral histories may reference additional rulers or alternative names. The list above represents the **documented succession** preserved in the catacomb calendar corridor and later Bikumaku tablets.
