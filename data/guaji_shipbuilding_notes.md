# Pre-colonial Shipbuilding in Guajicutea — Internal Reference

Internal notes for the history of Guaji watercraft, from dugout canoes to the Kanu Maku class. Not synced to wiki. Source material for future wiki article(s).

## Overview

Guaji shipbuilding evolved through four phases over roughly 1500 years, driven by trade expansion, naval warfare, and the need for larger cargo capacity. The technology remained stone-and-copper-tool based throughout the pre-colonial period — no iron fasteners were ever used. The critical innovations were sewn-plank construction (breaking the single-tree size limit), canoe-towed barges (the conceptual bridge to large vessels), and the Yabikú interlocking joinery technique (enabling the Kanu Maku class).

## Phase 1 — Dugout canoes (pre-516 CE)

Single-log dugout canoes. Hollowed from a single tree trunk by fire and stone adze. Limited by tree size — the largest canoe is as large as the largest log available.

- **Use**: Coastal fishing, short inter-island hops, local transport
- **Propulsion**: Paddled. No sails.
- **Size**: ~3–8 meters, depending on tree size
- **Crew**: 1–5
- **Construction**: Fire-hollowing and stone-tool shaping. Days to weeks per canoe.
- **Regional variation**: The Caracurí (eastern sea people) had more developed canoes than the Yukurí (western mountain people) — coastal life demanded better watercraft. Caracurí canoes were larger, more seaworthy, used for reef fishing and inter-island travel. Yukurí canoes were smaller, riverine, used for mountain stream transport.

## Phase 2 — Sewn-plank trade canoes (post-516 CE)

After unification (516 CE), trade expansion required larger vessels. The sewn-plank technique broke the single-tree size limit: instead of hollowing one log, planks were split from hardwood logs and stitched together edge-to-edge with fiber cordage. Seams were caulked with tree resin.

- **Use**: Regular trade routes to Tapuya and Capuyaquiran islands. Bulk cargo transport.
- **Propulsion**: Paddled + cotton sail (sails adopted once cotton textiles became available through trade). Sailing vessel, not just paddling.
- **Size**: ~8–15 meters length, 1.5–2 meters beam
- **Crew**: 6–15 (sailors + paddlers)
- **Cargo**: ~0.5–2 tons
- **Construction**: Planks split from tropical hardwood (mahogany, cedar, etc. — species TBD). Edges stitched with fiber cordage (henequen, cotton, or other plant fiber). Seams caulked with tree resin. Pole mast for cotton sail. Steering oar at stern.
- **Key innovation**: Breaking the single-tree limit. A sewn-plank canoe can be larger than any single log. The technique was originally developed for trade vessels — peaceful cargo craft.

## Phase 3 — Canoe-towed barges (~800s–1300s CE)

During calm water periods (especially the Month of Still Sea), Guaji traders lashed broad, flat wooden cargo barges — platforms built from lashed logs — and towed them with teams of canoes. The barge carried bulk goods (cassava flour, pottery, stone tools) that would swamp individual canoes.

- **Use**: Calm-season bulk transport between ports. Not for open-sea voyaging.
- **Propulsion**: Towed by teams of canoes (paddled). No independent propulsion.
- **Size**: Variable — platforms of lashed logs, ~5–10 meters, broad and flat
- **Cargo**: Several tons of bulk goods
- **What it taught Guaji mariners**:
  - How large hulls behave in open water (different stability, different turning characteristics)
  - Cargo distribution and weight management on a broad platform
  - That a large wooden structure can carry far more than any canoe
  - The limits of towing — slow, hard to steer, vulnerable if weather turns

**Conceptual bridge to the Kanu Maku**: The leap from barge-towing to the Kanu Maku was: instead of lashing a barge to canoes, make the barge itself a self-sustaining vessel with its own hull, sail, and steering. The barge-towing experience provided the conceptual foundation for large-vessel design.

## Phase 4 — War canoes (1100s–1300s CE)

As the tributary system developed under Kanu Maku (ruler 31, r. 1268–1295 CE) and successors, sewn-plank construction was adapted for war. War canoes were longer and narrower than trade canoes, designed for speed and boarding rather than cargo.

- **Use**: Tributary enforcement, punitive raids, naval combat
- **Propulsion**: Paddled (20–30 paddlers). Faster than trade canoes. Some had raised bow platforms for warriors.
- **Size**: ~12–15 meters length, 1.5–2 meters beam (narrower than trade canoes for speed)
- **Crew**: 20–30 paddlers + warriors
- **Design**: Longer, narrower hull. Raised bow platform for warriors (boarding advantage). Possibly outrigger-stabilized for boarding actions. Faster than trade canoes — designed for speed and interception, not cargo.
- **Construction**: Same sewn-plank technique as trade canoes. No structural innovation — just a different hull shape for a different purpose.

## Phase 5 — Sewn-plank war fleet (Y864–Y870, 1380–1386 CE)

The Kari Wirá (Sea Hurricane War, 1344–1392 CE) was the direct catalyst for the shift from dugout war canoes to large sewn-plank war vessels. Before the war, sewn-plank construction was used only for trade. Bika Kuru (then a nitaino, later ruler 35) commissioned sewn-plank war canoes in Y864 (1380 CE) after recognizing that dugout canoes could not match Tapuyic vessel size.

- **Shipyards**: Established at Waribá and Barikú in Y865 (1381 CE). Mainland hardwood unavailable (Tapuyic blockade), so local tropical hardwoods used. Fiber cordage and tree resin sourced from the interior.
- **First launches**: Y868 (1384 CE). Sea trials in Waribá bay. Vessels were ~15m, longer, narrower, faster, with raised platforms for warriors.
- **Full fleet**: Y869 (1385 CE). ~30 sewn-plank war vessels + ~50 remaining dugout canoes. Formation tactics developed for the larger, faster ships.
- **Combat proof**: Battle of Warikúru (Y871, 1387 CE). The sewn-plank war canoes proved superior in speed and maneuverability against the Tapuyic fleet. Decisive Guaji victory.
- **Post-war**: The technique became standard for all Guaji war vessels.

## Phase 6 — The Kanu Maku class (1400s CE)

The first true large vessel in Guaji history — a self-sustaining sewn-plank ship, not a barge towed by canoes. Broader, deeper hull with outrigger, cotton sail, partial deck. Developed during the post-war peace period (1392–1421 CE) under Guani Buka (ruler 36).

### Design

- **Hull**: Sewn-plank construction — planks split from tropical hardwood, edges stitched with fiber cordage, seams caulked with tree resin. ~15–20m long, 3–4m beam (war canoes ~15m but only 1.5–2m beam). Deeper belly than a war canoe — hull curves down to carry cargo. Rounded hull, shallow draft (can enter river mouths and port harbors). No keel — or a shallow carved keel from the dugout base. Cannot sail close to the wind, but downwind and reaching works fine — sufficient for the trade circuit (west on trade winds, east on countercurrent).
- **Outrigger**: Single outrigger lashed alongside on one side with cross-booms. Key stability innovation — lets the hull be broader and deeper without becoming tippy. Can be detached for harbor/river work.
- **Sail**: Single cotton sail on a pole mast — crab-claw or square shape. This is a sailing vessel, not a paddling vessel. The fundamental difference from war canoes — fewer crew, reliance on wind, not muscle. A small foresail on larger vessels for maneuverability.
- **Deck**: Partially decked — cargo hold covered with woven mats or wooden planks to protect from tropical rain. Open bow and stern for crew work. No enclosed cabin — tropical climate.
- **Steering**: Large steering oar at the stern (not a rudder — beyond this tech level). One steersman.
- **Crew**: 6–10 sailors (vs. 20–30 paddlers on a war canoe)
- **Cargo**: 3–5 tons. Gold and high-value goods stowed center (lowest, most stable point). Bulk goods (cassava flour, dried fish) stowed forward and aft.
- **Construction time**: 3–5 years per vessel

### The Yabikú technique

The critical innovation that made the Kanu Maku possible. A wood-to-wood fastening technique developed in the 1400s, inspired by the limitations of fiber stitching alone. Fiber cordage stitches planks edge-to-edge, but stitches can chafe, rot, or work loose in rough seas.

**Yabikú** (from *yabi* = hidden/old + *kú* = joint/fastening, "the old hidden joint") uses interlocking carved joints instead of (or in addition to) fiber stitching. Similar to Japanese kigumi.

**Principles**:
- Plank edges carved with precise interlocking geometry — dovetails, stepped laps, tapered mortise-and-tenon shapes — so that planks lock together under pressure. The more load on the joint, the tighter it becomes.
- No metal fasteners. No iron nails. The wood itself is the fastener.
- Joints carved to accommodate wood movement — tropical humidity makes wood swell and shrink. Small calculated gaps let joints breathe without losing grip.
- Some joints use wooden pegs (hardwood dowels) to prevent withdrawal — small carved pins driven into drilled holes.
- Requires master craftsmen (yabiku-kú) who know the grain, characteristics, and behavior of each wood type. Years of apprenticeship.

**Application to Kanu Maku**:
- Plank-to-plank seams: interlocking edge joints replace or reinforce fiber stitching. Hull is stronger and less dependent on cordage maintenance.
- Internal frames: ribs and cross-beams interlock with hull planks using carved sockets and notches, giving hull structural rigidity without metal brackets.
- Mast step: mast base sits in an interlocking socket carved into the keel structure, distributing sail forces through the wood itself.
- Outrigger attachment: cross-booms interlock with hull at carved notches, transferring stability forces through wood geometry.

**Post-Kanu Maku spread**: The technique was likely developed specifically for the Kanu Maku project. Afterward, it spread to war canoe construction and other wooden architecture.

### Copper axes

By the 1400s, Guajicutea had been mining local copper for decades (since Bika Kuru's post-war push, ~1390s CE). Copper was not mass-produced, but master craftsmen — including canoe-builders — had access to copper axes. Copper axes were a significant improvement over obsidian and andesite for woodworking: they hold an edge longer, cut deeper into hardwood, and allow more precise shaping of curved planks. This made the Kanu Maku project feasible — still years-long, but achievable. Without copper axes, splitting and shaping enough planks for a 15–20m hull with pure stone tools would have been nearly impossible.

### Construction workforce

Each Kanu Maku required:
- Master canoe-builders (yabiku-kú, specialists in the joinery technique)
- Fiber-workers for residual stitching and caulking
- Resin-workers for seam sealing
- Loggers and haulers for timber (80–100 workers to haul large logs from forest to shipyard)
- Sustained food supply for the workforce (feasts at each construction milestone)
- Calm-season work for final hull assembly (rough seas make precise joinery impossible)

### The three vessels

Only three Kanu Maku were ever built:

1. **Kanu Maku** based at Yukubá (SW port) — assigned to the Tapuya trade route. Carried gold west, returned with maize, tin, textiles, and specialty woods.
2. **Kanu Maku** based at Waribá (NE port) — assigned to the eastern Capuyaquiran trade route. Carried goods to and from tributary islands and trade partners. This is the vessel Kari Buka sailed north to the Aruanãyára Empire in 1502. It crashed on the coast of Lacashe and was lost.
3. **Kanu Wirá** (hurricane canoe) — retrofitted to warship from a cargo hull. Cargo hold covering removed, replaced with open deck for warriors. Raised fighting platform added at bow. Sail kept (warship can sail, not just paddle). More crew added (warriors, not cargo handlers). Flagship of the Guaji fleet. Used to suppress the Boraku Revolt (1421–1424 CE). Later served as prestige vessel for the Cacique Má.

After the loss of the Waribá vessel in 1502, only two remained: the Yukubá cargo vessel and the Kanu Wirá warship.

## Phase 7 — Mixed tribute fleets (1300s–1500 CE)

By the peak period, the Guaji fleet operated as a mixed force:
- **War canoes** for escort, intimidation, and punitive raids
- **Large trade canoes** for carrying tribute back to Guajicutea
- **Kanu Maku** for high-value, long-distance trade (gold to Tapuya, tin back)
- **Kanu Wirá** as flagship for tributary enforcement

Annual tribute collection became a political ritual — the fleet arriving at a tributary island was a display of Guajicutea's power. The Kanu Wirá, with its sail and size, was the most visible symbol of that power.

## Technology constraints

Throughout the pre-colonial period:
- **Tools**: Stone (obsidian, andesite) → copper (1390s+) → bronze (late 1450s+, for axes and tools)
- **Fastening**: Fiber cordage stitching → Yabikú interlocking joints (1400s+) → combination of both
- **No iron**: No metal fasteners of any kind. No nails. Wood, fiber, and resin only.
- **Max hull size**: ~15–20m (limited by timber availability, tool quality, and construction time)
- **Sail technology**: Cotton sail on pole mast. Crab-claw or square. Cannot sail close to the wind — downwind and reaching only. Steering oar, not rudder.
- **Comparison**: Burawi ocean-going ships (1520+) were far larger and more advanced — the technology gap was immediately visible at first contact.
