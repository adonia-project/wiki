# Guajicutea Era Notes: 1723–1768 (Early Republic)

## Overview

First four decades of the Republic of Guajicutea. Constitution drafted, senate and judiciary established, civil law adopted. Rigid class structure with Sartans at top. Economy shifts from plantation to light manufacturing (can't compete with Lacashe's slave-labor plantations). Astillero Real seized from Companyia Reial (1761). Piracy surge from Volisanian Civil War + Burawi departure. Capuyaquiran and Abyalan League formed (1764) as anti-piracy alliance. Education spreads to masses via Church schools → peasant rebellions (1760s) → constitutional reform (1768): expanded voting rights to all men, senate doubled to 2 reps per distrito.

- 4-year terms, no term limits.
- Tradition of single terms (not codified, but politically expected).
- 12 Cónsuls over 45 years (1723–1768).
- First Cónsul (de la Vega) only did one term, establishing the precedent.

## Government Structure

- **Senate**: 16 senators (1 per distrito), elected by landowning men. Expanded to 32 (2 per distrito) in 1768.
- **Cónsul**: Head of state, international affairs. 4-year terms, elected by senate. No term limits.
- **Judiciary**: Civil law system adapted from Sartan legal code. Local courts per distrito, high court in Tabakurí.
- **Districtos**: 16, based on former feudal bikabás. Sartan colonial names assigned during 1609–1723 colonial period (replacing Guaji feudal names):
  - San Mateo (formerly Guanaran)
  - Altavista (Barikan)
  - Santa Lucía (Karuban)
  - San Esteban (Wariban — named after Castillo de San Esteban fort at Waribá)
  - San Felipe (Taban)
  - Río Azul (Yakuban)
  - San Andrés (Makuan)
  - Sierra Alta (Yukuman)
  - Las Minas (Bukan — the gold-mining district)
  - San Miguel (Kurun)
  - Santa Ana (Wakunan)
  - Costa del Sol (Caracuran — the eastern maritime coast)
  - San Ventura (Venturan — contains Tabakurí)
  - San Antonio (Trogonan)
  - Valle del Sur (Barian — the southern plantation valley)
  - Santa Rosa (Ganan)
- **Suffrage**: Landowning men only → all men (1768). Women, non-landowning Kaftians/Bayani still excluded.

## Class Structure

1. **Sartan landowning elite** — top. Purchased royal lands, dominate senate and Cónsul's office.
2. **Sartan-Guaji mixed class** — merchants, professionals, administrators. Sartan-fluent, integrated into Sartan cultural sphere.
3. **Guaji natives** — rural majority. Farmers, fishermen, labourers. Guaji language, rural communities.
4. **Kaftians** — descendants of enslaved from Burawi colonial period. Freed but marginalized. Agricultural labour, domestic service.
5. **Bayani** — migrants from Sartan colonial period. Agricultural labourers. Most marginalized.
- Rigid but not legally codified. Barriers are economic (land ownership) and cultural (Sartan language fluency).
- Hardened over this period rather than softened.

## Education

- Sartan Petran Catholic Church = primary educational institution.
- Parish schools in Tabakurí, Waribá, Barikú from 1730s.
- Expanded into rural areas 1740s–1750s.
- Basic literacy in Sartan spread to significant minority of rural population by 1760s.
- Irony: Church education → peasant literacy → political awareness → rebellions.
- Church caught between educational mission and political order it supported.
- Guaji syncretic Catholicism thrived among majority (sea/mountain/sky → Trinity, cremation persisted).

## Culture & Literature

- Sartan-language literature by Guaji-born authors emerged as distinct tradition.
- Kari Yabí transcription (c. 1650s) established precedent.
- Guajicutean Enlightenment: intellectual circle in Tabakurí, absorbed Enlightenment ideas from Mercia, Breisland, Lacashe.
- Waribá: cosmopolitan port culture, Sartan + Guaji + Zong influences.
- Barikú: smaller, more military in character.

## Economy

- **Plantation agriculture** (sugar, tobacco, dyes) initially dominant.
- **Problem**: Can't compete with Lacashe's slave-labor plantations (cotton, sugar at lower cost).
- **Shift to light manufacturing** from 1740s:
  - Sugar refining, tobacco curing, food processing (value-added to raw agricultural output)
  - Textile production (imported raw cotton from Zong)
  - Metalworking (copper/bronze traditions from pre-colonial period)
  - Ship repair and construction (after 1761 Astillero Real seizure)
  - Pottery/ceramics
- **Astillero Real seizure (1761)**: Accelerated manufacturing shift. Shipyard = largest industrial installation. Demand for timber, rope, sailcloth, iron fittings, skilled labour.
- **Population**: ~80k (1723) → ~115k (1768). Growth from natural increase, Bayani migration, Sartan/Zong merchants.

## Astillero Real Seizure (1761)

- Companyia Reial del Capuyaquiran operated Astillero Real since Treaty of 1705.
- Source of friction: republic sovereignty over Waribá, but primary industrial asset in Volisanian hands.
- Volisanian Civil War (1736–1801) weakened Companyia Reial's capacity to enforce claims.
- Cónsul Joaquín Delgado (1759–1763) calculated Volisania couldn't respond.
- 1761: Republic nationalised Astillero Real and port facilities. Expelled Companyia Reial management. Created naval ministry.
- Companyia Reial issued formal protest but could deploy no force.
- Popular domestically — seen as colonial asset rightfully belonging to republic.

## Piracy

- Volisanian Civil War + Burawi departure = security vacuum in Capuyaquiran Sea.
- Volisanian naval patrols ceased. Burawi naval forces withdrawn to Abyalan coast.
- Piracy surged 1740s–1750s. Uninhabited islands, hidden coves as pirate bases.
- Republic's naval capacity limited (small, aging Waribá squadron).
- Trade routes to east and south increasingly vulnerable.

## Capuyaquiran and Abyalan League (1764)

- Collective security alliance. Anti-piracy: coordinated naval patrols, shared intelligence, joint courts.
- **Founding members**: Republic of Guajicutea, Lacashe (autonomous), Asikyira (independent 1743), former Burawi Capuyaquiran possessions (independent 1755).
- Also served as diplomatic forum for independent states of the region.
- Marked shift from colonial-order rivalries to post-colonial cooperation among small states.

## Peasant Rebellions

### Rebelión de los Pueblos (1762–1763)
- First major peasant rebellion.
- Centred in rural interior districtos.
- Armed bands seized estate buildings, refused rents, demanded franchise extension.
- Suppressed by republic's small military force.
- Scale alarmed the senate.

### Rebelión de Tabakurí (1766)
- More threatening upheaval. Rebels marched on capital.
- Occupied distrito courthouse and cathedral square for several days.
- Dispersed by troops from Waribá garrison.
- Leaders: literate peasants, former parish school students.
- Demands: universal male suffrage, doubling of senate, judges from outside landowning class.

## Foreign Relations

### Lacashe (closest ally)
- Autonomous parliamentary republic from 1709. Fully independent 1789.
- 1723–1743: only two independent states in region.
- Close trade, diplomatic, intellectual exchange.
- Lacashe's parliamentary system = model and cautionary tale.

### Asikyira
- Independent from 1743. Relations established after independence.
- Shared interest in regional stability and anti-piracy.
- Distance limited depth of relationship.
- Mixed Burawi-indigenous character vs. Guajicutea's Sartan-Guaji colonial heritage.

### Breisland (souring)
- Key trade partner during colonial period (Lurandia supply line).
- Protested Astillero Real seizure (commercial ties to Companyia Reial).
- Imposed trade restrictions on Guajicutean vessels at Breislandic ports.
- Breislandic ships implicated in piracy (privateers during Volisanian Civil War).
- Trade declined significantly by mid-1760s.

### Zongosphere (improving)
- Zong emissaries arrived in Waribá 1765.
- Seeking trade partners as Volisania weakened.
- Offered manufactured goods, textiles, porcelain on favourable terms.
- Senate welcomed Zong initiative despite Breislandic objections.
- Zong goods flowing through Waribá in increasing volumes by late 1760s.

### Volisanian Viceroyalties
- Balisca, Balboa, Tapuya: semi-autonomous during Volisanian Civil War.
- Traded directly with Guajicutea without reference to Volisanian crown.
- Market for Guajicutean manufactured goods and agricultural products.
- Source of raw materials and luxury goods.

### Burawi Capuyaquiran Possessions
- 1755: Burawa granted independence to remaining Capuyaquiran possessions.
- Republic courted them to join Guajicutea.
- Only Warikú joined (1756). Others preferred independence.
- Most joined Capuyaquiran and Abyalan League in 1764.

## Constitutional Reform of 1768

- Expanded franchise to all adult men (regardless of land ownership).
- Senate doubled: 16 → 32 (2 reps per distrito).
- Second seat designed for non-landowning candidates to compete without displacing landowning representative.
- Did NOT extend to women or non-landowning Kaftians/Bayani (narrow construction of "man" in civil law code).
- Championed by Cónsul Diego Alvarado (1767–1771).
- Response to peasant rebellions + growing political awareness.
- Opposition from conservative landowning senators. Support from commercial/reformist blocs.
- Marked end of first phase of republic. Landowner republic → broader (still imperfect) political order.

## Key Figures

- **Don Vicente de la Vega** — first Cónsul (1723–1727). Republican visionary. One term, stepped aside.
- **Joaquín Delgado** — Cónsul (1759–1763). Seized Astillero Real (1761).
- **Nicolás Vega** — Cónsul (1763–1767). Formed Capuyaquiran and Abyalan League (1764). Possible descendant of Don Vicente.
- **Diego Alvarado** — Cónsul (1767–1771). Signed 1768 constitutional reform.
- **Esteban Makurí** — PD founder. Guaji native, parish school-educated. Surname from makú (native tuber) + -rí (person of). Began organising in late 1760s, published pamphlets in Sartan. Founded Patrimonio Diversidad movement that would dominate next era's politics.
- **Warikú** — small island (ID 1) near Guajicutea. Joined republic 1756. Name from warí (moon) + -kú (diminutive) = "little moon."

## New Entities Introduced

- **Republic of Guajicutea** (1723). Senate + Cónsul. Landowner suffrage → all men (1768).
- **Districtos** — 16 electoral districts based on former feudal bikabás.
- **Capuyaquiran and Abyalan League** (1764). Anti-piracy alliance. Guajicutea, Lacashe, Asikyira, former Burawi possessions.
- **Warikú** — small island that joined republic in 1756.
- **Rebelión de los Pueblos** (1762–1763). First major peasant rebellion.
- **Rebelión de Tabakurí** (1766). Second peasant rebellion, reached capital.
- **Constitutional reform of 1768** — expanded voting rights, doubled senate.

## Internal Notes (not for wiki)

- **Language**: Sartan dominant in cities, government, law. Guaji survives rural. By 1760s, basic Sartan literacy spreading to rural population via Church schools.
- **Astillero Real**: Seized 1761. Boosted ship repair/building. Created demand cluster for supporting industries.
- **Breisland souring**: Double grievance — Astillero Real seizure protest + Breislandic privateers implicated in piracy.
- **Zong emissaries**: Arrived 1765. Zongosphere expanding as Volisania weakens. Trade terms more favourable than Breisland.
- **1768 reform**: Did not extend to women or non-landowning Kaftians/Bayani. "All men" construed narrowly. Property qualification removed for men only.
- **Next era**: 1768–1801. Continued population growth, further reforms, end of Volisanian Civil War (1801), Lacashe independence (1789).
