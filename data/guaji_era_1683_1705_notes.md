# Guajicutea Era Notes: 1683–1705 (Sarta-Volisania War Period)

## Overview

Sarta-Volisania War (1683–1705) impact on Guajicutea. War fought mostly in Old World. Colony becomes fortress, then collapses economically. Governor killed by peasant mob (1691). Three months of anarchy (Interregnum of 1691). Burawi retaking attempt fails. Order restored by Breisland-Mercia (1692). Ship captain governs as temporary administrator. War ends 1705 — Sarta bankrupted, landlocked, fleets destroyed. Treaty cedes Astillero Real de Waribá to Volisanian royal company. Colony fundamentally transformed: plantation system collapsed, smallholder agriculture replaces it, colonial hierarchy broken.

## Key Figures

### Felipe Ortega (Governor, 1674–1691)
- Last crown-appointed governor of the pre-war period
- Longest-serving governor (17 years) — wartime made replacement difficult
- Killed by peasant mob in Waribá, 1691
- Food shortages + increased taxation = trigger for mob violence

### Waraku (Peasant Ruler, 1691 — 3 months)
- Guaji peasant. Name means "Turning Moon" (warí=moon + waku=cycle/turn) — evokes lunar calendar, old ways
- Emerged as leader during peasant revolts
- Seized power in Waribá after Ortega's death
- Rule brief, chaotic, confined to Waribá area
- Rest of island in varying states of disorder — local communities self-governing, military fighting to restore order
- Not a governor — peasant who seized power during interregnum

### Andrés Caldera (Temporary Administrator, 1692–1705)
- Sartan ship captain, Waribá naval squadron
- One of few Sartan authority figures remaining on island
- Installed by Breisland-Mercia alliance through diplomatic channels with Sarta
- Not crown-appointed — military officer acting as interim civil administrator
- Governed with reduced bureaucracy + implicit Breislandic/Mercian naval backing
- 13-year administration: relative stability amid continued economic hardship

## Sarta-Volisania War (1683–1705)

- Fought primarily in Old World between Sarta and Volisania
- New World impact: economic and political, not military
- No land fighting on Guajicutea
- Sartan naval squadron at Waribá ordered to remain in port — survived the war (only Sartan fleet to survive)
- Sarta's fleets destroyed on the high seas during the war
- Sarta bankrupted, lost coastal access → landlocked
- War lasted 22 years (1683–1705)

## Economic Crisis

### Causes
- Sartan mainland funding dried up as war drained treasury
- Colonial officials and military went unpaid
- Taxation increased to compensate → peasant resentment
- Volisanian trade ended (enemy)
- Supply lines disrupted
- Population ~80k straining agricultural capacity
- Deforestation and soil erosion from previous decades

### Effects
- Colonial administration can't pay officials/military → corruption, desertion
- Military loses discipline → some soldiers join peasants
- Plantation system collapses (no Sartan capital, no export markets)
- Barter economy emerges in rural areas
- Colonial currency loses value
- Food shortages worsen
- Colonial hierarchy breaks down → local leaders (Guaji village heads, Bayani elders) gain influence

### Shipbuilding
- Astillero Real shifts to war production — galleons for Sartan war effort
- Hybrid Sartan-Guaji construction (Illypnian hull forms + Yabikú joinery + local hardwoods)
- As Sarta's finances deteriorate, can't pay for ships under construction
- c. 1690: shipbuilding trickles to halt. Unfinished hulls in dry docks. Shipwrights unpaid.
- 1692–1705: minimal capacity — maintenance/repair of Waribá squadron only

### Trade
- Volisanian trade ended (enemy)
- Trade continues with neutral parties: Mercia, Breisland, Burawa
- Foreign navy vessels dock at Waribá — Mercian, Breislandic, Burawi ships
- Waribá becomes de facto free port — foreign naval presence provides security
- Trade with Mercia/Breisland/Burawa = only economic lifeline

## Harsher Laws and Taxation

- 1685: Governor Ortega declares martial law — emergency powers over judiciary, movement, labor
- Curfews in Waribá and Tabakurí. Movement between districts restricted (official passes required)
- All foreign trade through official channels. Private commerce with Volisania criminalized
- War tax levied on top of existing colonial taxes
- Shipbuilding levy: communities must contribute timber, resin, fiber, labor to Astillero Real
- Tax collectors increasingly aggressive — property seizure for non-payment, imprisonment for evasion
- Tax burden falls heaviest on Guaji/Bayani peasants. Sartan elites largely exempt or can evade
- Forced labor conscription for Astillero Real — men pulled from agriculture into shipyard
- 1687: Food requisitioning instituted — colonial authorities seize crops/livestock "for war effort," paying below-market or nothing
- Communities resisting requisition face military punishment
- By late 1680s: extracting from a population that has less and less to give → spiral → Ortega's death 1691

## Conditions on the Ground

- **Banditry**: Deserters from unpaid military turn to robbery. Roads unsafe. Rural self-defense militias form. Travel between Tabakurí and Waribá dangerous without armed escort.
- **Internal displacement**: Rural populations flee to Waribá (food, foreign naval protection). Others flee cities to rural areas (escape taxation/violence). Both directions.
- **Disease**: Fever and dysentery outbreaks. Malnutrition + crowded conditions. Colonial medical system can't cope. Church charitable institutions depleted.
- **Women's labor**: Men conscripted → women carry agricultural production. Gain economic authority in communities. Shift accelerates social change. Persists after war.
- **Church fracture**: Sartan Petran Church caught between colonial administration (demands support) and starving population. Some priests distribute church food stores to peasants (defying governor). Others align with administration (preach obedience). Church fractures.
- **Murani lifeline**: Cross-strait smuggling to Murani becomes essential for food, medicine, information. Murani fishermen/traders bring supplies. Cultural connection → survival mechanism.
- **Military desertion**: Unpaid, hungry soldiers desert. Some join peasant communities. Some become bandits. Castillo de San Esteban garrison shrinks. Administration's ability to enforce order reduced.

## Peasant Revolts and Plantation Collapse

- Multiple peasant revolts late 1680s–early 1690s. Escalating sequence:
  - **Waribá Food Riots (1686)**: First major outbreak. Food prices doubled. Dock workers, fishermen, Bayani laborers rioted in market district, seized food stores, clashed with colonial police. Suppressed but marked beginning of sustained unrest. Future article: `[[Waribá Food Riots]]`
  - **Southern Peasant Revolt (1688–1689)**: Larger, more organized. Plantation workers in southern agricultural districts refused to deliver harvests, seized estates, expelled Sartan landowners. Spread across multiple plantations. Military force needed to suppress. Some seized lands never returned. Future article: `[[Southern Peasant Revolt]]`
  - **Tabakurí Uprising (1690)**: Unrest spread to inland capital. Peasants from central river valleys marched on Tabakurí protesting taxation and food requisitioning. Garrison dispersed marchers. Demonstrated unrest had spread from ports/plantations to interior. Future article: `[[Tabakurí Uprising]]`
  - **1691 overthrow**: Climax. Governor Ortega killed by peasant mob in Waribá.
- Driven by: food shortages, increased taxation, forced labor, breakdown of colonial economic system
- Guaji and Bayani peasants revolt against colonial land-tenure system
- Each revolt weakened governor's authority further. Administration could suppress individual outbreaks but not address underlying causes.
- Each suppression required military resources administration couldn't afford → recruited more people to the cause
- Forces transition away from plantation-feudal system
- Estates abandoned or seized by peasant communities
- Colonial land-tenure (Sartan landowners, Guaji/Bayani laborers) → smallholder farming
- By end of war: plantation system largely collapsed, replaced by small farms and communal land use
- Colonial social hierarchy broken — more egalitarian but poorer

## Interregnum of 1691

- Governor Ortega killed by peasant mob in Waribá
- 3 months of ungoverned chaos
- Waraku (peasant ruler) seizes power in Waribá — confined to local area
- Rest of island: local self-governance, military fighting to restore order
- Castillo de San Esteban garrison unpaid, divided, can't project authority beyond walls
- Astillero Real idle

### Burawi Retaking Attempt
- Word of collapse reaches Burawi colonial administration in Lacashe
- Burawa sees opportunity to retake island (lost to Sarta in 1609)
- Fleet dispatched from Lacashe to Guajicutea
- Arrives at Waribá — finds colony in chaos
- Unable to establish beachhead — not organized resistance, but chaos prevents any party from establishing control
- Skirmishes with peasant militias AND colonial garrison remnants
- Burawi commander concludes retaking requires more resources than Lacashe willing to commit
- Fleet withdraws

### Restoration (1692)
- Breisland and Mercia (both concerned about power vacuum) dispatch ships
- Breislandic-Mercian fleet arrives at Waribá, secures port
- Captain Andrés Caldera (Sartan, Waribá squadron) installed as temporary administrator
- Arranged through diplomatic channels: Sarta, Breisland, Mercia
- Caldera = military officer, not crown-appointed governor
- Governs with Breislandic/Mercian naval backing

## Treaty of 1705

- Sarta-Volisania War ends
- Sarta: bankrupted, landlocked (lost coastal access), fleets destroyed
- Only remaining Sartan fleet = Waribá squadron (ordered to remain in port throughout war)
- Treaty confirms Guajicutea as Sartan colonial possession
- BUT: Astillero Real de Waribá ceded to Companyia Reial del Capuyaquiran (Volisanian royal trading company)
- Colony's primary industrial asset → Volisanian control
- Sartan naval squadron at Waribá depends on Volisanian company for repair/maintenance

### Companyia Reial del Capuyaquiran
- Volisanian royal trading company
- Catalan-influenced naming (Companyia Reial = Royal Company)
- Focused on Capuyaquiran Sea trade
- Takes control of Astillero Real de Waribá under Treaty of 1705
- Shipyard output serves Volisanian commercial interests, not Sartan military

## Key established details

- **War dates**: 1683–1705 (22 years). Previously noted as 1701 (18 years) — updated to 1705.
- **Governor killed**: Felipe Ortega, 1691, by peasant mob in Waribá.
- **Peasant ruler**: Waraku ("Turning Moon" — warí + waku). Guaji peasant. 3 months. Waribá area only.
- **Interregnum**: 3 months, 1691. Colony ungoverned.
- **Burawi attempt**: Fleet from Lacashe. Pushed out by chaos, not organized resistance.
- **Restoration**: 1692. Breisland-Mercia alliance. Captain Andrés Caldera.
- **Treaty**: 1705. Guaji back to Sarta. Astillero Real ceded to Companyia Reial del Capuyaquiran.
- **Sarta post-war**: Bankrupted, landlocked, fleets destroyed. Only Waribá squadron remains.
- **Plantation system**: Collapsed during peasant revolts. Replaced by smallholder agriculture.
- **Colony post-war**: Poorer, more autonomous, more Guaji in character. Colonial hierarchy broken.
