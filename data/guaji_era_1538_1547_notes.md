# History of Guajicutea (1538–1547) — Supporting Notes

Internal reference for the era article. Not synced to wiki.

## Guaji-styled Catholicism (Christianization period, 1538–1547)

The 1538–1547 period was the Christianization period — when Catholicism was actively imposed and began to take root, but in a form that differed from standard Burawi Catholicism. The Guaji adapted Christianity to their existing cosmology rather than adopting it wholesale.

### The Three Powers and the Trinity

The Guaji mapped their three powers onto the Christian Trinity:
- **Sea** (creator/origin) → **God the Father** (creator of all things)
- **Mountain** (fertility/judgment) → **God the Son** (Christ — judge of the living and dead, source of life and resurrection)
- **Sky** (spirits/messengers) → **Holy Spirit** (the spirit moving through the world)

This mapping allowed the Guaji to understand the Trinity through familiar cosmological categories. The structure was the same — three powers, three persons — which made the theological transition less alien than it might have been.

### What was suppressed
- Bohíques and priestesses abolished as institutions
- Catacombs sealed — no more ash niches, calendar carving, or storm ledger
- Bikumaku carving ceased (the glyph writing tradition)
- Lunar calendar officially replaced with Burawi solar calendar
- Traditional rites forbidden

### What was kept or adapted (syncretism)
- **Cremation** continued (see burial customs section) — the church could not dislodge this
- **Lunar calendar** survived on paper. Officially replaced by the Burawi solar calendar, but with the introduction of paper (via Burawi trade), farmers and fishers continued tracking lunar cycles in written form. The calendar that had been carved in stone for a thousand years moved to a new medium — no longer sacred, but practical. The carved year-marks (bikú) in the catacombs became historical artifacts; the living calendar was now handwritten.
- **Healing and herbal knowledge** of the bohíques was adopted by the missions for medicinal purposes. The Baraka Order recognized the practical value of Guaji herbal medicine and incorporated it into mission practice. Some of this knowledge was translated back to Burawa — a rare case of knowledge flowing from colony to colonizer.
- **Priestesses → nuns.** Former priestesses (bohíques) could convert to nuns. Nunneries were established on Guajicutea, allowing the female religious tradition to survive in diminished form. Nuns had no political rights — unlike the Warí, who had held ultimate authority in Guaji society — but they could manage and establish nunneries. The shift was from sacred political power to institutional religious life. The women who had once confirmed kings now lived behind convent walls.

### What changed in daily life
- Religious authority shifted from bohíques/priestesses to Catholic priests
- Sunday worship replaced the old ceremonial rhythm
- Baptism was adopted, primarily among the elite and those connected to the Burawi
- Marriage customs shifted toward Christian marriage
- The official calendar (Burawi solar) governed administration, trade, and church schedules; the lunar calendar survived as a practical tool for agriculture and fishing

## Burawi establishment in Guajicutea

### Two institutions (both based in Waribá)

| Entity | Type | Head | Location | Labor source |
|--------|------|------|----------|-------------|
| Mission (Dabra) | Religious | Liqa (Guaji head priest) | Hill near port | Mostly Guaji labor |
| Emissary of Burawa | Political | Emissary (Burawi official) | Fort near port area | Workers from Burawa/New Burawa |

### Religious establishment
- **Liqa**: Title for the head priest of a Dabra (mission). Created by the Burawi Catholic Church on the mainland. The Liqa is a Guaji convert, trained in Catholic doctrine and ordained to lead the mission.
- Mission located on a hill near the port of Waribá — physically separate from the secular settlement.
- Relied primarily on Guaji labor — local converts and workers maintained the compound, assisted in the hospice, supported missionary work.
- Operated by the Baraka Order.

### Political establishment
- **Emissary of Burawa**: Position created by the government of New Burawa. Represents Burawi political interests in Guajicutea.
- Based in a fort near the port area — serves as both residence and administrative center.
- Drew workers from Burawa and New Burawa (bureaucrats, clerks, support staff), not local labor.
- Role: maintain vassal relationship, oversee Burawi interests, ensure kingdom's policies align with colonial strategy.

### Military presence
- Three captains, three battalions.
- Two captains/battalions based in Waribá.
- One captain/battalion based in Tabakurí (the inland capital).
- Primary function: show of force to keep Guaji leadership compliant. Usually doing nothing.
- Burawi officers' journals describe posting as a vacation compared to New Burawa front lines against the Aruanãyára.

### Burawi population in Waribá
- Small but diverse community.
- Soldiers, missionaries, bureaucrats, families of non-military personnel (Emissary's family, bureaucrats' families).
- Two poles of colonial life: mission on the hill, fort at the port, Guaji town between them.

## Burial customs contention

- **Guaji tradition**: Cremation since 516 CE. Elites' ashes in kurubá (catacomb niches). Commoners' ashes scattered (sea, river, earth). Cremation = sacred mechanism: fire (mountain's domain) carries spirit to sky, ashes return to earth/sea (creator).
- **Christian requirement**: Catholic Church requires burial, not cremation.
- **Conflict**: Burawi missionaries pressed for Christian burial. Guaji resisted — cremation is not just custom but the sacred path of the spirit. Not abandoned.
- **Resolution**: Guaji continued cremating. Burawi maintained a cemetery at the Dabra (mission station) for their own dead — first burial ground on the island.
- **Significance**: The cemetery at the Dabra was the first physical presence of burial as a practice on Guajicutea. It remained a Burawi institution, not adopted by the Guaji population.
