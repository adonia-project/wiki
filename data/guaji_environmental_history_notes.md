# Guaji Environmental History Notes

Internal working notes on how the Guaji shaped the landscape of Guajicutea through agriculture, settlement, and deforestation from ~500 BCE to 1500 CE.

## Agricultural geography

### Best agricultural land

The most productive land is concentrated on the **eastern side** of the island:

- **Rainfall**: 1800–2200mm on the windward east, ideal for yams, makú, leren, and cacao
- **Soil**: young volcanic Andisols, extremely fertile
- **Elevation**: lowlands and gentle slopes below 600m are best
- **Water**: proximity to rivers for irrigation and processing cassava
- **Slope**: flatter valley bottoms and lower slopes are preferred for conuco mounds

The **west** is drier and better suited to cassava, beans, and squash. Agriculture there is possible but less intensive.

### Agricultural suitability map

A suitability model combining elevation, slope, rainfall, soil, and river proximity shows:
- **Dark green (high suitability)**: eastern lowlands and river valleys, plus some western coastal pockets
- **Light green (moderate)**: eastern foothills up to ~800m, western lowlands
- **Pale/yellow (low)**: high mountains, steep slopes, dry leeward extremes

The east is clearly the agricultural engine of the island.

## Urbanization geography

Best areas for larger, centralized settlements:

- **Coastal plains** near good harbors
- **Flat land** (<5° slope) near fresh water
- **Proximity to trade routes** — especially the cross-island Barikú–Waribá corridor
- **River mouths and inlets** — for water, fish, and canoe access

The three major ports — **Yukubá**, **Waribá**, and **Barikú** — sit in the best coastal locations. Secondary settlements cluster along the cross-island route and in major river valleys.

## Deforestation patterns (0–1500 CE)

### Model assumptions

Deforestation probability is driven by:
1. **Agricultural suitability** — the primary driver; good land gets cleared first
2. **Proximity to trade route** — the Barikú–Waribá corridor intensifies clearing along its path
3. **Proximity to ports** — settlements around Yukubá, Waribá, and Barikú need fuel and farmland
4. **Elevation/slope penalty** — steep highlands remain forested

### Results

The deforestation probability map shows three main cleared zones:

1. **Eastern lowlands and river valleys** — broad, continuous clearing for agriculture. This is the largest deforested zone.
2. **The Barikú–Waribá corridor** — a linear zone of intensified clearing along the cross-island trade route. Settlements, porters, and travelers all need wood and food.
3. **Southern tip and western coastal pockets** — cleared around Yukubá, Barikú, and the route connecting them.

### What remained forested

- **Monte Yukumá and the high mountains** — too steep, too cold, and sacred. The mountain forest is protected by taboo.
- **North coast** — less settled, possibly more intact forest.
- **Mangrove coasts** — protected fishing grounds, not cleared.
- **Sacred groves** — patches maintained around ceremonial sites, caves, and the catacombs.
- **Remnant old-growth** — scattered patches in areas too steep or too marginal for farming.

## Timeline of landscape change

### ~500–250 BCE: First clearance
- Small-scale clearing around initial coastal settlements
- North coast and early landing beaches first affected

### ~200 BCE–200 CE: Guajicutea Vieja period
- Clearing expands in the southern/western foothills
- Guajicutea Vieja becomes a major center; surrounding forest converted to fields

### ~200–500 CE: Two-tribe expansion
- Yukurí clear western valleys and slopes for cassava and trade agriculture
- Caracurí clear eastern lowlands intensively for yams, makú, and cacao
- Population reaches ~22,000 by 500 CE; deforestation accelerates

### 516 CE: Yukubara
- The mudflow reforests the Guajicutea Vieja area and surrounding western slopes
- Temporary setback for western agriculture; eastern Caracurí lands mostly unaffected

### 516–1100 CE: Unified expansion
- Population recovers and grows to ~38,000
- Barikú–Waribá corridor develops; linear deforestation along the route
- Eastern lowlands reach maximum clearance
- Charcoal peaks in soil profiles during the "Month of Smoke" burning season

### 1143 CE: Great hurricane
- Storm damage and flooding; some fields buried or salinized
- Temporary forest regrowth on abandoned or damaged fields
- Recovery leads to more cautious coastal settlement

### 1143–1500 CE: Recovery and intensification
- Population returns to ~40,000 by 1400 CE
- Deforestation reaches maximum pre-contact extent
- Soil erosion becomes noticeable on cleared eastern slopes
- Sedimentation increases in coastal waters near Waribá

## Ecological consequences

### Soil erosion
- Cleared slopes in the east lose topsoil during heavy rains
- Eroded sediment washes into rivers and coastal inlets
- Waribá's river-formed inlet may have accumulated sediment over centuries

### Hydrology
- Reduced forest cover increases runoff and flash flooding
- Rivers may have become more sediment-laden
- Some small streams may have shifted course

### Biodiversity
- Loss of old-growth forest habitat
- Some bird species decline, affecting feather-cloak production
- This may have driven demand for imported feathers or feathers from more distant highland forests
- Small forest mammals and reptiles lose habitat

### Fire regime
- The "Month of Smoke" in the Guaji calendar reflects widespread agricultural burning
- Charcoal layers in soil profiles peak during the unified expansion period
- Fire-maintained grasslands and secondary growth replace primary forest in many areas

## Archaeological signatures

- **Pollen cores** from eastern valley wetlands would show:
  - Decline in forest pollen (especially after ~200 CE)
  - Rise in grass, weed, and crop pollen
  - Charcoal peaks matching burning seasons
- **Soil profiles** show increased charcoal and sometimes erosion horizons
- **Coastal sediment cores** show increased terrestrial sediment input after major clearing phases
- **Vegetation today** would be a mosaic of secondary forest, agricultural fallow, and remnant primary forest, not pristine rainforest

## Implications for the Guaji worldview

- The contrast between cleared lowlands and forested mountains reinforces the sacredness of Yukumá
- The "Month of Smoke" is not just agricultural practice but a visible transformation of the landscape
- Deforestation may have been understood as part of the balance between the three powers — the mountain gives soil, the people clear and farm, the sea provides rain
- Scarcity of certain forest resources (feathers, specific woods) may have driven trade with other islands

## Open questions

- Did the Guaji practice any form of forest management or conservation beyond sacred groves?
- How much old-growth forest survived by 1500 CE?
- Did deforestation contribute to the severity of the 1143 hurricane's impacts (more runoff, landslides)?
- How did colonization and population collapse affect forest regrowth?
