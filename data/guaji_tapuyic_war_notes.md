# The Kari Wirá — The Sea Hurricane (Tapuyic War)

## Overview

The **Kari Wirá** (Sea Hurricane) was the largest naval conflict in Guaji history, fought between Guajicutea and the Tapuyic Empire — a coalition of mainland Tapuya and the Tapuri islanders of the Lesser Capuyaquiran. The war spanned roughly Y828–Y876 (1344–1392 CE), with the most intense fighting during Y846–Y871 (1362–1387 CE). It reshaped Guaji shipbuilding, fortification, and political structure, and directly prompted the development of large sewn-plank war vessels.

The name **Kari Wirá** combines *kari* (sea) and *wirá* (hurricane) — "the hurricane that came from the sea." The Tapuyic fleet descended on the Capuyaquiran Sea like a storm from the west.

## Background

### The Tapuyic Empire (~Y804, 1320 CE)

The Tapuri islanders of the Lesser Capuyaquiran had long been trade partners with Guajicutea — they were the western link in the trade route to the Tapuya mainland. But the mainland Tapuya was a growing continental power with resources the islands lacked: large timber, metalworking, fiber, and manpower.

Around **Y804, Month of Smoke** (1320 CE), the Tapuri islanders formalized an alliance with the mainland Tapuya. The Tapuri provided seafaring knowledge, sea route expertise, and naval tradition; Tapuya provided resources, shipbuilding materials, and manpower. The coalition declared itself the **Tapuyic Empire** — a mainland-island power with both continental depth and maritime reach.

Recorded in Bikumaku tablets as: *"the western islands turned to the mainland."*

### Guaji expansion

Simultaneously, Guajicutea was expanding its own sphere of influence. **Kanu Maku** (Y753–Y779, 1268–1295 CE) established the first tributary system in the eastern islands. **Wirá Waku** (Y780–Y806, 1295–1322 CE) consolidated the tributaries through raiding campaigns. By the time **Kawa Kuru** (Y807–Y834, 1322–1350 CE) took the throne, Guaji tribute was flowing regularly from the eastern and southwestern islands.

The two expanding powers were on a collision course. The contested zone was the **Boraku islands** (SW) — the islands between Guajicutea's tributary sphere and the Tapuyic Empire's eastern frontier.

## Timeline of the War

### Pre-War — The Tapuyic Empire Forms (Y804–Y828, 1320–1344 CE)

**Y804, Month of Smoke (1320 CE)** — Tapuri-Tapuya alliance formalized. Tapuri chieftains and Tapuya mainland lords meet on the largest Tapuri island (ID 107). Recorded in Bikumaku tablets as: *"the western islands turned to the mainland."*

**Y806, Month of Long Nights (1322 CE)** — Wirá Waku takes the Guaji throne. His reign focuses on eastern tributary consolidation; the western threat is not yet recognized.

**Y808, Month of First Rains (1324 CE)** — The Tapuyic Empire is formally declared. A Tapuri-Tapuya joint fleet is commissioned. Mainland timber and fiber flow to the Tapuri shipyards.

**Y814, Month of Dry Wind (1330 CE)** — Tapuyic fleet takes the first Lesser Capuyaquiran islands. The Yaruba peoples of the western Lesser Capuyaquiran are subjugated. Tapuya trade routes now extend to the central Capuyaquiran Sea.

**Y818, Month of Flying Fish (1334 CE)** — Tapuyic expansion continues. More Yaruba islands fall. Yaruba refugees flee eastward — some reach Guaji trade ports, bringing warnings.

**Y822, Month of First Storms (1338 CE)** — Kawa Kuru takes the Guaji throne. Trade is at its peak, tribute flowing regularly. But Yaruba refugees and disrupted western trade routes are growing concerns.

**Y824, Month of First Rains (1340 CE)** — Tapuyic fleet reaches the western edge of the Greater Capuyaquiran. The Boraku islands (SW) are now the frontier between the two powers.

**Y826, Month of Dry Wind (1342 CE)** — Guaji trade canoes to the mainland are harassed. Kawa Kuru sends patrol fleets to the SW waters. Tensions rise.

### Phase 1 — First Clashes (Y828–Y846, 1344–1362 CE)

**Y828, Month of Flying Fish (1344 CE)** — First blood. A Tapuyic squadron attacked a Guaji tribute canoe near the Boraku islands. The tribute collector (a nitaino) was killed, the cargo seized. Recorded in the calendar corridor with a crossed spears glyph — the first war mark of the Kari Wirá.

**Y829, Month of Dry Wind (1345 CE)** — Kawa Kuru sends a protest embassy to the Tapuyic fleet. The embassy is turned back. Guaji begins reinforcing SW patrol fleets.

**Y830, Month of Still Sea (1346 CE)** — **Battle of Karikuri** (Reef Battle). A Guaji patrol fleet encountered a Tapuyic squadron near the western reefs of the Boraku islands. First major naval battle. The Guaji fleet, composed of dugout war canoes, was outmatched by the larger Tapuyic vessels (built with mainland timber). Guaji losses: 7 canoes broken, ~40 warriors lost. Calendar corridor: broken canoe glyph.

**Y832, Month of Great Waters (1348 CE)** — Tapuyic raids on Guaji trade routes intensify. Three trade canoes captured between Yukubá and the Lesser Capuyaquiran. Trade to the mainland is severely disrupted.

**Y834, Month of Harvest (1350 CE)** — Kawa Kuru orders construction of additional war canoes. Guaji fleet strength increased. But the dugout design limitation is recognized — Guaji canoes cannot match Tapuyic vessel size.

**Y836, Month of Dry Wind (1352 CE)** — Tapuyic systematically takes remaining Lesser Capuyaquiran islands. The Yaruba are fully subjugated. Guajicutea's western trade (through Yukubá and Barikú) drops to a fraction of pre-war levels.

**Y840, Month of First Storms (1356 CE)** — Tapuyic builds up fleet for a major eastern offensive. Shipyards on the Tapuri islands and mainland coast produce large vessels at an unprecedented rate. Guaji intelligence (from Yaruba refugees and Boraku islanders) reports the buildup.

**Y844, Month of Planting (1360 CE)** — Kawa Kuru dies. Yuku Waku takes the Guaji throne. His regnal name "Mountain Cycle" proves apt — the mountainous interior becomes a refuge as coastal threats escalate.

**Y845, Month of Smoke (1361 CE)** — Yuku Waku orders all coastal settlements to build watchtowers and stockpile reserves. War preparations begin in earnest.

### Phase 2 — The Sea Hurricane (Y846–Y864, 1362–1380 CE)

**Y846, Month of First Storms (1362 CE)** — The Tapuyic grand fleet sails east. Dozens of large vessels, built with mainland timber and Tapuri naval knowledge. War formally begins. Calendar corridor: large crossed spears glyph — the formal declaration of war.

**Y847, Month of Great Waters (1363 CE)** — Tapuyic fleet raids the SW coast of Guajicutea. Small coastal villages are attacked, food stores seized. The population begins moving inland and to fortified settlements.

**Y848, Month of Great Waters (1364 CE)** — More raids. The SW coast between Yukubá and Barikú is repeatedly hit. Guaji patrol fleets engage Tapuyic squadrons but cannot prevent the raids.

**Y851, Month of Great Waters (1367 CE)** — **Raid on Yukubá**. The Tapuyic fleet attacked Yukubá, Guajicutea's SW port. The attack came during the stormy season — the Tapuyic fleet used the weather as cover. Warehouses were burned, canoes destroyed, trade goods seized. The port's wooden defenses were overwhelmed. First time an enemy force struck a major Guaji settlement directly. Calendar corridor: large broken canoe glyph with crossed spears beneath — the same symbol used for the great hurricane, but with war marks. Storm ledger records this as the worst maritime disaster since the great hurricane of Y627.

**Y852, Month of Long Nights (1368 CE)** — Yuku Waku orders the full mobilization of Guaji war canoes. Every coastal village is ordered to contribute vessels and warriors. The largest fleet Guajicutea has ever assembled begins to gather at Waribá (NE port, safer from the western threat).

**Y853, Month of Dry Wind (1369 CE)** — Guaji fleet assembles. Training and formation drills intensify. Yuku Waku personally leads the fleet from Waribá to the SW waters.

**Y855, Month of Still Sea (1371 CE)** — **Battle of Makuwári** (the Great Fleet Battle). The largest naval battle in Guaji history. Yuku Waku engaged the Tapuyic fleet in open water between Guajicutea and the Boraku islands. Guaji had more canoes (~80 vs ~40 larger Tapuyic vessels). Fought in the calm of Still Sea month — ideal for fleet maneuvers. Guaji victory through numbers and maneuverability, but heavy losses: ~20 canoes broken, ~100 warriors lost. Tapuyic fleet broken but not destroyed — retreats west. Calendar corridor: crossed spears glyph enclosed in a ring.

**Y857, Month of First Rains (1373 CE)** — Stalemate. Both sides rebuild. Tapuyic fleet repairs and reinforces in the Lesser Capuyaquiran. Guaji fleet rebuilds at Waribá and Yukubá (partially restored).

**Y859, Month of Great Waters (1375 CE)** — **Second raid on Yukubá**. The Tapuyic fleet returned. This time Guajicutea was prepared — Bika Kuru (then a nitaino, not yet king) had organized coastal defenses, watchtowers, and a reinforced fleet. The raid was repelled with heavy Tapuyic losses. Calendar corridor: broken canoe glyph struck through with a line — a failed attack.

**Y862, Month of Long Nights (1378 CE)** — Yuku Waku dies. Bika Kuru takes the throne as Cacique Má. His regnal name "Path Rest" — but his reign would be defined by war and reconstruction.

### Phase 3 — Counter-Offensive (Y864–Y876, 1380–1392 CE)

**Y864, Month of Planting (1380 CE)** — Bika Kuru commissions the construction of sewn-plank war canoes — the first systematic production of large fleet vessels in Guaji history. Recognizes that dugout canoes cannot match Tapuyic vessel size. The sewn-plank technique, previously used only for trade vessels, is adapted for war. This is the technological turning point of the war.

**Y865, Month of First Rains (1381 CE)** — Shipbuilding yards established at Waribá and Barikú. Mainland hardwood is unavailable (Tapuyic blockade), so local tropical hardwoods are used. Fiber cordage and tree resin for caulking sourced from the interior.

**Y866, Month of Sprouting (1382 CE)** — Fleet construction in full progress. Bika Kuru personally oversees the shipyards. Canoe-builders (craftsmen) are given priority resources and food.

**Y868, Month of Still Sea (1384 CE)** — First new sewn-plank war canoes launched. Sea trials in Waribá bay. The vessels are longer (~15m), narrower, faster, with raised platforms for warriors. They outmaneuver the old dugout canoes easily.

**Y869, Month of Dry Wind (1385 CE)** — Full fleet of new war canoes completed. ~30 sewn-plank war vessels plus ~50 remaining dugout canoes. Training of crews in the new vessels. Formation tactics developed for the larger, faster ships.

**Y870, Month of Dry Wind (1386 CE)** — Guaji fleet ready for offensive. Bika Kuru plans a western campaign to break the Tapuyic blockade and secure the western trade route.

**Y871, Month of Still Sea (1387 CE)** — **Battle of Warikúru** (the Decisive Battle). Bika Kuru led the rebuilt Guaji fleet westward — the first Guaji offensive expedition. The fleet engaged the Tapuyic navy near the strait between the Greater and Lesser Capuyaquiran. The new sewn-plank war canoes proved superior in speed and maneuverability. Decisive Guaji victory — the Tapuyic fleet was shattered. ~25 Tapuyic vessels captured or broken. Calendar corridor: crossed spears glyph within a double ring — the decisive battle of the war.

**Y872, Month of Second Storms (1388 CE)** — Guaji fleet secures the western passage. Boraku islands confirmed as Guaji tributaries. Tapuyic remnants flee to the Lesser Capuyaquiran.

**Y873, Month of First Rains (1389 CE)** — Guaji reopens the western trade route to the mainland. Trade is limited but restored. Bika Kuru sends emissaries to the Yaruba islands — some Yaruba communities welcome liberation from Tapuyic rule.

**Y875, Month of Harvest (1391 CE)** — Tapuyic Empire sues for peace. Internal strife between the Tapuri islanders and mainland Tapuya weakens the coalition. A Tapuyic embassy arrives at Guajicutea.

**Y876, Month of Long Nights (1392 CE)** — **Treaty of Kariwári** (Sea Peace). War ends. Terms: Tapuyic Empire withdraws from the central Capuyaquiran; western trade route open to both parties; Boraku islands remain Guaji tributaries; no tribute or prisoners exchanged. Calendar corridor: clasped hands glyph (same symbol as the original unification of Y1, but smaller). The storm ledger records this as the end of the Kari Wirá.

## Aftermath

### Shipbuilding Revolution

The Kari Wirá was the direct catalyst for the shift from dugout canoes to large sewn-plank war vessels. Before the war, sewn-plank construction was used only for trade canoes. Bika Kuru's commission of sewn-plank war canoes (Y864, Month of Planting, 1380 CE) and the fleet's completion (Y869, Month of Dry Wind, 1385 CE) proved the design in combat at the Battle of Warikúru (Y871, 1387 CE). After the war, the technique became standard for all Guaji war vessels. The post-war fleet was larger, faster, and more capable than anything Guajicutea had built before.

### Shipbuilding Evolution

The full evolution of Guaji watercraft, from simplest to most complex:

**Phase 1 — Dugout canoes (pre-516 CE):** Single-log dugouts. Limited by tree size. Coastal fishing, short inter-island hops. Paddled, no sails. Caracurí (sea people) had more developed canoes than Yukurí (mountain people).

**Phase 2 — Sewn-plank trade canoes (post-516 CE):** Post-unification trade expansion. Planks split from hardwood logs, edges stitched with fiber cordage, seams caulked with tree resin. Breaks the single-tree size limit. Sails adopted (cotton textiles available through trade). Regular routes to Tapuya and the Capuyaquiran islands.

**Phase 3 — Canoe-towed barges (~800s–1300s CE):** During calm water periods (especially the Month of Still Sea), Guaji traders began lashing large wooden cargo barges — broad, flat platforms built from lashed logs — and towing them with teams of canoes. The barge carried bulk goods (cassava flour, pottery, stone tools) that would swamp individual canoes. This practice taught Guaji mariners:
- How large hulls behave in open water (different stability, different turning)
- Cargo distribution and weight on a broad platform
- That a large wooden structure can carry far more than any canoe
- The limits of towing — slow, hard to steer, vulnerable if the weather turns

The barge-towing practice was the conceptual bridge to the Kanu Maku. The leap: instead of lashing a barge to canoes, make the barge itself a self-sustaining vessel with its own hull, sail, and steering.

**Phase 4 — Kanu Maku (1400s CE):** The first true Guaji large vessel — a self-sustaining sewn-plank ship, not a barge towed by canoes. Broader, deeper hull with outrigger, cotton sail, partial deck. Only 3 built. See below for full design.

### Wood Fastening Without Iron — the *Yabikú* Technique

The critical innovation that made the Kanu Maku possible was a wood-to-wood fastening technique developed in the 1400s, inspired by the limitations of fiber stitching alone. Fiber cordage stitches planks edge-to-edge, but the stitches can chafe, rot, or work loose in rough seas. The new technique — called **Yabikú** (from *yabi* = hidden/old + *kú* = joint/fastening, "the old hidden joint") — uses interlocking carved joints instead of (or in addition to) fiber stitching.

**Principles (similar to Japanese kigumi):**
- Plank edges are carved with precise interlocking geometry — dovetails, stepped laps, tapered mortise-and-tenon shapes — so that the planks lock together under pressure. The more load on the joint, the tighter it becomes.
- No metal fasteners. No iron nails. The wood itself is the fastener.
- Joints are carved to accommodate wood movement — tropical humidity makes wood swell and shrink. Small calculated gaps let the joints breathe without losing grip.
- Some joints use wooden pegs (hardwood dowels) to prevent withdrawal — small carved pins driven into drilled holes.
- The technique requires master craftsmen who know the grain, characteristics, and behavior of each wood type. Years of apprenticeship, like the canoe-builders themselves.

**Application to the Kanu Maku:**
- Plank-to-plank seams: interlocking edge joints replace (or reinforce) fiber stitching. The hull is stronger and less dependent on cordage maintenance.
- Internal frames: ribs and cross-beams interlock with the hull planks using carved sockets and notches, giving the hull structural rigidity without metal brackets.
- Mast step: the mast base sits in an interlocking socket carved into the keel structure, distributing sail forces through the wood itself.
- Outrigger attachment: cross-booms interlock with the hull at carved notches, transferring stability forces through wood geometry.

**Construction time:** The Yabikú technique, combined with copper axes (available since the 1390s), made the Kanu Maku feasible but still extremely labor-intensive. Each vessel took an estimated **3–5 years** to build, comparable to large Pacific voyaging canoes (e.g., Tongan kalia, which took 3–5 years with iron tools). The process required:
- Master canoe-builders (yabiku-kú, specialists in the joinery technique)
- Fiber-workers for residual stitching and caulking
- Resin-workers for seam sealing
- Loggers and haulers for timber (80–100 workers to haul large logs from forest to shipyard, as documented in Pacific analogues)
- Sustained food supply for the workforce (feasts at each construction milestone, as in Pacific practice)
- Calm-season work for the final hull assembly (rough seas make precise joinery impossible)

The Yabikú technique was likely developed specifically for the Kanu Maku project — the need for a stronger, larger hull than anything previously built drove the innovation. After the Kanu Maku, the technique spread to war canoe construction and other wooden architecture.

### The Peace and the Cargo Ships (Y876–Y905, 1392–1421 CE)

The Treaty of Kariwári (Y876, 1392 CE) opened a period of lasting peace that lasted nearly 30 years. With the Tapuyic threat neutralized and the western trade route restored, trade flourished. This peace period — from the end of the Kari Wirá to the Boraku Revolt — was the most prosperous era in Guaji history.

**The Kanu Maku (Great Canoe):** The sewn-plank technique, proven in war, was adapted for trade. The war canoes were long and narrow for speed; the new cargo vessels were broader and deeper, designed for capacity rather than maneuverability.

**Design:**
- **Hull:** Sewn-plank construction — planks split from tropical hardwood, edges stitched with fiber cordage, seams caulked with tree resin. ~15–20m long, 3–4m beam (war canoes are ~15m but only 1.5–2m beam). Deeper belly than a war canoe — hull curves down to carry cargo. Rounded hull, shallow draft (can enter river mouths and port harbors). No keel — or a shallow carved keel from the dugout base. Cannot sail close to the wind, but downwind and reaching works fine — sufficient for the trade circuit (west on trade winds, east on countercurrent).
- **Outrigger:** Single outrigger lashed alongside on one side with cross-booms. Key stability innovation — lets the hull be broader and deeper without becoming tippy. Can be detached for harbor/river work.
- **Sail:** Single cotton sail on a pole mast — crab-claw or square shape. This is a sailing vessel, not a paddling vessel. The war canoe is paddled; the Kanu Maku is sailed. That's the fundamental difference — fewer crew, reliance on wind, not muscle. A small foresail on larger vessels for maneuverability.
- **Deck:** Partially decked — cargo hold covered with woven mats or wooden planks to protect from tropical rain. Open bow and stern for crew work. No enclosed cabin — tropical climate, no need for cold-weather shelter.
- **Steering:** Large steering oar at the stern (not a rudder — beyond this tech level). One steersman.
- **Crew:** 6–10 sailors (vs. 20–30 paddlers on a war canoe).
- **Cargo:** 3–5 tons. Gold and high-value goods stowed center (lowest, most stable point). Bulk goods (cassava flour, dried fish) stowed forward and aft.

**Production — only 3 built:** Without iron fasteners, sewn-plank construction at this scale is extremely labor-intensive. Each plank must be split from a hardwood log, shaped to curve, and stitched edge-to-edge with precisely woven cordage. The resin caulking must be watertight across the entire hull. A single Kanu Maku is a years-long project requiring the best canoe-builders, the best fiber-workers, and the best resin-workers in Guajicutea. Only **three** were ever built — each one a major investment, a prestige project of the Cacique Má.

**Copper axes:** By the 1400s, Guajicutea had been mining local copper for decades (since Bika Kuru's post-war push, ~1390s CE). Copper was not mass-produced, but master craftsmen — including canoe-builders — had access to copper axes. Copper axes were a significant improvement over obsidian and andesite for woodworking: they hold an edge longer, cut deeper into hardwood, and allow more precise shaping of curved planks. This made the Kanu Maku project feasible — still years-long, but achievable. Without copper axes, splitting and shaping enough planks for a 15–20m hull with pure stone tools would have been nearly impossible.

**The three vessels:**
1. **Kanu Maku** based at Yukubá (SW port) — assigned to the Tapuya trade route. Carried gold west, returned with maize, tin, textiles, and specialty woods.
2. **Kanu Maku** based at Waribá (NE port) — assigned to the eastern Capuyaquiran trade route. Carried goods to and from the tributary islands and trade partners.
3. **Kanu Maku** retrofitted to warship — the third vessel was converted for military use. The cargo hold covering was removed, replaced with an open deck for warriors. A raised fighting platform was added at the bow. The sail was kept (the warship can sail, not just paddle). More crew added (warriors, not cargo handlers). This became the **flagship** of the Guaji fleet — the largest, most powerful war vessel in Guaji history. Used to suppress the Boraku Revolt (Y905–Y908, 1421–1424 CE) and later as a prestige vessel for the Cacique Má.

The warship version was likely named differently from the cargo vessels — a war name, not a trade name. If the cargo ship is *Kanu Maku* (great canoe), the warship might be *Kanu Wirá* (hurricane canoe) — the most powerful vessel, named for the most powerful force the Guaji know.

**Gold exports to Tapuya:** The post-war peace opened the western trade route to the Tapuyic mainland. Guajicutea's gold — panned from stream gravels and weathered veins in the volcanic highlands (Mina Yaruma and other sites) — became the largest export to Tapuya. Gold was the ideal trade good: high value, low weight, perfect for sea transport. The Tapuyic mainland valued gold for ornamentation and status. The larger cargo ships made it possible to export significantly more gold than the old trade canoes could carry.

**Introduction of maize:** Trade with the Tapuyic mainland brought new crops to Guajicutea. The most significant was **maize** — a mainland cereal grain unknown on the islands. Maize was introduced during the peace period (likely ~Y890–Y900, 1405–1420 CE) through Tapuyan trade contacts. It did not replace the existing root-crop complex (cassava, yams, makú) but supplemented it. Maize grew well in the drier western leeward side of the island, where the root crops were less productive. By the time of the Boraku Revolt (Y905), maize was established in western Guajicutea but had not yet spread widely.

**Trade balance (post-war peace):**
- **Guaji exports:** Gold (largest), copper ornaments, volcanic stone tools (obsidian, andesite), pottery, dried fish, cassava flour, tobacco, sulfur
- **Guaji imports:** Maize (new), tin (for bronze development), textiles/cotton, wool/camelid fiber (Tapuya), shells/pearls, specialty woods, marriage partners

The post-war peace was the golden age of Guaji trade. The larger cargo ships, the gold exports, and the introduction of maize all transformed Guaji economy and agriculture. This prosperity was what the Boraku tributaries resented — they saw Guajicutea growing wealthy while they recovered from the war.

### Fortification

Post-war settlements were systematically fortified. Yukubá, which had been raided twice, received stone-and-limestone fortifications (replacing the wooden defenses that had failed). Watchtowers were built along the SW coast. The inland capital of Tabakurí (already moved from the coast after the 1143 hurricane) was further reinforced.

### Tributary System Solidified

The tributary system, which had been a tool of economic extraction, became a defensive buffer. The Boraku tributaries (54, 55, 57, 59, 60) were the front line — their loyalty was reinforced after the war. The tributary system was no longer just about tribute; it was about maritime defense.

### The Metal Gap — Copper and Bronze

**During the war:** The Tapuyic Empire had a metallurgical advantage. Mainland Tapuya had access to copper ores, and the Tapuri islanders brought metalworking knowledge. Tapuyic forces carried copper and bronze weaponry — axes, spearpoints, possibly short blades — in limited quantities. These were not mass-produced; they were likely carried by officers, champions, and elite warriors rather than common fighters. But even limited metal weapons against Guaji stone, bone, and hardwood gave the Tapuyic a serious edge in close combat.

This explains why Guaji took heavy losses even when they won. At the Battle of Makuwári (Y855), the Guaji needed ~80 canoes against ~40 Tapuyic vessels — superior numbers to compensate for inferior weapons. The sewn-plank war canoes gave Guaji speed and maneuverability, but in boarding actions, Tapuyic metal weapons were devastating.

**Post-war copper mining (Y876–Y920, 1392–1436 CE):** Bika Kuru recognized the metal gap immediately. Guajicutea already worked native copper from stream gravels (cold-hammered ornaments), but actual mining of copper ore was new. Bika Kuru commissioned prospecting in the volcanic highlands — copper deposits are plausible in volcanic settings (porphyry copper, volcanogenic sulfides). Progress was slow: mining, smelting, and hot-working were all new skills. The first locally-mined copper appeared during the late 1390s, but output was small for decades.

By Guani Buka's reign (Y890–Y916, 1405–1432 CE), copper production was steady but modest. Copper was used for weapons (axes, spearpoints) for nitainos and war chiefs, and for ornamentation. Common warriors still fought with stone and hardwood. The Boraku Revolt (Y905–Y908) was put down partly because the Guaji fleet now had some copper-armed officers — a small but visible advantage over tributary forces with no metal at all.

**Bronze casting (late 1450s, ~Y939–Y942):** The concept of tin mixing — alloying tin with copper to produce a harder, more durable metal — was introduced in the late 1450s, during the final years of Warí Kuru's reign. This was **independently developed** in Adonia, not imported from any Old World tradition. The discovery was likely experimental: Guaji smiths (or trade contacts from the Tapuyic mainland) found that certain ores, when heated with copper, produced a distinctly harder metal. The process and techniques are Adonian — different from Old World bronze production in their specifics, though the fundamental principle (copper + tin = harder alloy) is the same.

Tin sources: tin is the limiting factor. Copper is available in the volcanic highlands of Guajicutea, but tin deposits are rarer. Tin may be imported through trade with the Tapuyic mainland or found in specific geological settings on Guajicutea or nearby islands. The scarcity of tin limits bronze production — it cannot be mass-produced.

**Bronze in military use (Y943–Y964, 1458–1480 CE):** Under Bari Kuru's reign, bronze began appearing in military contexts:
- **Armor:** Bronze breastplates, arm guards, and helmets for high-ranking nitainos and war chiefs. Not full plate armor — more like reinforced leather/cotton padding with bronze plates sewn in. Protective gear for officers, not rank-and-file.
- **Ornamentation:** Bronze regalia for caciques and military leaders — armbands, chest discs, helmet fittings. Bronze complemented the existing gold and copper ornamentation. A military official in bronze armor and regalia was visually distinct — the metal marked rank.
- **Weapons:** Some bronze-edged weapons (axes, clubs with bronze fittings) for the highest-ranking warriors. But most fighters still used stone and hardwood — bronze was too scarce for general issue.

By 1500 CE, the Guaji military had a clear visual hierarchy: bronze-armored nitainos and war chiefs leading warriors with traditional stone and wood weapons. The bronze was a mark of authority and command, not a mass battlefield technology. The Tapuyic advantage that had shocked the Guaji during the Kari Wirá had been partially matched — but only at the officer level.

### Political Legacy

The Kari Wirá reinforced the dual-authority system. The war required coordination between the Cacique Má (military command) and the Warí (spiritual authority, morale, recording). The calendar corridor's dense war record — with multiple priestess handprints — shows the Warí's role as archivist and witness. No king attempted to seize sole power during the crisis; the system held.

Bika Kuru, who won the war, was celebrated as a warrior-king — but his regnal name "Path Rest" suggests the Guaji valued the return to peace more than the victory itself. The post-war rulers (Guani Buka, Warí Kuru) presided over a period of cultural and economic flowering built on the security the war had secured.

## The Boraku Revolt (Y905–Y908, 1421–1424 CE)

### Overview

The **Boraku Revolt** (*Boraku Buka* — "the Boraku uprising") was a tributary rebellion that occurred ~29 years after the Kari Wirá ended. The Boraku tributary islands (IDs 54, 55, 57, 59, 60), which had been the front line of the Kari Wirá, revolted against Guajicutea's continued tribute demands. The Murani tributaries (IDs 29, 30) joined the revolt. The revolt was crushed within three years by the post-war sewn-plank fleet.

### Background

The Boraku islands bore the worst of the Kari Wirá. They were the contested zone — raided by the Tapuyic fleet, fought over by both powers, and their communities were devastated. After the Treaty of Kariwári (Y876, 1392 CE), the Boraku remained Guaji tributaries. But the war had destroyed their canoes, depleted their populations, and disrupted their food supply.

By the reign of **Guani Buka** (Y890–Y916, 1405–1432 CE) — the "Open Gold" ruler — Guajicutea was experiencing post-war prosperity. Trade was flowing, wealth accumulating, the fleet was strong. But the Boraku tributaries were still recovering. The contrast between Guaji wealth and Boraku hardship bred resentment.

The tribute demands continued as if the war had never happened. The Boraku were expected to provide cotton, shells, pearls, food, and marriage partners — the same tribute as before the war. But they had less to give, and what they gave went to a Guajicutea that was visibly growing richer.

### Timeline

**Y905, Month of First Rains (1421 CE)** — The Boraku chiefs refuse the annual tribute. A tribute fleet arrives at the Boraku islands (ID 57, the largest) and is turned away. The Boraku chiefs declare they will pay no more tribute until their islands are rebuilt. Calendar corridor: crossed spears glyph — the first revolt mark.

**Y905, Month of Flying Fish (1421 CE)** — A Guaji tribute collector (a nitaino) is killed on Boraku island ID 57. The killing is deliberate — the Boraku chiefs send the collector's body back to Guajicutea in a canoe. This is the act of open rebellion. Calendar corridor: crossed spears with a broken canoe beneath — a tribute collector killed.

**Y905, Month of Great Waters (1421 CE)** — The Murani tributaries (IDs 29, 30) join the revolt. They refuse tribute and declare solidarity with the Boraku. The revolt now spans the SW and E tributary zones. Guajicutea faces a two-front tributary crisis.

**Y906, Month of Dry Wind (1422 CE)** — Guani Buka orders the punitive fleet. The sewn-plank war fleet — built during the Kari Wirá and maintained since — sails from Waribá. The fleet is larger and more capable than anything the tributaries can field. The tributaries have only small dugout canoes — no sewn-plank vessels, no war fleet.

**Y906, Month of Still Sea (1422 CE)** — **Battle of Karibári** (Sea Storm Battle). The Guaji fleet engages the combined Boraku-Murani canoe force near the Boraku islands. It is not a battle between equals — the sewn-plank war canoes outmaneuver and overwhelm the tributary dugouts. The revolt fleet is crushed. ~15 tributary canoes captured or broken. Calendar corridor: broken canoe glyph with a ring — a decisive suppression.

**Y907, Month of First Rains (1423 CE)** — The Murani surrender. Their islands (29, 30) are too small and too close to Guajicutea to sustain resistance. Tribute is restored with harsher terms: increased quotas, stationed nitaino garrisons on the two largest Murani islands.

**Y907, Month of Flying Fish (1423 CE)** — Guaji fleet lands on Boraku island ID 57 (the largest, ~1312 km²). Boraku holdout fighters resist briefly but are overwhelmed. Revolt leaders are captured. Calendar corridor: crossed spears with a captive mark — leaders taken.

**Y908, Month of Planting (1424 CE)** — Tribute fully restored. New terms imposed on all revolting tributaries:
- Increased tribute quotas (cotton, shells, pearls, food)
- Stationed nitaino garrisons on Boraku ID 57 and Murani ID 30
- Children of Boraku and Murani chiefs sent to Guajicutea as "guests" (marriage hostages / political hostages)
- Annual tribute collection reinforced with armed escort

Calendar corridor: clasped hands glyph — tribute restored. The revolt is over.

### Aftermath

The Boraku Revolt was short and decisive — the post-war fleet made quick work of the tributary canoes. But the harsher terms bred deeper resentment. The stationed garrisons and political hostages were new — before the revolt, tributaries were controlled through trade pressure and occasional raids. After the revolt, Guajicutea maintained direct military presence on the largest tributary islands.

This heavier-handed control may have contributed to the later loss of some tributaries under **Bari Kuru** (Y943–Y964, 1458–1480 CE). As Guajicutea's power declined with increasing storms and weakening trade, the garrisons became harder to maintain. Some tributaries may have drifted away — not through revolt, but through neglect, as Guajicutea could no longer project power to the outer islands.

- **Guani Buka's fleet** — the post-Kari Wirá sewn-plank war fleet, maintained and used to suppress the revolt

## The Northern Voyage — Chronicle of Kari Yabí (1502–1514 CE)

### The Aruanãyára Empire

The **Aruanãyára Empire** was a major continental power on Abyala, far to the north of the Capuyaquiran Sea. Both the Tapuya and the Aruanãyára were mainland powers on the same continent, and the Tapuyic traded with them directly — overland and by coastal routes. The Guaji knew of the Aruanãyára only through Tapuyic references: secondhand descriptions of a powerful northern empire with vast resources, including tin.

The Tapuyic acted as middlemen. Tin, metals, textiles, and other mainland goods flowed from the Aruanãyára south through Tapuyic territory, then to the Capuyaquiran Sea via Tapuri maritime routes. The Guaji bought these goods at Yukubá and Barikú — at a markup, filtered through Tapuyic hands.

Tin was the strategic bottleneck. Copper was available locally in Guajicutea's volcanic highlands (mined from the 1390s onward), but tin was not. Without tin, no bronze. The Tapuyic controlled the tin supply, and they knew it.

### The Kari Bika — Great Maps of the Sea

By the 1300s, generations of Guaji explorers and traders had compiled a body of cartographic knowledge: the **Kari Bika** ("sea marks" or "sea writing" — *kari* = sea, *bika* = carved mark/writing). These were not single maps but a tradition of geographic recording — bark cloth charts and carved wooden tablets showing the known islands, trade routes, and coastlines of the Capuyaquiran Sea and the Tapuyan mainland.

The Kari Bika included:
- **Island positions** — all known Capuyaquiran islands, grouped by archipelago, with relative distances and travel times
- **Trade routes** — the standard passages between islands, marked with current direction and seasonal wind notes
- **The Tapuyan coast** — the mainland coastline known from western trade, with major ports and landmarks
- **The northern approximation** — the key deduction. Traders on the northernmost Warabi islands (ID 47 and nearby) reported consistent signs of land to the north: bird migrations heading north and returning, cloud formations on the northern horizon at certain seasons, driftwood of species not from the Capuyaquiran islands, and refracted light patterns at dawn. The Kari Bika compilers approximated a landmass somewhere north of the Warabi islands, at an unknown distance. No Guaji had confirmed it. But the approximation was strong enough to plan a voyage.

The Kari Bika gave the Guaji the confidence — or the recklessness — to attempt the northern route. Without confirmed landfall, but with decades of accumulated inference.

### Strategic Motivation for the Kanu Maku

The Kanu Maku class — the first true large vessels, built in the 1400s — was motivated by more than just cargo capacity. The Guaji wanted to sail **directly north** to the Aruanãyára Empire, bypassing the Tapuyic middleman entirely.

The problem was geography. There were no islands to hop between the northernmost Warabi islands and the Aruanãyára coast. The northern route was open ocean — a straight northward passage across the Abyala Sea, against the prevailing northeast trade winds and the southward coastal current. The journey required a vessel large enough to carry provisions for weeks, sturdy enough to survive open-ocean conditions, and capable of sailing upwind on the return.

The Kanu Maku was the answer — or so the Guaji hoped. A 15–20m sewn-plank hull with outrigger, cotton sail, and steering oar could carry 3–5 tons of cargo and 6–10 crew. It was the first Guaji vessel that could theoretically make the open-sea passage.

### The Captain and the Chronicle

The voyage was commanded by **Kari Buka** — "Sea Opener" (*kari* = sea, *buka* = open/burst) — a Waribá-based navigator and trade captain. The chronicle of the voyage is called **Kari Yabí** — "The Hidden Sea" (*kari* = sea, *yabí* = hidden) — the story of the sea that was hidden until this voyage revealed it.

### The Outbound Voyage (1502 CE)

**Y987, Month of Great Waters (1502 CE)** — Kari Buka departed Waribá in the Waribá-based cargo Kanu Maku, loaded with trade goods: gold ornaments, volcanic stone tools, Makú tubers, cassava flour, dried fish, sulfur. The crew included experienced mariners and a bohíque as navigator-spiritual guide.

**Leg 1 — Island-hopping to Bukaí (520 km)**

The first leg was familiar territory. The Kanu Maku sailed northeast through the Greater Capuyaquiran, island-hopping along known trade routes — the same routes used for centuries by Guaji traders heading to the northern islands. 520 km of intermittent island-hopping, stopping at known trade islands for resupply and rest.

The final stop was **Bukaí** (island ID 47) — "the opening" (*buka* = open, *-í* = place) — the northernmost Warabi island, the last known land before the open sea. The Warabi people of Bukaí were friendly trade partners. They had reported the northern signs — birds, clouds, driftwood — that fed the Kari Bika approximation. Kari Buka and his crew waited on Bukaí for the calm window.

**The calm window — Still Sea month**

The open-sea crossing required a period of calm winds. The northeast trade winds blow constantly from Abyala toward the southwest, making northward sailing nearly impossible under normal conditions. But during **Still Sea month** — the brief calm after the first storm season — the trade winds weaken and the sea flattens. This is the doldrums window, when the ITCZ passes overhead and the wind dies. It lasts perhaps a few weeks. This is the only time a northward crossing is possible.

**Leg 2 — Open-sea crossing to Lacashe (280 km, ~180 miles)**

**Y987, Month of Still Sea (1502 CE)** — Kari Buka sailed north from Bukaí into open ocean. 280 km of open sea — no islands, no rest points, no landfall until the Aruanãyára coast. The Kanu Maku carried enough provisions for the crew during the crossing. The crossing likely took 7–10 days under oar and sail, depending on the remnant winds.

The Kanu Maku made landfall at the **tip of Lacashe** (modern-day Lacashe), at an Aruanãyára coastal village. The crew had done what no Guaji had ever done: crossed the open sea to the northern mainland and reached the Aruanãyára world directly.

### The Crash

The ship **crashed** on arrival. The details are uncertain — the hull may have been damaged on coastal rocks or reefs during the approach, or the vessel may have been driven ashore by a sudden wind shift after the calm window ended. Either way, the Kanu Maku was wrecked on the coast of Lacashe. The crew survived, the cargo largely survived, but the ship was beyond repair. One of only three Kanu Maku ever built — gone.

### The Regional Capital (500 km inland)

The Aruanãyára villagers convinced the crew to take their trade goods to a **regional capital** inland — another **500 km** from the coast. This was likely a major Aruanãyára administrative center, a place where foreign traders could be received and goods properly valued. The walk/transport inland took weeks.

At the regional capital, Kari Buka and his crew presented their goods — gold ornaments, volcanic stone tools, Makú, cassava flour, dried fish, sulfur. They had come to trade, and trade they did. But they had no ship to carry goods back, and no easy way home.

### The Long Stay (1502–1514)

The crew stayed at the regional capital and surrounding territory for **years**, trying to secure passage or funds to return. The Aruanãyára Empire was vast and wealthy — the Guaji mariners were exotic visitors from a distant sea, and they were received with curiosity. But the return was the problem: the open-sea route south was against the wind, and no Aruanãyára vessel was heading to the Capuyaquiran Sea.

Over the years, the crew split:
- **Some sailors decided to go via Tapuya** — traveling south through Tapuyic territory, the overland/coastal route back to the Capuyaquiran. They left earlier, in small groups, joining trade caravans or coastal vessels heading south through Tapuyic lands.
- **Some sailors stayed** — settling in the Aruanãyára Empire, taking local wives or husbands, assimilating. They chose the empire over the long road home.
- **Kari Buka decided to return.** The captain would not stay. He would go home the long way.

### The Return — Waku Yabí (1514 CE)

Kari Buka's return route covered approximately **1,900 km** through three legs:

**Leg 1 — Regional capital to the coast (400 km)**

Kari Buka and his remaining crew traveled 400 km back from the regional capital to the coast — the same distance they had come inland, now reversed. Overland or by river, through Aruanãyára territory, back to the sea.

**Leg 2 — Along the Tapuyan coast (1,000 km)**

From the coast of Lacashe, Kari Buka sailed/traveled south along the **Tapuyan coast** — 1,000 km of coastal passage. This was Tapuyic territory — the very middlemen the voyage had set out to bypass. Kari Buka may have traveled as a trader, a guest, or a curiosity — a Guaji captain in a foreign land, moving through the empire that had once been his people's enemy. He likely used coastal trade vessels, hitching passage on Tapuyic ships moving along the coast.

**Leg 3 — Island-hopping home (500 km)**

From the southern Tapuyan coast, Kari Buka island-hopped back through the Lesser Capuyaquiran — 500 km of familiar waters, known islands, and trade routes. This was the route he knew. The final stretch home.

**Y999 (1514 CE)** — Kari Buka arrived back in Guajicutea, twelve years after he left. He returned with firsthand knowledge of the Aruanãyára: their culture, their goods, their tin sources, their power. He also brought the bitter lesson that the direct northern route was beyond Guaji maritime technology — the ship could reach the Aruanãyára, but could not return by sea.

The Guaji called the return **Waku Yabí** — "the hidden return" (*waku* = cycle/return, *yabí* = hidden) — the return that was hidden, that took the hidden way, that no one expected. The chronicle of the entire voyage — outbound and return — is **Kari Yabí**, "The Hidden Sea."

### Consequences

- **The Kanu Maku was lost.** Only two remained (the Yukubá-based cargo vessel and the Kanu Wirá warship, by then likely retired). The Guaji could not replace it quickly — each took 3–5 years to build with master craftsmen.
- **The direct northern route was proven possible but not sustainable.** A ship could reach the Aruanãyára, but could not return by sea. The technology gap — sail design, hull shape, upwind capability — was too large.
- **The Tapuyic middleman position was reinforced.** Even the captain who bypassed them had to come back through them. The irony was not lost on anyone.
- **Tin remained a Tapuyic-controlled import.** The Guaji never established direct tin trade with the Aruanãyára before colonization disrupted the entire network.
- **Kari Buka's knowledge was invaluable but came too late.** His firsthand account of the Aruanãyára — their tin sources, their trade goods, their political structure — was the most detailed intelligence any Guaji had ever brought back. But by 1514, the colonial disruption was already beginning. There was no time to act on it.
- **Kari Yabí became the last great chronicle of the Guaji maritime age.** The captain who went north, crashed, and walked home through the empire he was trying to bypass — 1,900 km, twelve years, the long way around.

### Calendar Corridor Record

The calendar corridor records the voyage with a rare glyph: a canoe pointing north, beyond the edge of the carved space — the only glyph that extends past the corridor's border, suggesting a journey beyond the known world. The return in Y999 is marked with a walking figure and a small canoe glyph beside it — the long return, the hidden way home.

### Record and Transmission

The Kari Yabí was recorded in three stages:

**Stone (Bikumaku, ~1514–1520s):** The Guaji had no paper, but they had Bikumaku — glyph writing carved in stone and clay by priestly specialists. The calendar corridor received its glyph — a canoe pointing north beyond the border, a walking figure coming home. But the full chronicle was also carved in stone, likely on dedicated tablets or corridor walls, by bohíques who heard Kari Buka's account directly. This was the first written record — carved within years of his return, while the details were fresh.

**Oral (ongoing, 1514 onward):** Alongside the stone record, the story lived in the mouths of its tellers. Kari Buka told it first — to the cacique, to the Warí, to the assembled village. Then trained oral historians, likely from the bohíque tradition, memorized and retold the voyage across generations. The oral and stone traditions reinforced each other — the stone fixed key facts, the oral carried the narrative texture.

**Paper (~1530s, colonial trade era):** Paper arrived with the colonial trade era. By the 1530s, the Guaji were in contact with colonial traders who brought paper and ink. The Kari Yabí was transcribed to paper relatively quickly — while Kari Buka was still alive. This is remarkable: the primary source himself could verify, correct, and expand the written account. The paper transcription is likely the most detailed and most accurate version — dictated by or written with the captain who lived it.

This triple transmission — stone, oral, paper — makes the Kari Yabí one of the best-preserved Guaji chronicles. Most pre-colonial stories survived only in stone fragments and oral tradition. The Kari Yabí had all three, and the paper version was made while the man who lived it could still check it.
