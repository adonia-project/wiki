# Guajicutea Era Notes: 1905–1911 (Economic Decline and Political Chaos)

## Overview

Period from 1905 through 1911. While the world recovered from the Mild Depression by 1908, Guajicutea trailed behind — economic decline persisted to 1911. Mass unemployment, violent protests against the state, and a movement to return to traditional agricultural life (supported by both leftist and right-wing factions). The Asamblea Nacional, expanded to 150 seats in 1900, was extremely divided — physical fights broke out in the assembly. The anti-industrialization Movimiento de la Yaya gained momentum. Communist and fascist movements radicalised in the streets.

## Economy

### Extended Economic Decline (1905–1911)
- While the broader Mild Depression ended ~1908 for the world, Guajicutea's economy continued to decline through 1911.
- Guajicutea trailed the global recovery because of its interconnected economy — deeply dependent on exports, foreign-owned industry, and imported raw materials.
- Ironworks shutdown (1905) was a major blow — the island's principal heavy industrial installation closed.

## Domestic — Social

### Movimiento de la Yaya
- Anti-industrialization movement. Name from yaya (earth/ground), Guaji root — "return to the earth."
- Supported by BOTH leftist and right-wing factions:
  - Leftist objection: industrialization = worker exploitation, unfair wages, foreign ownership of labour.
  - Right-wing objection: industrialization = foreign influence, loss of traditional Guaji culture, dependence on imports.
  - Both converge on: return to traditional agricultural life (cassava, yams, makú — the three staples).
- Connected to cultural revival (Bikuismo) and pre-colonial interest — "return to the earth" is both economic and cultural.
- Anti-industrial, anti-foreign, pro-agrarian.
- Not a single party — a movement that cuts across party lines. Both PSG (socialist) and NPdA (fascist) elements participate, though for different reasons.

### Violent Protests Against the State
- Not just labour strikes — broader anti-state violence.
- Targets: government buildings, police stations, foreign-owned businesses.
- State response: police, possible military deployment.
- Escalation from the 1904 Huelga de Waribá and 1905 Noche de las Camisas Rojas.

## Political — Asamblea Nacional

### Expansion to 150 Seats (1900)
- Previously 32 seats (set by 1768 reform: 2 reps per distrito, 16 distritos).
- Expanded to 150 seats in 1900 — reflecting population growth (~325k by 1900) and democratic reform.
- Seats by population (established by 1803 amendment).

### Extreme Division and Assembly Violence
- 6+ parties competing: PdI, PpLT, PpE, PdT, PSG, NPdA.
- Assembly extremely divided — no clear governing majority.
- Physical fights broke out in the assembly between members.
- The expansion to 150 seats meant more voices, more factions, more chaos.
- Parliamentary breakdown — governance difficult with this level of fragmentation.

## Parties (c. 1905–1911)

| Party | Ideology | Base | Formed |
|-------|----------|------|--------|
| PdI | Free trade, international integration | Commercial, urban | 1832 |
| PpLT | Rural/worker, labour protections, amendments | Rural, workers | 1851 |
| PpE | Nativist labour, protectionist, anti-immigration | Nativist workers | 1861 |
| PdT | Urban, multi-racial workers' coalition, inclusive labour | Urban, all races | 1883 |
| PSG | Socialist, explicitly socialist programme | Industrial workers, unemployed | ~1901–1908 |
| NPdA | Fascist, ultranationalist, protectionist, anti-foreign | Nationalists, anti-foreign | ~1901–1908 |

- 6 parties in the Asamblea. Extreme fragmentation.
- PSG and NPdA both rising — elections and streets.
- PpLT in power (president Lorenzo Mora, 1901–1905) but overwhelmed by economic crisis and assembly chaos.
- Traditional parties (PdI, PpLT, PpE) losing ground to PSG and NPdA.
