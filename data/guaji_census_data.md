# Guajicutea Census & Employment Data (from 1885)

## National Census Bureau

The National Census Bureau was established in 1885 as part of the greater bureaucracy expansion of the early 1880s. The first systematic census was conducted in 1885, with subsequent censuses every 5 years (1890, 1895, 1900, 1905, 1910). The bureau tracked population, employment by sector, unemployment, and (after 1904) hours and wages.

The census system was reformed under the Third Republic constitution (December 18, 1947). The first official census under the new system was conducted in 1950, with subsequent censuses every 10 years (1960, 1970, 1980, etc.). The 1950 census and all subsequent censuses recorded: age, race and ethnicity, family size, sex, and date of birth. The pre-war 5-year employment census (1885–1910) was replaced by the 10-year comprehensive census.

## Methodology Notes

- **Labor force**: ~50% of total population (working-age adults, including children before 1904 child labor ban). After 1904, child labor ban slightly reduced labor force participation.
- **Sector definitions**:
  - Agriculture: farming, fishing, guano extraction
  - Industry: ironworks, manufacturing, mining, shipbuilding (Astillero Real)
  - Services: trade, port operations, government, education, domestic service
  - Construction: infrastructure, building
- **Unemployed**: actively seeking work. Does not include those who left the labor force for subsistence agriculture (Movimiento de la Yaya effect post-1905).
- **Not in labor force**: subsistence farmers, homemakers, children (post-1904), elderly, disabled.

## Notes by Census Year

### 1885 (First Census)
- Economy growing. Ironworks operating (est. 1864). Mining licensing scheme just beginning.
- Agriculture dominant (65% of labor force).
- Industry small but growing (ironworks, mining, Astillero Real).
- Services expanding with government bureaucracy.
- Unemployment low (5%) — natural frictional unemployment.

### 1890
- Continued industrialization. Farm mechanization pushing rural-to-urban migration.
- Agriculture share declining (60%) as workers move to industry and services.
- Industry growing (15%) — ironworks, mining expansion, manufacturing.
- Unemployment stable (5%).

### 1895
- PpLT corruption scandals beginning to affect economic confidence.
- Agriculture continues declining as share (55%).
- Industry continues growing (17%).
- Unemployment stable (5%) but beginning to rise toward end of decade.

### 1900
- Pre-depression. Economy at peak of industrial growth.
- Agriculture now 50% of labor force — milestone: industry + services + construction = 45%.
- Industry at 20% — highest point. Ironworks, mining, manufacturing, Astillero Real all active.
- Unemployment still low (5%) but depression about to hit.

### 1905
- Mild Depression (1901–1908) underway. Ironworks shuts down this year.
- Mass unemployment jumps to 11% (~19,000) — ironworks closure + industrial contraction.
- Agriculture share drops to 48% (industrial workers not yet shifting back).
- Industry drops to 18% (ironworks closure reducing industrial employment).
- Construction slows (4%) — less investment during depression.
- Violent protests, Noche de las Camisas Rojas.

### 1910
- World recovering by 1908 but Guajicutea trailing. Extended decline persists.
- Movimiento de la Yaya effect: people leaving industrial workforce for subsistence agriculture. Agriculture share rebounds to 55%.
- Industry contracted to 12% (ironworks gone, other industry shrinking).
- Unemployment at 15% (~26,000) — still high. Some improvement from 1905-1908 peak (~19%) as people shift to agriculture, but still depressed.
- Construction minimal (3%) — little investment.
- Services contracting slightly (16%) — reduced trade, government revenue constraints.

## Sector Trends

### Agriculture
- Dominant employer throughout, declining share from 65% (1885) to 48% (1905).
- Rebounds to 55% by 1910 — Movimiento de la Yaya pushing people back to the land.
- Reflects both economic necessity (no industrial jobs) and ideological movement (return to traditional life).

### Industry
- Grows from 12% (1885) to peak of 20% (1900).
- Collapses to 12% (1910) after ironworks shutdown and depression.
- Ironworks closure (1905) is the single biggest industrial job loss event.
- Mining also contracts (depression reduces demand, licensing revenues fall).

### Services
- Grows from 13% (1885) to 18% (1900) — government expansion, trade, port activity.
- Contracts slightly to 16% (1910) — reduced trade, government revenue constraints.

### Construction
- Small sector throughout (5-7%).
- Peaks at 7% (1900) during industrial expansion.
- Collapses to 3% (1910) — no investment during depression.

### Unemployment
- Low and stable (5%) from 1885–1900.
- Jumps to 11% (1905) — ironworks shutdown + depression.
- Peaks ~19% (1908, estimated between census years).
- Still high at 15% (1910) — some improvement as people shift to agriculture but economy still depressed.

### Notes on 1950 Census

- First comprehensive census under the Third Republic. Conducted January 18, 1950 (same day as first election).
- Baby boom visible in 0–4 age group (9.6% of population — elevated for a small post-war population).
- Sex ratio approximately balanced (49.5% male / 50.5% female). War casualties slightly reduced male share.
- Zong population reduced from ~25k (pre-war) to ~18k — internment, camp riot (October 1941), and wartime casualties. Some internees released after liberation (July 1947).
- Sartan share declined from ~10% to ~9% — Volisanian military personnel withdrew 1947–1948. Sartan civilians remain.
- "Other" category includes AC consultants and Asikyira aid workers present during reconstruction.
- Average household size 4.8 — multi-generational households common during reconstruction and baby boom.
- Fertility rate ~4.2 births per woman — baby boom level, well above replacement rate (2.1). Consistent with the 0–4 age group being 9.6% of population.
- Labor force ~50% of population, consistent with historical pattern.
- Date of birth recorded for all residents — enables age tracking and future census verification.

## Population Trajectory (1947–1970)

| Year | Population | Notes |
|------|------------|-------|
| 1947 | ~453,000 | End of war. Famine. Liberation (July 3, 1947). |
| 1950 | ~490,000 | First census. Baby boom underway. Reconstruction. |
| 1956 | ~570,000 | Baby boom + reconstruction growth. Waribá rebuild nearing completion. |
| 1960 | ~680,000 | Natural increase + immigration begins. Tourism boom accelerating. |
| ~1975 | ~1,750,000 | Mid-decade partial census/estimate. Growth continuing but immigration slowing from peak. |
| 1970 | ~1,400,000 | Peak immigration + natural increase. Fertility 4.5. Density ~427/km². |

- Growth rate 1947–1950: ~2.5% per year (baby boom + food restoration + reconstruction).
- Growth rate 1950–1956: ~2.6% per year (baby boom + natural increase).
- Growth rate 1956–1970: ~6.6% per year (3.5% natural increase at fertility 4.5 + 3.1% from immigration ~34,000/year average).
- 1954 global postwar recession: Guajicutea buffered by reparations funds. Not severely affected.
- Later 1960s: large child population strained resources. AC loans required. AC pushed for reduction of natalist policies.
- 1970 density: ~427/km². High but feasible (Malta ~1,300/km², Caribbean islands 300-500/km²).
