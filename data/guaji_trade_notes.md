# Guaji Trade Notes

Internal working notes on Guajicutea's trade networks, ports, and maritime geography.

## Adonia geography context

- **Planet**: Adonia
- **Sea**: Capuyaquiran Sea
- **Greater Capuyaquiran**: eastern archipelago (Guajicutea is here, largest island)
- **Lesser Capuyaquiran**: western archipelago
- **Abyala**: continent to the north; Tapuya is on or near Abyala's coast
- **Aethiopean Ocean**: open ocean to the south

## Ocean currents around Guajicutea

Guajicutea sits at ~5.5–6.5°N, in the equatorial zone. Three forces shape the currents:

1. **Northeast trade winds** — blow from Abyala (north) toward the southwest, pushing surface water west and south across the Capuyaquiran Sea.
2. **Equatorial Countercurrent** — flows eastward at roughly 3–10°N, driven by water piling up on the western side of the sea (near Lesser Capuyaquiran). Guajicutea is in this band.
3. **Abyala continental boundary** — water pushed west by the trades along Abyala's south coast creates a southward coastal current, the highway toward Tapuya.

### Practical effects

- **West coast**: wind-driven surface current flows west-southwest toward Lesser Capuyaquiran and Tapuya. Easy outbound passage.
- **East coast**: Equatorial Countercurrent flows eastward toward the rest of the Greater Capuyaquiran. Slower but usable.
- **South coast**: exposed to open Aethiopean Ocean; westward/southward drift; dangerous landing, not a trade route.
- **North coast**: against wind and current; hard passage to Abyala except in seasonal wind shifts.

This creates a natural round-trip circuit: west on the wind, east on the countercurrent.

## Trade ports

### Yukubá

- **Location**: SW bay, ~(-83.026, 5.907)
- **Role**: Ancient western trade port; pre-516 Yukurí port; gateway to Lesser Capuyaquiran and mainland Tapuya
- **Character**: Large, sheltered bay below Monte Yukumá; wealthy from mainland trade; sacred/profane tension because it sits in the volcano's shadow
- **Status after 516 CE**: Remained active for the mainland trade, but awkward for cross-island movement because the volcano blocks direct eastward routes

### Waribá

- **Location**: NE coast, in a river-formed inlet, ~(-82.765, 6.088)
- **Role**: Eastern trade port; Caracurí port facing Greater Capuyaquiran
- **Character**: Two rivers create a landmass jutting out, forming a protected inlet. Good natural harbor. Caracurí territory, sea-people culture.
- **Current**: Served by the eastward Equatorial Countercurrent.

### Barikú

- **Location**: West coast south of Yukubá, in a small cove away from harsh cliffs, ~(-82.934, 5.808)
- **Role**: Post-516 cross-island redistribution port; unified Guaji hub
- **Character**: Smaller cove than Yukubá, but positioned so the cross-island route can swing south around Monte Yukumá rather than climbing over it.
- **Historical development**: Built or expanded after unification in 516 CE to move goods between west and east efficiently. Handles the bulk of resupply and redistribution for transit canoes.

## Cross-island route

- **Pre-516**: Yukurí (west) and Caracurí (east) traded separately; no need for a high-volume cross-island route.
- **Post-516**: Unified Guaji needed to move goods between the mainland trade (west) and the archipelago trade (east). The direct route over Monte Yukumá is too difficult. The practical route runs south from Yukubá/Barikú, around the volcano's southern foothills, then northeast to Waribá.
- Barikú exists because it sits at the west end of this southern detour, avoiding the mountain barrier.

## Trade goods

### Exports from Guajicutea

- **Gold and copper ornaments** — highest-value goods; Mina Yaruma gold, cold-worked metal
- **Volcanic stone tools** — obsidian blades, andesite adzes, grinding stones
- **Pottery** — volcanic clay fires well; practical trade good
- **Makú** — native Adonian tuber; possibly unique to or best on Guajicutea
- **Dried fish / cassava flour** — bulk provisions for smaller islands
- **Tobacco** — ritual/cash crop if it grows well in volcanic soil
- **Sulfur** — ritual/medicinal use by bohíques on other islands

### Imports to Guajicutea

- **Cotton and cotton textiles** — not grown locally in quantity
- **Wool/camelid fiber from Tapuya** — ceremonial capes
- **Shells, coral, pearls** — from atoll islands with reefs
- **Specialty woods** — species not available on Guajicutea
- **Marriage partners / political alliances** — hereditary chiefly exchanges

## Hospitality and trade systems

- **Designated landing beaches** — specific coves where visiting canoes were expected; landing elsewhere risked being read as a raid.
- **Guest houses (bohíos)** — temporary shelters maintained by the local cacique near landing beaches.
- **Fresh water and provisions** — clay jars of water, dried cassava bread, smoked fish, fruit for resupplying canoes.
- **Canoe repair** — materials and labor for fixing hulls, replacing paddles, patching leaks.
- **Gift protocols** — formal exchange between visiting caciques and local hosts; the gift is the price of safe landing and establishes reciprocal obligation.
- **Hereditary trade partnerships** — specific families on specific islands bound by generations of hosting each other.
- **Trade chiefs** — specific nitainos responsible for maintaining relationships with particular islands or archipelagos.
- **Seasonal timing** — voyages timed to lunar calendar and trade-wind seasons.

## Two-tribe trade dynamics

- **Pre-516**: Yukurí (western/mountain) dominated mainland-facing trade at Yukubá; Caracurí (eastern/sea) controlled archipelago-facing trade at Waribá.
- **516 CE**: Yukubara buried Guajicutea Vieja and weakened the Yukurí; Caracurí absorbed them.
- **Post-516**: Unified Guaji controlled both networks. The Caracurí, as sea people, likely managed the combined trade system, but Yukurí descendants retained influence in the western/mainland trade.
- **Wealth imbalance**: West coast (mainland trade) remained wealthier per capita; east coast (archipelago trade) had more usable land and population.

## Sacred/profane trade restrictions

- Gold from the northern mountain (Mina Yaruma) was profane and tradeable.
- Gold found in Yukumá's sacred streams might have been treated differently — perhaps not mined, only collected as already given.
- Some sacred objects (certain zemís, catacomb regalia, feather cloaks of high rank) were probably not traded.

## The Northern Trade — Aruanãyára and Tin

### The Tin Problem

Copper was available locally in Guajicutea's volcanic highlands (mined from the 1390s onward), but **tin** was not. Without tin, no bronze. Tin was the limiting factor for bronze production — and bronze was the strategic material for military armor and regalia.

Tin came through **Tapuyic trade**. The Tapuyic, as a mainland power on Abyala, had direct overland and coastal trade with the **Aruanãyára Empire** — a vast continental power to the north. The Aruanãyára had access to tin (whether from their own territory or through their own trade networks). The Tapuyic bought tin and other mainland goods from the Aruanãyára, then sold them to the Guaji at a markup through the maritime route via the Lesser Capuyaquiran.

The Guaji knew about the Aruanãyára only through Tapuyic references — secondhand descriptions of a powerful northern empire. They had no direct contact until the Kari Yabí voyage of 1502 CE.

### The Kari Bika — Sea Maps

By the 1300s, Guaji explorers and traders had compiled the **Kari Bika** — "sea marks" — a cartographic tradition of bark cloth charts and carved wooden tablets showing known islands, trade routes, and coastlines. The Kari Bika included an approximation of land to the north of the Warabi islands, deduced from bird migrations, cloud formations, driftwood, and current patterns. No Guaji had confirmed the landmass, but the approximation was strong enough to plan a voyage.

### The Northern Voyage (1502–1514 CE)

In 1502 CE, Captain **Kari Buka** ("Sea Opener") sailed the Waribá-based Kanu Maku north from Guajicutea:
- **Leg 1**: 520 km island-hopping to **Bukaí** (island ID 47), the northernmost Warabi island — friendly resupply stop
- **Leg 2**: 280 km open-sea crossing (~180 miles) from Bukaí to the tip of **Lacashe**, during Still Sea month (the calm window when trade winds weaken)

The ship crashed on arrival at an Aruanãyára coastal village in Lacashe. The crew survived, took their goods 500 km inland to an Aruanãyára regional capital, and spent years trying to secure passage home.

The crew split: some went via Tapuya earlier, some stayed in the Aruanãyára Empire, and Kari Buka returned through Tapuya — 400 km to the coast, 1,000 km along the Tapuyan coast, 500 km island-hopping home. He arrived in 1514 CE, twelve years after departing.

The chronicle of the voyage is **Kari Yabí** — "The Hidden Sea." The return is **Waku Yabí** — "the hidden return." Full details in `guaji_tapuyic_war_notes.md`.

### Consequences for Trade

- The direct northern route was proven possible but not sustainable — the ship could reach the Aruanãyára but could not return by sea
- Tin remained a Tapuyic-controlled import — the Guaji never established direct tin trade before colonization
- Kari Buka's firsthand knowledge of the Aruanãyára came too late to act on — by 1514, colonial disruption was beginning

## Open questions

- Do other islands in the Capuyaquiran archipelagos have their own metals, or does Guajicutea dominate the regional gold trade?
- How far does the Equatorial Countercurrent extend eastward? Does it reach all the way across the Greater Capuyaquiran?
- Are there seasonal wind reversals that allow northward travel to Abyala/Tapuya? (Yes — Still Sea month provides a brief calm window, confirmed by the Kari Yabí voyage)
- Do the Guaji maintain permanent trade enclaves or seasonal fairs on other islands?
- How did colonization disrupt these trade networks?
- What exactly did the Aruanãyára trade goods look like? What did Kari Buka bring back besides knowledge?
