# First Contact and the Colonial Trade Era (1516–1538+ CE)

## Background

### The Lacashe Colony (Early 1516)

The **Empire of Burawa** — a maritime colonial power — established the **Lacashe colony** on the northern coast of Abyala in early 1516. This was the same coast where Kari Buka's Kanu Maku had crashed fourteen years earlier (1502). The Aruanãyára Empire, once vast, was weakening — and the Burawi had taken Aruanãyára coastal territory at Lacashe.

The colonization of Lacashe was the first step in Burawa's expansion into the Capuyaquiran region. From Lacashe, Burawi explorers would begin probing the islands to the south.

### First Sightings (April 1516)

By **April 1516**, word had reached Guajicutea of massive vessels sighted in the northern waters. Local records — oral accounts and Bikumaku notes from coastal watchtowers — described ships far larger than anything in the Capuyaquiran Sea. The Guaji had never seen ocean-going ships of Burawi scale.

The political elite — the Cacique Má, the Warí, and the council of caciques — understood these to be people of a far-off tribe looking to trade. There were words of a **new empire from the sea** with massive ships that had taken land from the Aruanãyára. This connected to what Kari Buka had told them: he had been to the Aruanãyára coast, he had seen the mainland. Now a new power had arrived on that same coast.

**Kari Buka was still alive in 1516** — an old man by then, but a living witness to the northern mainland. He had walked through the Aruanãyára Empire and returned through Tapuya. His knowledge of the north, the Aruanãyára, and the mainland geography made him a strategic asset. The political elite likely consulted him as sightings of the great ships multiplied.

## First Contact — December 5, 1520

### Guled Badweyn's Expedition

On **December 5, 1520**, a Burawi expedition led by **Guled Badweyn** — a Burawi explorer and colonial officer — arrived at Guajicutea. Guled had been conducting a trade and exploration mission with the islands off the coast of Lacashe, making his way west through the Capuyaquiran archipelago.

Guled's **directive from the Empire of Burawa** was to make note of lands and armies — to assess possibilities for trade or expansion. He was scouting.

### The Encounter

Communication was attempted using a **translator kidnapped earlier in the year from the Lacashe colony**. The translator likely spoke an Aruanãyára or Lacashe dialect — not Guaji. The language gap was vast: Burawi, Aruanãyára, and Guaji (Arawak-based) are entirely unrelated language families. Communication was hard.

But trade etiquette is universal. Guled's troops dropped off **gold coins and maize** as gifts — a peaceful signal. The Guaji understood: these strangers came to trade, not to raid. The gold coins were exotic — minted currency, a concept the Guaji did not have (they traded by barter and weight). The maize was familiar — the Guaji had been growing it for nearly a century, introduced through Tapuyan trade ~1405–1420 CE. The same grain, arriving from a different direction.

### Guled Badweyn's Observations

In his notes, Guled recorded:

- **The coastal cities were the largest he had seen so far.** He had not yet reached Tapuya — Guajicutea's ports (likely Yukubá, Waribá, or Barikú) were the most developed settlements he'd encountered in the region.
- **"Cosmopolitan."** The Guaji ports felt like the many smaller port cities in Burawa — active trade hubs with multiple peoples, goods from various islands, functioning markets. But with **much smaller vessels** — sewn-plank canoes and Kanu Maku were impressive for the Capuyaquiran, but tiny beside Burawi ocean-going ships.
- **He assumed more people lived there.** He only saw the coast. The interior — the inland capital of Tabakurí, the highland villages, the catacombs — was invisible to him.

Guled departed the following day. He had come to assess, not to stay. His notes would guide Burawa's next moves.

## The Trade Era (1523–1530)

### Rotating Trade Pattern

From **1523 to 1530**, the Lacashe colony sent trade vessels to Guajicutea in a **rotating pattern** — regular but not continuous visits, likely seasonal. The trade was documented in Burawi colonial records.

The trade goods exchanged are not fully recorded, but likely included:
- **Burawi exports to Guajicutea**: metal tools, textiles, possibly weapons, exotic goods from the Burawi empire
- **Guaji exports to Burawa**: gold ornaments, volcanic stone tools, Makú, dried fish, cassava flour, tobacco, sulfur — the same trade goods the Guaji had been exporting for centuries

This was the first time the Guaji traded with a power that was not connected to the Tapuyic mainland network. The Burawi were a new trade partner — and a colonial one.

### The Paper Era

It was during this trade era that **paper arrived in Guajicutea** — brought by Burawi traders. The Guaji had Bikumaku (stone and clay writing), but paper was new: lightweight, portable, high-capacity. By the **1530s**, while Kari Buka was still alive, the **Kari Yabí was transcribed to paper** — the chronicle of his northern voyage, written down while the captain could still verify the details. Paper transformed Guaji record-keeping, though Bikumaku remained the sacred carving tradition.

## The Great Pestilence (1530s–1538)

### The Disease

By the early 1530s, the **Great Pestilence** struck the Capuyaquiran region. This was part of a broader pandemic — Old World diseases spreading through Adonia as colonial contact increased. The Burawi, coming from a world where these diseases were endemic, carried them to populations with no immunity.

The disease hit Guajicutea hard. The Guaji had no natural resistance to Old World pathogens — no prior exposure, no inherited immunity. The pestilence spread through the trade ports first (Yukubá, Waribá, Barikú — the points of contact with Burawi ships), then inland.

### Population Impact

| Period | Year (CE) | Population | Notes |
|--------|-----------|------------|-------|
| Pre-contact peak | 1500 | ~40,000 | Population peak before colonization |
| First contact | 1520 | ~38,000 | Some pre-contact decline |
| Pestilence onset | ~1530 | ~36,000 | Disease begins spreading |
| **Pestilence peak** | **1538** | **~25,000** | **30% population loss. Stabilizing.** |

By **1538**, the disease had taken roughly **30% of the population** — approximately 12,000 dead. The population stabilized around ~25,000–28,000 as the first wave passed and survivors developed some resistance.

### Trade Reduction and the Chiefs' Response

During the pestilence, **Burawi ships came less and less**. The Guaji chiefs — seeing the connection between foreign ships and disease — attempted to **reduce trade**. This was a quarantine measure: limit contact, limit the spread.

But the chiefs' attempt to protect their people had a devastating economic side effect:

### Domestic Depression

The trade reduction caused a **domestic depression**. The Guaji economy had become dependent on Burawi trade — metal tools, textiles, and other goods that could not be produced locally. When the trade stopped, the economy contracted. Craftsmen who produced goods for export lost their market. Chiefs who taxed trade lost their revenue. The tributary system, already weakened, strained further.

### Internal Conflicts

The depression bred **internal conflicts**. Resource scarcity, economic hardship, and the trauma of epidemic disease created fractures:
- **Blame**: Some blamed the chiefs for allowing foreign trade in the first place. Others blamed the Burawi. Some blamed the Warí and the priestesses for failing to spiritually protect the people.
- **Trade faction vs. isolation faction**: Some wanted to resume trade (the economy was collapsing without it). Others wanted to seal the islands completely (the trade was bringing death).
- **Tributary resistance**: The tributary islands, already resentful after the Boraku Revolt and the harsher terms imposed, saw the crisis as an opportunity. With Guajicutea weakened, tribute enforcement became impossible.

## Political Upheaval (1530s–1540s)

The dual-authority system — Cacique Má (secular king) and Warí (high priestess) — faced its greatest test since the usurpation crisis of Guani Waku (912–938 CE). The pestilence, the depression, and the internal conflicts placed enormous strain on the political structure.

## Key Terms

- **Empire of Burawa** — maritime colonial power; established the Lacashe colony (1516); source of first contact and colonial trade
- **Guled Badweyn** — Burawi explorer who made first contact with Guajicutea (December 5, 1520)
- **Lacashe colony** — Burawi colony on the northern Abyala coast, established early 1516 on former Aruanãyára territory
- **Great Pestilence** — Old World disease pandemic, struck the Capuyaquiran region in the 1530s; 30% population loss on Guajicutea by 1538
- **Trade Era (1523–1530)** — period of rotating Burawi trade vessels from Lacashe colony to Guajicutea
- **Kari Buka** — captain of the Kari Yabí voyage (1502–1514); still alive during first contact and colonial trade era; likely consulted as a strategic elder
