# Guajicutea Era Notes: 1917–1935 (Fascist Infiltration and the Road to War)

## Overview

Period from 1917 through 1935. The economic recovery that began in 1915–1916 continued under liberal governance, but Guajicutea's political landscape was increasingly subverted by fascist influence from two directions: Volisania (National Volisan Party, from 1911) and Sarta (fascist party, from 1928). Volisania leased parts of Waribá port for naval ships as part of a military buildup planning an invasion of Zong Balboa. The Círculo Volisano, a Sartan front group, bought the largest newspaper in Guajicutea (1920) and ran an anti-Zong press campaign that stoked fears against the small Zong minority. Zong sent a condemnation in 1924. The Escándalo de Balboa — the liberal president's visit to Zong Balboa during a murder accusation — discredited the liberals and brought conservatives to power. The conservative party was gradually taken over by fascist elements, culminating in the Atentado de Waribá (1930), a staged bombing that enabled emergency powers, a subversive Jefe de la Asamblea role, and the suspension of elections. A religious revival of both Guaji traditional religion and Sartan Catholicism produced communal conflicts. The era ended in 1935 with the opening of the Balboa campaign of the Great Adonian War, when Volisanian and Balboan forces invaded Zong Balboa.

## Foreign Fascist Influence

### Volisania: National Volisan Party (from 1911)
- The National Volisan Party, fascist movement in Volisania from 1911, sought to project influence into Guajicutea.
- Military buildup (1920s–1930s): Volisania leased parts of Waribá port for large sums of money to store naval ships. The ships were stationed for a planned invasion of Zong Balboa.
- The lease of Waribá port was a major source of revenue for Guajicutea but also a source of foreign military presence on Guajicutean soil.
- The military buildup was framed as protection against Zong, reinforcing the anti-Zong press campaign.

### Sarta: Fascist Party (from 1928)
- The fascist party rose in Sarta in 1928 and heavily attempted to influence politics in Guajicutea through economic and political meddling.
- Sartan fascist groups supported the subversion of Guajicutea's conservative party, helping fascist elements take control from within.
- The Sartan fascist government provided funding and organisational support to the Círculo Volisano and fascist elements within Guajicutea.

## Media Manipulation

### Círculo Volisano
- A Sartan cultural society that served as a front for Volisanian fascist influence in Guajicutea.
- Technically a Sartan organisation, which sidestepped the 1910 ban on Volisanian ownership of private industries. The ban targeted Volisanian entities, not Sartan ones.
- Bought El Guajicuteano, the largest newspaper in Guajicutea, in 1920.
- Existing laws forbade foreign ownership of media, but enforcement was weak and the Círculo Volisano operated through local intermediaries.
- Ran an anti-Zong press campaign, decrying the Zong population as violent and dangerous despite their being a very small minority.
- Stoked fears through sensationalist reporting, connecting Zong people to crime, violence, and alleged espionage.

### El Guajicuteano
- The largest newspaper in Guajicutea, bought by the Círculo Volisano in 1920.
- Previously a mainstream newspaper, became a propaganda outlet for anti-Zong sentiment.
- Published sensationalist stories about Zong violence, crime, and alleged espionage.
- Framed the Zong condemnation (1924) and the president's visit to Zong Balboa as subservience.
- Other newspapers followed El Guajicuteano's lead, amplifying the anti-Zong campaign.

## The Zong Condemnation and the Escándalo de Balboa (1924)

### Zong Condemnation (1924)
- Zong sent a formal condemnation to Guajicutea protesting the treatment of Zong people in Guajicutea.
- The condemnation was a response to the anti-Zong press campaign run by El Guajicuteano and other outlets.
- Newspapers had been decrying the Zong population as violent and perpetrators, stoking fears.
- The liberal government was trying to prevent the situation from escalating but was struggling against the media campaign.

### Escándalo de Balboa
- The liberal president visited Zong Balboa on a diplomatic mission to repair relations after the condemnation.
- During the visit, a Zong man living in Guajicutea was accused of murdering his Guaji wife/partner. The case was genuine domestic violence, not a political killing.
- El Guajicuteano and the fascist press connected the two events: "While a Guaji woman is murdered by a Zong man, our president bows to Zong."
- The visit was framed as subservience and weakness. The president was accused of prioritising Zong relations over Guaji justice.
- The scandal discredited the liberal government and contributed to the conservative victory in the 1925 election.

## Religious Revival

### Guaji Traditional Religion
- A revival of pre-colonial Guaji spiritual practices, connected to the Bikuismo cultural movement and the rediscovery of the catacombs (1892).
- Growing interest in traditional ceremonies, community rituals, and the three powers (sea, mountain, sky).
- Seen by some as a cultural resistance movement against foreign influence — a return to Guaji roots.

### Sartan Catholic Revival
- A revival of Sartan Catholic religious practice, connected to the fascist movement's promotion of traditional Catholic values.
- The Sartan Catholic Church in Guajicutea was split between fascist-aligned clergy (who supported the conservative/fascist political movement) and anti-fascist clergy.
- The church served as a social organising tool for the fascist movement, mobilising congregations around traditional values and anti-Zong sentiment.

### Conflicts
- Tensions between Guaji religion revivalists and Sartan Catholic revivalists.
- Conflicts over cultural policy, education, and the role of religion in public life.
- The fascist movement exploited religious divisions to consolidate power, framing the Guaji revival as backward and the Sartan Catholic revival as modern and patriotic.

## Conservative Party and Fascist Takeover

### 1925 Election
- The Escándalo de Balboa discredited the liberals. The conservatives won the election.
- The conservative government was led by a popularish president, but the assembly was split between multiple parties.
- The PpE (Partido de Estabilización), the largest conservative party, had grown by absorbing nativist sentiment during the depression and the anti-Zong campaign.

### Fascist Takeover of the PpE
- The NPdA (Nuevo Partido de Aislamiento, fascist) merged with the PpE.
- The PpE leader lost the leadership mandate to an upstart fascist who took control of the merged party.
- The takeover was supported by Sartan fascist groups and the Círculo Volisano, who provided funding and organisational support.
- The merged party retained the PpE name but was effectively fascist-controlled.
- The fascist leader positioned himself as the defender of Guajicutea against the Zong threat, using the anti-Zong press campaign to build support.

## El Atentado de Waribá (1930)

- A bombing at Waribá port, staged by fascist agents within the PpE, blamed on Zong sympathizers.
- Several people killed. The fascist press immediately blamed Zong and connected the bombing to alleged Zong military plans.
- The bombing was plotted by the fascist leadership of the PpE as a pretext for seizing emergency powers.
- The fascist party leader used the crisis to force through a sweeping emergency package:
  1. **Emergency powers** — the president (still the popularish conservative) was pressured into signing the emergency decree.
  2. **Jefe de la Asamblea** — a new executive position that gave the fascist party leader control over the legislative agenda and, effectively, over the government.
  3. **Suspension of elections** — citing national security concerns and fears of a Zong invasion.
  4. **Abolition of the 1910 anti-Volisanian laws** — the bans on Volisanian ownership of private industries and Volisanian language in commercial goods were repealed, opening Guajicutea to Volisanian economic and military presence.
  5. **Volisanian military base** — Volisania was permitted to establish a formal military base on Guajicutean soil, expanding beyond the port lease into a permanent installation.
  6. **Ban on political parties** — all opposition parties (PdI, PpLT, PdT, PSG) were banned. Only the PpE (fascist-controlled) remained as the sole legal party.
- The president remained nominally in power but was subverted by the Jefe de la Asamblea.

### Jefe de la Asamblea
- A new role created in 1930 that subverted the president's authority.
- Gave the fascist party leader executive power over the assembly and the government apparatus.
- The president became a figurehead while the Jefe de la Asamblea ran the government.
- The role was justified as a temporary emergency measure but became permanent.

### Protests and Civil Disobedience (1930–1935)

- The emergency measures triggered immediate local protests and civil disobedience.
- Riots broke out in Waribá, Tabakurí, and other cities.
- The government clamped down hard — police and military force used to suppress protests.
- **Liberty movements** emerged as organised resistance to the fascist takeover:
  - Some funded by Zong — Zong saw an opportunity to undermine Volisania's staging ground and supported resistance elements.
  - Zong funding was covert, channelled through intermediaries and Zong-connected trade networks.
  - The fascist press used Zong-funded resistance as "evidence" of the Zong threat, reinforcing the emergency powers.
- The protests and resistance were met with increasing repression through 1930–1935.
- The ban on political parties drove opposition underground — former party members, labour organisers, and civil society activists operated covertly.

## Military Buildup and Industrialisation (1920s–1935)

### Port Lease and Military Base
- Volisania leased parts of Waribá port for naval ships, paying large sums of money.
- The ships were stationed for a planned invasion of Zong Balboa.
- After 1930, this expanded into a formal Volisanian military base on Guajicutean soil.
- The military buildup was a major source of revenue for Guajicutea but also a source of foreign military presence.
- Anti-Zong sentiment was fueled by the military buildup and the press campaign.
- The buildup connected to the broader fascist geopolitical strategy: Volisania planning to retake Balboa from Zong influence.

### Rapid Industrialisation (1930s–1935)
- A second period of rapid industrialisation in the 1930s, driven by military demand from Volisania and Sarta.
- Guajicutea was seen as a safe harbour for industrial manufacturing to support the Volisanian and Sartan militaries. Unlike Balboa and Tapuya, which were clearly under Volisanian influence and vulnerable to attack or sabotage, Guajicutea was technically an independent state — not Volisania, not Sarta — making it a safer location for war production.
- The ironworks in Waribá — shut down since 1905 — was reopened and modernised to support military production.
- Factories and manufacturing capacity expanded in Waribá and surrounding areas.
- Production focused on military supplies: processed materials, manufactured components, ship repair and maintenance at the Astillero Real, and other industrial outputs feeding the Volisanian and Sartan war machines.
- Unemployment, still high from the depression era, dropped to near-zero as war production absorbed the available workforce.
- Sentiments were generally positive — employment restored, wages up, industry thriving — even as fascists ran the government. The economic recovery created a degree of public acquiescence to the fascist regime.
- However, the industrialisation deepened Guajicutea's dependence on Volisania and Sarta — the factories existed to serve their militaries, not the Guajicutean domestic economy.

### Racial Policy and the 1931 Memorandum
- The National Volisan Party (NVP) and the Sartan Fascist Party (SFP) were advocating for racial purity and ethnic cleansing in their home territories (Volisania and Sarta respectively).
- However, the fascist leadership in Guajicutea wrote in a leaked memorandum to Volisania in 1931 advising against implementing racial laws in Guajicutea.
- The argument: Guajicutea's population was racially mixed (Guaji, Bayani, Kaftian, Sartan). Imposing racial laws would trigger unnecessary internal conflict and destabilise the war production effort.
- The memorandum was leaked, causing embarrassment but also revealing the pragmatic streak of Guajicutea's fascists — they prioritised industrial output over ideological purity.
- Despite this, the regime still engaged in repression:
  - Kidnappings of journalists who criticised the regime.
  - Arrests of politicians who protested the emergency measures.
  - Suppression of political discontent, though not along racial lines.
- The regime's violence was political rather than racial — targeting dissent, not ethnicity.

### Repression of Arts and Homosexuality
- The fascist regime repressed arts and homosexuality, framing both as degenerate behavior and institutions.
- **Arts**: Bikuismo and independent artistic expression were suppressed. The regime promoted state-approved art — militaristic, traditionalist, propagandistic. The cultural revival that had flourished since the 1890s catacombs rediscovery was stifled. Artists who continued producing independent work risked arrest. The regime's cultural policy framed pre-colonial Guaji art as backward and modern fascist aesthetics as progressive.
- **Homosexuality**: In Guaji history, homosexuality was negatively looked upon but never codified as illegal. It was seen as part of warrior naval culture — a tolerated practice among the warrior/maritime class, with a long tradition rooted in the pre-colonial naval culture where men spent long periods at sea together. Now the fascist regime clamped down, especially in the military. Homosexuality was criminalised, and enforcement was particularly strict within the armed forces. The regime's repression targeted a practice that had been quietly accepted for centuries, framing it as degenerate and un-Guajicutean despite its deep roots in Guaji naval tradition.

### Guaji Military Buildup
- The fascist regime built up Guajicutea's own military forces, which had been minimal since the republican era.
- The military was primarily used for domestic enforcement — suppressing protests, policing dissent, enforcing the ban on political parties.
- However, a specialised marine force was developed for amphibious assault — a specialty practiced by commandos of Guaji's small military.
- **Marine force name**: **Kari Buka** — "sea openers," from *kari* (sea) + *buka* (to burst/open). Evokes the pre-colonial tradition of "opening" the sea through navigation and raiding. Connects to Captain Kari Buka from the Kari Yabí voyage (1502–1514 CE). The name links the amphibious commandos to Guaji's deepest naval heritage.
- The amphibious assault specialty drew on Guaji's long naval tradition — pre-colonial canoe warfare, the Astillero Real shipbuilding heritage, and the island's maritime culture. The commandos trained for coastal raids, beach landings, and small-unit amphibious operations.
- The marine force was small but highly trained — a commando specialty rather than a mass army. Guajicutea's military was never large enough for conventional warfare, but the marine commandos represented a niche capability.
- Training was likely conducted with Volisanian and Sartan military advisors, integrating fascist military doctrine with Guaji naval traditions.

### Public Mood and the Coming War
- News and consensus in Guajicutea by the mid-1930s was that a war was coming.
- Most people were afraid — the military buildup, the industrial war production, and the geopolitical tensions between Volisania/Sarta and Zong made conflict seem inevitable.
- The general public sentiment was a mix of economic satisfaction (employment, recovery) and fear of the approaching war.
- The fascist government used the fear of war to justify continued emergency powers and the suspension of elections.

## End of Era: Great Adonian War (1935)

- In 1935, the Balboa campaign of the Great Adonian War opened.
- Volisania and Balboan forces invaded Zong Balboa.
- Guajicutea, with its Waribá port leased to Volisania, was effectively a staging ground for the invasion.
- The era of fascist subversion ended with Guajicutea drawn into the Great Adonian War.

## Key Figures

- **Adrián Figueroa** — Liberal president (term 22, 1917–1921, PpLT). Continues moderate coalition trajectory. Economic recovery period.
- **Marcos Acosta** — Liberal president (term 23, 1921–1925, PpLT). Visited Zong Balboa to repair relations. Escándalo de Balboa discredited his government.
- **Ernesto Guerrero** — Conservative president (term 24–25, 1925–1933, PpE). Popularish but subverted by Jefe de la Asamblea after 1930. Became figurehead.
- **Damián Cordero** — Fascist upstart who took over the PpE, became Jefe de la Asamblea in 1930. The real power in Guajicutea from 1930 onward.
- **Simón Soler** — PpE party leader who lost mandate to Cordero. Traditional conservative, not a fascist.
