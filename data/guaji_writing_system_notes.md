# Guaji Writing System Notes (Bikumaku)

Internal working notes on the glyph-based writing system used by the Guaji.

## Overview

- **Name**: Bikumaku (bika "carved/walk" + maku "mark") — "carved marks"
- **Type**: Glyph system forming logograms — glyphs represent words/concepts, not syllables or letters
- **Two phases**:
  - **Early Bikumaku** (pre-500 CE): predominantly **pictographic** — glyphs resemble what they mean
  - **Late Bikumaku** (post-500 CE, mature by 900–1000s): predominantly **ideographic** with **phonetic strokes** — abstracted glyphs + stroke combinations that encode vocalizations
- **Origin**: Glyph concept imported from the **Tapuyan mainland** (Tapuyic script) through Caracurí trade contact, then gradually adapted by the Guaji over generations
- **Earliest surviving document**: Clay tablets dated to **315 CE** — but this is the oldest surviving record, not the introduction date. The script likely arrived earlier and transformed gradually through sustained Tapuyan contact. Earlier tablets may have been lost to the tropical environment.
- **Usage**: Specialist priestly writing — not mass literacy. Used by **bohíques** and **priestesses** to record calendars, genealogies, storm ledgers, treaties, and ritual knowledge
- **Medium**: Primarily carved in **stone** and impressed in **clay** (both survive in tropical environments; wood, bark-cloth, and fiber do not)
- **Relationship to oral tradition**: Bikumaku supplements but does not replace oral tradition. Most Guaji are orally literate — they know their genealogies, songs, and histories through speech. Bikumaku is the archive of the priestly class.

## Historical development

### Pre-contact with script (date uncertain)
- The Guaji arrived on Guajicutea with no writing system — only oral tradition, pictographic art (cave paintings, pottery decoration), and tally marks
- Pictographic art was already sophisticated: red and black pigments on cave walls, geometric designs on pottery, triangular cosmological symbols (sea/mountain/sky)
- This pictographic tradition provided a **cognitive bridge** — the Guaji were already thinking in images and symbols, which made adoption of a formal glyph system easier
- **[Oral tradition]** claims a very ancient arrival (500–250 BCE) and 750–1000 years of two-tribe separation. These figures are apocryphal — oral histories routinely exaggerate time depth. The actual separation period was probably shorter.

### Introduction from Tapuya (date uncertain, before 315 CE)
- The **Caracurí** (eastern sea people) controlled trade routes to the mainland near Tapuya
- Through sustained trade contact, Caracurí merchants and voyagers encountered Tapuyan societies who used a glyph-based script — carved on monuments, clay tablets, and cave walls
- The script was brought back to Guajicutea not as a complete system but as a **concept**: the idea that marks on stone could carry precise meaning across time and distance
- The script arrived and transformed **gradually** through ongoing Tapuyan contact — there was no single "introduction event." Caracurí traders and bohíques adapted Tapuyic forms over generations.
- The **315 CE tablets** are the **oldest surviving documents**, not the introduction date. Earlier tablets likely existed but were lost to the tropical environment (clay can survive, but unfired or poorly fired tablets degrade; wood and fiber are gone entirely).
- The adaptation was probably driven by **bohíques** who saw the potential for recording calendars, genealogies, and storm histories more permanently than oral memory allowed

## Phase 1: Early Bikumaku (~315–500 CE) — Pictographic

### Characteristics
- Glyphs are **pictographic** — they directly resemble what they represent
- A mountain glyph looks like a peak. A wave glyph looks like a wave. A canoe glyph looks like a canoe.
- The connection between glyph and meaning is **visual and intuitive** — you can guess many glyphs without knowing the system
- Tapuyic source forms are still recognizable in many glyphs, though already being simplified and redrawn
- **No phonetic components** — glyphs represent concepts, not sounds. You recognize the word from the picture, not from how it's pronounced.
- Limited vocabulary: probably 80–150 glyphs covering core cosmology, calendar notation, storm types, and basic social categories

### Glyph forms (early pictographic style)
- **Curvilinear** — Guaji aesthetic favors curves over angles (contrast with angular Tapuyic originals)
- **Organic** — forms drawn from nature: peaks, waves, crescents, hands, birds, roots
- **Simple** — few internal details; a glyph is usually one continuous carved line or shape
- **Red pigment** sometimes rubbed into carved grooves for emphasis

### What early Bikumaku recorded
- **Calendars**: year-marks (bikú), moon-marks (warí), seasonal notations
- **Storm ledgers**: wave-marks, spirals, crossed figures — the pictographic storm notation that already existed in cave art was formalized into a consistent glyph set
- **Genealogies**: simple human figures with distinguishing marks (feathered = cacique, disk = bohíque)
- **Ritual instructions**: sequences of cosmological glyphs (three-power circle, mountain, sea, sky) carved on tablets

### Limitations of pictographic system
- **Cannot represent abstract concepts easily** — "harmony," "authority," "season," "cycle" are hard to draw as pictures
- **Cannot represent proper names** — you can't draw someone's name unless it happens to mean a visible object
- **Cannot represent grammatical markers** — tense, plurality, possession have no obvious picture
- **Cannot record spoken language** — only frozen concepts, not sentences
- This is why the system evolved

## Phase 2: Late Bikumaku (post-500 CE, mature by 900–1000s) — Ideographic + Phonetic

### The shift
After unification (516 CE), the pictographic system came under pressure:
- The unified Guaji needed to record more complex things: treaties between former rivals, detailed genealogies across merged lineages, trade agreements with specific terms, calendar refinements
- The catacomb calendar corridor required a more precise notation system — year-counts, event markers, and cross-references between the calendar and the storm ledger
- Bohíques needed to record **spoken formulas** — ritual incantations, genealogical recitations, formal speeches — not just frozen concepts

### Characteristics of Late Bikumaku
- Glyphs become **ideographic** — they represent ideas rather than directly resembling objects
- Pictographic roots are still visible but **abstracted** — a mountain glyph is no longer a literal peak but a stylized triangular form with internal modifications
- **Phonetic strokes** are added: a system of **modifier strokes** carved beside or within a glyph that encode **vocalizations** — sound values matching Guaji's phonology
- This makes Late Bikumaku a **mixed system**: part logographic (glyph = word), part phonetic (strokes = sound)
- A trained reader can both recognize the concept from the glyph AND pronounce the word from the strokes

### The stroke system
The phonetic stroke system is the key innovation of Late Bikumaku. Strokes are added to a base glyph to indicate how it should be read aloud:

#### Vowel strokes
Guaji has three vowels (/a i u/). Each is represented by a specific stroke type:
- **Horizontal stroke** = /a/ — the most common vowel, the "default" stroke
- **Vertical stroke** = /i/ — a rising line, possibly from the visual association with "small/up"
- **Curved/diagonal stroke** = /u/ — a descending or rounding stroke

These strokes can be added to a glyph to indicate the vowel of the word it represents, or to spell out syllables in proper names and grammatical markers.

#### Consonant modifiers
Strokes and marks that modify the base glyph to indicate which consonant sound begins the word:
- **Dot/circle** = /b/ — possibly from the "roundness" of the b sound
- **Cross/intersection** = /k/ — a sharp crossing mark
- **Wave/curve** = /w/ — derived from the sea glyph (kawa/water association)
- **Angle/wedge** = /t/ — a sharp angular mark
- **Double line** = /m/ — two parallel strokes
- **Slash** = /r/ — a single diagonal
- **Hook** = /y/ — a curved terminal stroke

#### How strokes combine with glyphs
A base glyph gives the **concept** (mountain, sea, storm, year). Strokes added to the glyph give the **vocalization** — how to pronounce the word for that concept. This allows:
- **Disambiguation**: when two concepts share a similar glyph, strokes tell the reader which word is meant
- **Proper names**: a person's name can be "spelled out" in strokes even if no glyph exists for their name
- **Grammatical markers**: tense, plurality, and possession can be indicated by stroke combinations rather than new glyphs
- **Foreign words**: Tapuyan trade terms or other Adonian loanwords can be written phonetically using strokes alone

### Glyph evolution (early → late)
| Concept | Early pictographic form | Late ideographic form | Phonetic strokes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mountain (yuku) | Literal peak shape, triangular | Abstract triangle with internal lines | /y/ hook + /u/ curve + /k/ cross + /u/ curve |
| Sea (kawa) | Horizontal wavy line | Stylized wave with parallel lines | /k/ cross + /a/ horizontal + /w/ wave + /a/ horizontal |
| Storm (bari) | Cloud + rain lines | Abstract cloud form with stroke marks | /b/ dot + /a/ horizontal + /r/ slash + /i/ vertical |
| Hurricane (wirā) | Large spiral | Multi-turn spiral with stroke modifiers | /w/ wave + /i/ vertical + /r/ slash + /a/ horizontal |
| Year (waku) | Simple circle (bikú) | Circle with internal radial marks | /w/ wave + /a/ horizontal + /k/ cross + /u/ curve |
| Moon (warí) | Crescent shape | Crescent with stroke marks | /w/ wave + /a/ horizontal + /r/ slash + /i/ vertical |
| Union/treaty | Clasped hands | Abstract interlocked forms | Strokes spell "waku" (cycle/return) — union as return to balance |
| Gold (guani) | Shining disk | Disk with internal crosshatch | /g/ (from /k/ cross + voicing mark) + /a/ + /n/ (double line) + /i/ |
| Death (yú) | Fallen figure | Horizontal figure with slash | /y/ hook + /u/ curve |

### Vocabulary expansion
- Early Bikumaku: ~80–150 glyphs (core pictographs)
- Late Bikumaku: ~250–400 glyphs (ideographic base) + the phonetic stroke system allows infinite combinations
- New glyphs created for: abstract concepts (harmony, authority, cycle, season), social roles (Cacique Má, Bohíque Má, nitaino, naboría), trade goods, specific ritual formulas
- Some glyphs become **purely phonetic** — used only for their sound value, not their original meaning (rebus principle)

### What Late Bikumaku recorded
- **Catacomb calendar corridor**: full year-count with event markers, cross-referenced to storm ledger — carved in mature ideographic script
- **Storm ledger**: pictographic storm marks retained (spirals, waves, crossed figures) but with phonetic strokes added for dates, names, and details
- **Genealogical tablets**: full lineages with names spelled phonetically, marriage records, inheritance lines
- **Treaties**: formal agreements between communities, with terms written out in phonetic strokes
- **Ritual texts**: incantations and formulas that could be "read aloud" from the strokes
- **Trade records**: goods, quantities, partner names, dates — the most secular use of the script

## Script characteristics summary

### Glyph structure
- **Base glyph** = concept (logographic/ideographic)
- **Modifier strokes** = vocalization (phonetic)
- **Compound glyphs** = two or more base glyphs combined (e.g., mountain + flow = yukubara)
- **Phonetic-only glyphs** = glyphs used purely for sound value, stripped of original meaning

### Reading direction
- Early Bikumaku: probably **right-to-left** along horizontal surfaces (following the direction of carving from the right hand)
- Late Bikumaku: may have standardized to a consistent direction — possibly **top-to-bottom in columns** for wall inscriptions (catacomb corridor) and **right-to-left in rows** for tablets
- Open question — needs more development

### Materials and survival
- **Stone carving** (tabamaku): the primary medium for permanent records. Calendar corridor, storm ledger, and monumental inscriptions. Survives well in tropical climate.
- **Clay tablets**: portable records — genealogies, treaties, ritual instructions. Fired or sun-dried. Clay survives in tropical environments; wood and fiber do not.
- **Clay seals/stamps**: carved glyphs pressed into wet clay — possibly used for marking trade goods or authenticating agreements. A few survive from trade contexts.
- **No bark-cloth or wood writing**: these materials rot within years in the tropics. If the Guaji ever wrote on perishable materials, none survive.

## Social and political implications

### Priestly authority
- Control of Bikumaku = control of the **archive**
- The Bohíque Má (high priestess) and her trained bohíques are the only literate class
- Written records give the priestess class:
  - **Calendar authority**: only they can read and maintain the carved calendar
  - **Genealogical authority**: written lineages determine inheritance and legitimacy
  - **Historical authority**: the storm ledger and event records are the official memory of the people
  - **Treaty authority**: written agreements between communities carry more weight than verbal ones
- This reinforces the **dual-authority structure**: Cacique Má (king, secular) + Bohíque Má (priestess, sacred). The king commands people; the priestess commands memory.

### The phonetic revolution and priestly power
- The addition of phonetic strokes in Late Bikumaku **increased** priestly power:
  - With phonetic strokes, bohíques could record **exact spoken formulas** — ritual incantations, formal speeches, genealogical recitations
  - This meant the written record was no longer just a memory aid — it was a **fixed text** that could be checked against oral tradition
  - The priestess class controlled not just what was remembered, but **how it was remembered** — the exact words mattered
  - Phonetic strokes also made the script **harder to learn** — more strokes, more combinations, more training required. This deepened the literacy monopoly.

### Limitations
- Bikumaku is **not used for everyday communication** — no letters, no market signs, no casual inscriptions
- Commoners (naborías) do not learn to read or write — they rely on oral tradition and the spoken word of bohíques
- The script is **sacred knowledge**, taught through initiation, not shared freely
- This means Bikumaku is a **closed system** — it records what the priestly class decides is worth recording

## Relationship to Tapuyic script

### What was borrowed
- The **concept** of glyph-based writing (marks = words)
- The **practice** of carving records on stone and clay
- Some **base glyph forms** (especially early transitional tablets, 315 CE)
- The **format** of sequential carved records (calendar walls, monumental inscriptions)

### What was changed
- **Glyph inventory**: completely reworked to match Guaji cosmology (three powers, yukubara, makú, etc.)
- **Visual style**: Guaji glyphs are more curvilinear and organic; Tapuyic originals tend to be more angular and geometric
- **Phonetic system**: the stroke system was a **Guaji innovation** — the Tapuyic script may have had some phonetic elements, but the systematic vowel/consonant stroke system is distinctively Guaji, mapped to the 3-vowel system (/a i u/)
- **Usage context**: Tapuyan script may have been broader (administrative, public); Guaji usage is narrower (sacred, archival)
- **Format**: Tapuyan tablets → Guaji catacomb walls + portable tablets

### What was NOT borrowed
- No Tapuyan **grammar or syntax** — Bikumaku records Guaji language
- No Tapuyan **mythology or cosmology** — the three-power system is entirely Guaji
- No Tapuyan **social structure** — the priestly monopoly on literacy is a Guaji innovation

## Archaeological evidence

### The Tabayabí Stone (~600 CE) — retrospective chronicle

**[Documented evidence]** — a carved stone tablet discovered during a modern construction project on Guajicutea. Named **Tabayabí** from *taba* (stone) + *yabí* (hidden/old) — "stone of the hidden time."

**Physical description**:
- Carved stone tablet, larger than the typical portable clay tablets
- Contains a retrospective chronicle attempting to record events from **before** the calendar started at 516 CE
- Uses **pre-calendar notation**: events are marked as "X years before Year 1" (i.e., X years before 516 CE)
- The chronicle reaches back to approximately **215–220 CE** (~296–301 years before 516 CE)
- At this earliest point, the stone shows **pictograms of boats** — interpreted by most scholars as imagery of arrival

**Disputed interpretation**:
- **Arrival interpretation**: the boat pictograms represent the first Guaji canoes reaching Guajicutea, placing first settlement at ~215–220 CE
- **Birth interpretation**: some scholars argue the boat pictograms could represent the **birth or arrival of a specific person** — perhaps a founding cacique or important ancestor — rather than the literal arrival of the people. The boats could be a personal name or title glyph, not a historical event marker.
- The stone is a **retrospective document** made ~400 years after the events it describes. Its dating is based on the writing system's relative dating and the chronicle's own internal notation, both of which could be inaccurate for events so far in the past.
- The 215–220 CE date is the **chronicle's earliest entry**, not necessarily the actual first arrival. The chroniclers may have been working backward from oral tradition and reached the limit of what they could reconstruct.

**Significance**:
- The Tabayabí stone is the only known document that attempts to systematically record pre-calendar events
- It demonstrates that by ~600 CE, the Guaji had developed a concept of **pre-calendar time** — a way of thinking about and notating events that happened before their calendar system began
- It is both the most important and the most contested piece of evidence for early Guaji history

### Pre-calendar notation and the concept of negation

The Tabayabí stone reveals that Bikumaku had developed a **negative notation system** — a way to mark years as occurring *before* a reference point (Year 1 / 516 CE) rather than after it.

**How it works**:
- Standard calendar notation counts forward from Year 1: Year 1, Year 2, Year 3, etc.
- Pre-calendar notation counts **backward** from Year 1: "X years before" — using a specific modifier mark on the year-glyph (bikú) to indicate backward counting
- The modifier is likely a **reversed or inverted stroke** — the same stroke used for forward counting, but drawn in the opposite direction or with a negation mark

**Origin in trade**:
- The concept of negative/backward counting was probably **adapted from trade practices**, not invented for the calendar
- In trade, Guaji merchants needed to track **debts, deficits, and amounts owed** — situations where someone had less than zero of something
- The notation for "owed" or "deficit" in trade records likely used the same inverted/negation marks
- Bohíques then applied this trade-derived concept to timekeeping: just as you could owe someone goods, time could "owe" the calendar — years that existed before the counting started
- This makes the negative notation one of the few Bikumaku innovations with a **secular origin** (trade) that was then adopted into sacred use (calendar/chronicle)

**[Oral tradition] vs [Documented evidence]**:
- The 750–1000 year two-tribe separation is **[oral tradition]** — apocryphal, likely exaggerated
- The 215–220 CE arrival date is **[documented evidence]** from the Tabayabí stone — but the stone is a retrospective chronicle, not a contemporaneous record, and its interpretation is disputed
- The existence of pre-calendar notation itself is **[documented evidence]** — the stone physically demonstrates it
- The trade origin of negation notation is **[interpretation]** — plausible but not directly proven

### Earliest tablets (~315 CE) — Early Bikumaku
- Found in contexts associated with **Caracurí** coastal settlements (east/north coast)
- Show **transitional script** — Tapuyic glyph forms with early Guaji adaptations
- **Pictographic only** — no phonetic strokes present
- Likely grave goods or ritual deposits, not casual discards
- Clay, fired — small rectangular tablets with carved glyphs on one or both faces
- Content: probably genealogical or ritual — too fragmentary to fully read

### Catacomb inscriptions (post-516 CE) — transition to Late Bikumaku
- The **calendar corridor** — the largest surviving Bikumaku inscription
- The **storm ledger** — pictographic storm marks with early phonetic stroke additions
- Both are wall-carved, not tablet-based
- Show the transition: early sections are purely pictographic, later sections (post-900s) show increasing use of phonetic strokes
- The corridor itself is a **palimpsest of the script's evolution** — you can read the history of Bikumaku by walking along it

### Later tablets (900–1000s CE) — mature Late Bikumaku
- Portable clay tablets from central valley and coastal contexts
- Fully developed ideographic + phonetic stroke system
- More standardized glyph forms
- Content: genealogies, treaties, ritual calendars, trade agreements, ritual incantations
- Some show **red pigment** in carved grooves — decorative or ritual emphasis
- Phonetically spelled proper names appear — confirming the stroke system was fully functional

## Open questions

- How many base glyphs vs. phonetic stroke combinations are in the full Late Bikumaku inventory?
- Are there regional variants? (Yukurí vs. Caracurí glyph forms? Did the Yukurí adopt the script differently?)
- Did the phonetic stroke system develop independently on Guajicutea, or was it inspired by a later wave of Tapuyan contact?
- Is there a cursive or simplified form for faster carving, or is every glyph formally rendered?
- Were there ever bilingual texts (Tapuyic + Bikumaku)?
- How were bohíques trained in the script? (Apprenticeship? Specific initiation rituals? Was the stroke system taught separately from the base glyphs?)
- Were there ever attempts to spread literacy beyond the priestly class?
- Can we reconstruct specific glyph drawings for key concepts?
- Did the stroke system ever develop into a full syllabary, or did it remain a phonetic supplement to logograms?
