# Guajicutea Era Notes: 1853–1875 (Industrialization, Immigration, and Upheaval)

## Overview

Period from outbreak of War of Liberation (1853) through the end of the PdI-PpLT coalition era (1875). Covers: War of Liberation (1853–1856) and its aftermath, Balboa independence (1854), realignment of Illpynian powers against Fosian threats (Zong, Mitoyagi, Nakamizu), renewed Volisanian influence through investment and trade, guano boom (1853+) on Warikú, industrialization (first ironworks 1864), Kaftian refugee influx from Lacashe, ethnic violence (race riot 1866, immigration ban), rise of workers movements, public universal primary education (1870), three-party politics (PdI, PpLT, PpE), and the PdI-PpLT secret coalition (1872).

## International Events

### War of Liberation (1853–1856)
- Coalition: Burawa, Volisania, Balisca, Asikyira vs Lacashe (last slave-holding state).
- Treaty of Guledga (1856): abolished slavery, partitioned Lacashe. Eastern coastal strip = Free Republic of Libertalia (state for freed slaves/freedmen, led by Marcus Galvan / Freedman Congress). Western/interior = Republic of Lacashe.
- Guajicutea's role: small troop assignment + granted docking rights to Burawa and Volisania. Waribá and Barikú as staging points.
- Post-war: Bloody Decade in Lacashe (1856–1866) — lynchings, population transfers, communal violence. Porto Vren Concession: Balisca controlled cotton exports until 1873.

### Balboa Independence (1854)
- Balboa (Volisanian viceroyalty) gained independence from Volisania in 1854.
- Context: Volisania restructuring colonial holdings. Balboa had banned slavery in 1848.

### Three Threats of Fosia
- Term coined by Volisanian politician Ernesto Bonastre in 1878.
- Refers to Mitoyagi, Zong, and Nakamizu — core industrial and military powers of Fosia (far eastern continent).
- Period 1850–1895: strongest industrial, economic, and military growth in Adonia.
- Illpynian powers (Volisania, Burawa, Mercia, etc.) alarmed by Fosian power accumulation and influence over Lurandia.
- Threat of Fosian invasion of Illpynian allied nations in the New World.
- The threat materialized in the Second War of the Isthmus (Zong + Louyang + Sinchew expanded into modern-day Balboa, occupied major ports by 1899) — this is in the next era.
- This realignment brought Illpynian powers closer together and affected their colonial policies. Guajicutea, as a small nation in the Capuyaquiran Sea, was affected by this geopolitical shift.

### Renewed Volisanian Influence
- After the War of Liberation (in which Volisania was a coalition partner), Volisania's influence in Guajicutea increased again.
- Channel: investment and trade. Volisanian commercial interests invested in Guajicutea's economy, particularly in port infrastructure and manufacturing.
- This marked a shift from the Sartan-dominated commercial influence of the previous decades. Volisania became a significant economic partner alongside Sarta.

### Libertad-Lacashe War (1875–1880)
- Lacashe invaded Libertalia in 1875. War lasted 5 years, fought primarily on Libertalian territory.
- Asikyira deployed substantial army, pushed Lacashian forces back.
- War ended 1880 with peace treaty reimposing partition and guaranteeing Libertalia's independence.
- 1889: Libertalia united with Asikyira, ending its independent existence.
- This war begins at the very end of this era and extends into the next.

## Domestic — Economy

### Industrialization
- Farm mechanization increased in the 1850s–1860s, reducing agricultural labor needs and prompting rural-to-urban migration.
- In 1864, the Sartan government established the first ironworks in Waribá and transferred it to the Guajicutean government to operate — the beginning of heavy industry in Guajicutea. Iron ore was not mined locally; the ironworks depended on imports from Tapuya, making it expensive and logistically vulnerable. This import dependence would eventually lead to the ironworks' closure and sale in the following era.
- Waribá became the industrial center of the island, combining port activity, the Astillero Real, and the new ironworks.
- Rapid growth of cities, especially Waribá, which became the most populous city.

### Guano Boom (1853+)
- Guano (bird droppings) became a valuable commodity as fertilizer in the mid-19th century.
- Warikú (island ID 1, "little moon" from warí + -kú) — small island near Guajicutea, joined the Republic in 1756.
- Warikú had large guano deposits. The guano boom from 1853 made the island economically valuable.
- A sub-group of Guaji lives on Warikú. They had maintained autonomy since joining the Republic in 1756.
- The guano boom created tension between the autonomous Warikú inhabitants and the central government / commercial interests over guano extraction rights and revenues.
- The inhabitants' autonomy was longstanding (since 1756) but the economic value of the island created pressure for greater central control.

## Domestic — Social

### Kaftian Refugee Influx
- After the War of Liberation (1853–1856) and the Treaty of Guledga, freed slaves in Lacashe/Libertalia fled the post-war violence (Bloody Decade 1856–1866 in Lacashe).
- Kaftian refugees crossed the sea to Guajicutea, arriving primarily at Waribá and Barikú.
- Significant influx: the Kaftian population nearly doubled from ~17k (1853) to ~36k (1875), with ~15k arriving as refugees (1856–1866).
- Both PdI and PpLT were sympathetic to the refugees. PdI saw them as labor for industrialization. PpLT saw them as fellow workers.
- Pushback from native Guaji population — competition for jobs, housing, resources.

### Workers Movements
- Rise of workers movements in the 1860s, driven by industrialization and urbanization.
- First workers riot in 1861 — against Kaftian workers being used for low wages by employers.
- The riot reflected tension between native workers and immigrant workers, with employers exploiting cheaper Kaftian labor.
- This riot directly led to the formation of the PpE.

### Ethnic Violence
- Anti-Kaftian sentiment: homes and businesses of Kaftian immigrants frequently attacked.
- State attempted to maintain peace with military occupations to protect Kaftian immigrants.
- Military occupations often met with fights and violence — soldiers vs. locals.
- Race riot in 1866 — major eruption of ethnic violence.
- Ban on immigration from all of Abyala established after the 1866 race riot.
- The ban reduced new immigration but did not address the existing Kaftian population, who continued to face discrimination.

### Public Universal Primary Education
- Established in 1870 under President José Moku.
- The state had been building parallel secular institutions since the 1824 church-state separation. Civil schools existed alongside parish schools.
- Universal primary education was the next step: state-funded, available to all children.
- Drivers: industrialization (ironworks 1864) needed literate workers; workers movements demanded education; political competition (whoever delivered education won working-class votes).
- PdI president signed it with PpLT support in the Asamblea. Undermined PpE's appeal — "you don't need the nativist party to get schools."

## Domestic — Politics

### Three-Party System

| Party | Platform | Economy | Immigration | Base | Formed |
|-------|----------|---------|-------------|------|--------|
| PdI (Partido del Internacionalismo) | International integration, free trade | Liberalization | Pro (needs labor) | Commercial, urban | 1832 |
| PpLT (Partido por la Tierra) | Rural/worker advocacy | Worker protections | Pro (inclusive labor) | Rural, workers | 1851 |
| PpE (Partido de Estabilización) | Nativist labor | Protectionist | Anti | Nativist workers | 1861 |

- PpE was protoleftist (pro-worker) but also protectionist and anti-immigration. A nativist labor party.
- PpE overlapped with PpLT on labor issues but diverged on immigration. PpE overlapped with PdA on protectionism but was more economically left-wing.
- The PpE's appeal grew with anti-Kaftian sentiment post-1861, intensified after the 1866 race riot.

### PdI-PpLT Secret Coalition (1872)
- In the 1872 Asamblea election, the PpE won a plurality of seats but not an outright majority.
- PdI and PpLT formed a backroom deal — a secret coalition government combining their seats to form a majority.
- The coalition was a marriage of convenience: PdI feared PpE's protectionism would kill trade. PpLT feared PpE's anti-immigration stance would divide the working class.
- The coalition allowed PdI to continue trade liberalization while giving PpLT influence on labor and education policy.
- Public education (1870) was an early demonstration of PdI-PpLT cooperation, predating the formal coalition.

## Demographics

### Population Trajectory

| Year | Population | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| 1853 | ~195,000 | Start of era. Pre-War of Liberation. |
| 1856 | ~195,000 | War of Liberation. Minimal Guaji casualties (small troop assignment). |
| 1860 | ~210,000 | Growth resumes. Guano boom driving economic expansion. |
| 1865 | ~225,000 | Continued growth + Kaftian immigration. |
| 1870 | ~240,000 | Industrialization + immigration. |
| 1875 | ~250,000 | End of era. |

- Growth from ~195k to ~250k (~55k increase over 22 years, ~1.2%/year). Faster than previous era due to immigration + economic boom.
- Kaftian immigration: ~15k refugees arrived 1856–1866. Ban in 1866 stopped new immigration.

- Guaji share declined from 57% to 55% — not because Guaji population shrank (it grew) but because Kaftian growth outpaced it.
- Volisanian influence brought some Volisanian commercial community to Waribá.

| 1853 | ~64,000 | 33% | ~131,000 | 67% |

**Urban centres by 1875:**
- Waribá: ~45,000 (ironworks, port, Astillero Real, manufacturing — most populous, fastest-growing)
- Tabakurí: ~30,000 (capital, government, cathedral — slower growth)
- Barikú: ~18,000 (secondary port, military)
- Small towns: ~14,000 (some growing into small urban centres with industrialization)

- Urbanisation accelerated: 33% → 43% in 22 years. Previous era was 30% → 33% in 29 years. Industrialization and guano boom drove faster urbanisation.
- Waribá pulled far ahead of Tabakurí — industrial center vs. administrative center.

## Culture

### Religion
- Church-state separation (1824) continued. Amicable relationship maintained.
- State's parallel secular institutions (civil schools, registries) expanded with public universal primary education (1870).
- Church retained spiritual authority. Parish schools coexisted with state schools.
- Guaji Catholicism (syncretic) continued in rural areas. Urban populations more secularized.

### Arts
- Proto-Symbolism movement (established 1840s) continued to develop. Sebastián Biku traveled to Sarta (1845), spreading the movement to Illpynia.
- The industrialization and urbanization of this period provided new subjects: factories, ironworks, port activity, urban crowding.
- The social upheaval (ethnic violence, workers riots) entered artistic representation — both realist and proto-Symbolist treatment.
- Warikú guano boom may have inspired landscape/seascape art reflecting the island's transformation.

### Sentiment
- Post-War of Liberation: sense of participation in a historic regional event. Cathartic (fighting Lacashe, the 1824–1825 occupier).
- Guano boom: economic optimism but also anxiety about rapid change.
- Kaftian immigration: fear, resentment, ethnic tension. The race riot (1866) was a national shock.
- Industrialization: excitement about progress but dislocation for rural communities losing population to cities.
- Political contention: the three-party system and the secret coalition created cynicism about democratic politics.
- Workers movements: rising class consciousness, but divided by immigration politics (PpE vs. PpLT).
