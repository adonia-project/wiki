# Early Guaji settlement of Guajicutea

The first inhabitants of Guajicutea were the **Guaji** people, an Arawak-speaking group related to the Taino cultures of the Antilles. Their own name for themselves probably meant something like "the people" or "the island people," though the exact word has been lost. Outsiders later linked them to the island itself, and "Guaji" became the name used in later chronicles. They were farmers, navigators, and spirit-workers who saw the volcano at the island's heart as a living ancestor.

## Origins and arrival

The Guaji reached Guajicutea by sea from the **western islands** and the mainland near modern-day **Tapuya**. They were skilled navigators in dugout canoes, moving from island to island along the continental shelf and using currents, seabirds, and the position of stars to guide them.

**Oral tradition** holds that the first arrivals came in great antiquity — some stories place the landing as early as 500–250 BCE, and oral histories claim the Yukurí and Caracurí tribes existed separately for 750–1000 years before unification. These figures are almost certainly exaggerated; oral histories routinely stretch time depth across the generations.

**Documented evidence** for the earliest period is limited to a single contested artifact: the **Tabayabí Stone** (*taba* = stone, *yabí* = hidden/old — "stone of the hidden time"). Discovered during a modern construction project, the Tabayabí Stone is a carved tablet dated to approximately **600 CE** on the basis of its script form and relative dating. It is a **retrospective chronicle** — a document written after the calendar began at 516 CE that attempts to reconstruct events from before Year 1. The chronicle uses **pre-calendar notation**, marking events as "X years before Year 1," which means the script had already developed a concept of negation or backward counting — probably adapted from trade practices, where merchants tracked debts and deficits.

The stone's earliest entry falls at approximately **215–220 CE** (~296–301 years before 516 CE). At this point, the stone shows **pictograms of boats**. Most scholars interpret these as imagery of arrival — the first canoes reaching Guajicutea. Others dispute this reading, arguing that the boat pictograms could represent the **birth or arrival of a specific person** — perhaps a founding cacique or important ancestor — rather than the literal arrival of the people. The stone is a retrospective document made roughly 400 years after the events it describes; its dating may be approximate, and its interpretation remains open.

Whether or not the Tabayabí Stone records the actual first landing, the arrival probably happened in several waves rather than a single event:

- **First scouts** — fishermen and traders who camped briefly on beaches.
- **Family groups** — settlers with root crops, seeds, and domesticated dogs.
- **Later arrivals** — people fleeing conflict or seeking new land on overpopulated islands to the west.

They brought with them a root-crop complex suited to tropical islands: **cassava (yuca), sweet potato, arrowroot, and yams**. They also carried **calabash** gourds and knowledge of how to process poisonous cassava into flour. Over generations, they added native Adonian plants to their fields, most importantly **makú**, a starchy tuber that thrived in the wet volcanic lowlands.

Cotton cloth and cotton padding were not grown locally in any quantity — the hot climate meant little demand for woven clothing — so raw cotton and finished textiles came in through trade with the western islands and the mainland near Tapuya.

## First settlements

The earliest Guaji settlements were on the **north coast** of Guajicutea, where the land is lower, the rainfall is moderate, and the reefs and inshore waters provided fish, shellfish, and turtles. These first villages were small:

- **Coastal shell middens** — heaps of conch, whelk, and fish bones.
- **Simple bohíos** — round houses with thatched roofs and wooden posts.
- **Canoe landings** — sandy coves where dugouts were pulled ashore.

Over time, families moved inland along the river valleys, where the volcanic soils were richest and fresh water was reliable. Because Guajicutea is a large island with extensive fertile lowlands, the Guaji population probably grew well beyond what a small coral islet could support. The river valleys became the most densely settled parts of the island — strings of villages separated by forest, each with its own cacique but linked by kinship, trade, and shared rituals.

The north remained the more populated coast, but the south held something the Guaji considered sacred: the mountain itself.

## Early culture

### Subsistence
The Guaji were farmers, fishers, and gatherers.

#### Farming
They farmed **conuco mounds** — raised planting beds — on the fertile volcanic slopes. The island's wet tropical climate and rich Andisol soils favored some crops over others:

**Wet windward east and river valleys:**
- **Makú** — a native Adonian tuber with a hard, woody rind and pale, starchy flesh that turns sweet when roasted. It grows in dense clusters along riverbanks and in the wet, mineral-rich volcanic soils of the east. The Guaji considered it a gift from the mountain because it flourished where other crops rotted.
- **Yams** — a major staple, especially in shaded valley bottoms and wet lowlands.
- **Leren** — a starchy root crop that thrives in damp, shaded conditions.
- **Cacao** — cultivated in the warm, shaded foothills, probably as a luxury and ritual crop rather than a daily staple.

**Drier leeward west and higher slopes:**
- **Cassava (yuca)** — drought-tolerant, reliable on the drier western side; the main source of flour.
- **Sweet potato** — grew on slopes and in poorer soils.
- **Beans and squash** — grown together with cassava.
- **Arrowroot** — processed into a fine starch.

**Highlands:**
- **Sweet potato and beans** — cooler-tolerant varieties at mid elevations.
- **Avocado, pineapple, and chilies** — grown in sheltered mountain pockets.

Cassava, yams, and makú were the three staples: cassava supplied flour and stored well, yams provided bulk calories in the wetter lowlands, and makú was the native crop that bound the Guaji to the volcanic soil.

#### Fishing, hunting, and gathering
- **Fishing**: hooks, nets, and harpoons; flyingfish were especially important.
- **Hunting**: small land animals, birds, and iguanas; no large game existed on the island.
- **Gathering**: wild fruits, palm hearts, medicinal plants, and shellfish.

### Society
- **Caciques** — hereditary chiefs who controlled villages and trade.
- **Nitainos** — nobles and warriors.
- **Naborías** — commoners, farmers, and fishers.
- **Bohíques** — shamans who healed, interpreted dreams, and spoke with spirits.

### Religion
The Guaji saw the world as a conversation between three great powers: **the sea**, **the mountain**, and **the sky**. These were not distant gods in human form but living forces that shaped every part of life. Religion was the practice of keeping them in harmony.

#### The three powers
- **The sea** — the oldest power and, in Guaji belief, the **creator**. The first ancestors had come from the sea, born from foam and driftwood, and the sea remained the source of all life. It gave fish, salt, trade routes, and the rain that fell on the island. The sea was patient but could also be merciless in storm and swell.
- **The mountain / volcano** — the power of fire, stone, and fertility. It pushed the island up from the sea and fed the soil with ash. It was the source of wealth and destruction alike. The Guaji understood it as a being that slept, woke, and judged.
- **The sky** — the realm of birds, wind, stars, and the spirits of the recently dead. Birds carried messages between worlds, and the northeast trades were the breath of the sky moving across the island.

#### Harmony and imbalance
Health, harvest, and peace depended on balance among the three. When the Guaji honored the sea with offerings, farmed the mountain's soil with care, and respected the sky by not killing certain birds, the island prospered. When one power was offended — especially through greed or pride — the others turned against the people. The destruction of Guajicutea Vieja was remembered as the moment the mountain punished the westerners for hoarding trade goods and forgetting their obligations to the sea and the sky.

#### Death and the caves
The Guaji did not believe the dead simply vanished. They passed downward, toward the underworld that lay beneath the mountain. **Caves were the doorways to death** — not because they were pleasant, but because they were dangerous. The cold, the sulfur fumes, the darkness, and the risk of never returning made caves the natural threshold between life and afterlife. Only those prepared for the journey — caciques, bohíques, and honored warriors — were placed deep inside them.

#### The yukubara
The Guaji word for a volcanic mudflow was **yukubara** — literally "mountain-flow" or "the mountain's muddy hand." A yukubara was not just a natural disaster; it was an act of the mountain, a physical expression of divine judgment. When a yukubara came without warning, it was understood as the mountain reaching down to reshape the world.

The word itself may be an old **Caracurí** term. The eastern sea people, who lived farther from the volcano and closer to the trade routes, seem to have preserved the earliest written-like records of the event. The Yukurí of the west probably had their own name for the flow, but after the great hurricane and the merger, the Caracurí version of history became the dominant one.

#### Ritual practice
They carved **zemís** — wooden or stone spirit idols — and used **tobacco** in rituals to carry prayers to the spirits. Tobacco was smoked in cigars or small pipes and grew well in the volcanic soil.

For deeper communion with the underworld, the **bohíques** entered volcanic caves and fissures near the summit, where sulfur deposits and volcanic gases produced altered states. Inhaling the fumes deliberately — or simply staying in the low-oxygen chambers long enough — brought visions that were interpreted as messages from ancestors and the mountain itself. This practice was dangerous and restricted to trained shamans. Volcanic eruptions were therefore seen not only as destruction but as moments when the underworld spoke most clearly.

### Art and memory
The Guaji did not develop writing independently, but they adopted and adapted a **glyph system** from the **Tapuyan mainland** — a Tapuyic script — by at least **315 CE**. This adapted system is called **Bikumaku** ("carved marks"). It was not a mass literacy system. It was a **specialist priestly writing**, used by bohíques and priestesses to record calendars, genealogies, storm ledgers, treaties, and ritual knowledge. Most people remained orally literate — they knew their histories, songs, and lineages through speech.

Bikumaku was carved primarily in **stone** and impressed in **clay** — the two materials that survive in a tropical environment. Wood, bark-cloth, and fiber rot within years, but carved stone walls and fired clay tablets endure.

The script evolved in two phases:

**Early Bikumaku** (~315–500 CE) was predominantly **pictographic**. Glyphs directly resembled what they meant — a mountain glyph looked like a peak, a wave glyph looked like a wave, a canoe glyph looked like a canoe. The connection between glyph and meaning was visual and intuitive. The earliest surviving tablets, found in Caracurí coastal contexts and dating to around 315 CE, show clearly Tapuyic glyph forms already being simplified and redrawn to match Guaji aesthetic conventions. The vocabulary was limited — perhaps 80 to 150 glyphs covering core cosmology, calendar notation, storm types, and basic social categories. It could record frozen concepts but not spoken language: no proper names, no grammatical markers, no abstract ideas.

**Late Bikumaku** (post-500 CE, mature by the 900s–1000s) became predominantly **ideographic** with a **phonetic stroke system**. Glyphs were abstracted — the mountain was no longer a literal peak but a stylized triangular form with internal modifications. The key innovation was a system of **modifier strokes** carved beside or within a glyph that encoded **vocalizations** — sound values matching Guaji's three-vowel system (/a i u/). Horizontal strokes marked /a/, vertical strokes /i/, curved or diagonal strokes /u/. Consonant modifiers were added: dots for /b/, crosses for /k/, waves for /w/, angles for /t/, double lines for /m/, slashes for /r/, hooks for /y/. A trained reader could both recognize the concept from the base glyph and pronounce the word from the strokes. This allowed proper names, grammatical markers, and even foreign words to be written phonetically — something the early pictographic system could not do.

The **Catacumbas del Abismo** calendar corridor and storm ledger — the largest surviving Bikumaku inscriptions — preserve the full history of this evolution. The earliest sections are purely pictographic. Later sections, carved after the 900s, show increasing use of phonetic strokes for dates, names, and cross-references. Walking the corridor is like walking through the script's own development.

Control of Bikumaku gave the priestly class institutional power: only they could read and maintain the carved records, determine genealogical legitimacy, and authenticate treaties. The phonetic stroke system deepened this monopoly — it made the script harder to learn, requiring more training, and allowed bohíques to fix exact spoken formulas in writing, controlling not just what was remembered but how it was remembered. This reinforced the dual-authority structure of later Guaji society, where the **Cacique Má** (elected king) held secular power and the **Bohíque Má** (high priestess) held sacred and archival authority.

Alongside the written script, older traditions of visual memory continued:

- **Pictographic art**: red and black pigments on cave walls, pottery, and wooden panels. Common motifs included the mountain, the sea, the sky, canoes, birds, and human figures with raised arms. Triangular designs — one point up for the sky, one down for the underworld, and a horizontal base for the sea — represented the balance of powers.
- **Pottery idols**: small clay figurines and incised jars representing the sea, the mountain, and the sky. These were placed in houses, caves, and burial grounds.
- **Body paint and weaving**: geometric designs carried on skin, cotton cloth, and baskets.
- **Oral tradition**: long genealogies, origin stories, and cautionary tales about greed, division, and the anger of the gods.

The art served both sacred and practical purposes. A pictograph on a cave wall might record a lineage, a trade agreement, a warning, or a prayer. Bikumaku glyphs grew out of this older pictographic tradition — the Guaji were already thinking in images and symbols, which made the adoption of a formal logographic system possible.

### Material life
- **Canoes**: large dugouts capable of island-to-island travel.
- **Pottery**: simple bowls and griddles for cooking cassava bread; also small figurines of mountain and sea spirits.
- **Stone tools**: axes and adzes made from local volcanic rock.
- **Basketry and weaving**: hammocks, nets, and carrying baskets.
- **Body ornamentation**: shell necklaces, feather headdresses, red ochre paint, and small pendants of native gold and copper.
- **Clothing**: in the hot lowlands, most people wore little — bark-cloth loincloths, woven grass skirts, and shell ornaments. Imported cotton was reserved for sashes, elite garments, and padding. Those who traveled into the cold high mountains wore layered cotton garments, feather cloaks, and quilted bark-cloth wraps.
- **Pictographs**: painted on cave walls, pottery, and wooden panels using red ochre, charcoal, and white clay.

## The southern site: Guajicutea Vieja

The best-preserved early Guaji settlement is **Guajicutea Vieja** — "Old Guajicutea" — located in the southern foothills of the central volcano, near the place the user has marked as Archeological Site 1.

### Location and setting
The site sits at roughly **328 meters elevation** on a gentle slope of about **4 degrees**, on **Pleistocene volcaniclastic sediments and lahar deposits**. It is about **2.6 kilometers from the nearest river** and roughly **46 kilometers from the coast** as the crow flies, placing it well inland in the drier, leeward western foothills.

This location was chosen for several reasons:

- **Water**: close enough to a permanent river for drinking and irrigation.
- **Soil**: the ground is young volcanic ash soil (*Andisol*), extremely fertile for root crops.
- **Elevation**: high enough to escape coastal storms and mosquitoes, low enough to stay warm.
- **Volcano**: the mountain was the center of the Guaji spiritual world.
- **Defense**: inland position made raids from the sea more difficult.

### Preservation by mudflows
The site was eventually buried by a **yukubara** — a volcanic mudflow — during a period when the volcano was more active than it is today. In the open tropics, wooden houses, thatch, fiber, and bone normally rot within decades. The mudflow did not preserve the village intact, but it did seal the ground surface quickly enough to protect the **shadows** of the settlement before the climate could erase them.

What survives at Guajicutea Vieja:

- **Stone and earth footings** — low walls and packed-earth platforms that held the wooden bohíos above the ground.
- **Postholes** — dark circular stains where wooden posts rotted away, leaving only their shape in the soil.
- **Clay-daub fragments** — baked and unbaked clay from wattle-and-daub walls, especially common in the south where volcaniclastic soils provide good clay.
- **Hearths and ovens** — fire-cracked rock, charcoal, and baked clay linings.
- **Fired pottery** — broken bowls, storage jars, and cassava griddles left in place.
- **Stone tools** — flakes, adzes, grinding stones, and hammerstones.
- **Carbonized wood** — charred roof beams or posts preserved only where a house burned before burial.
- **Shell and bone** — scattered remains, better preserved in the alkaline shell middens than in acidic forest soils.

Wooden posts, baskets, hammocks, and most organic material decayed long ago. The yukubara preserved the **architecture of absence**: the holes, stains, and fragments that show where the houses stood.

Today, Guajicutea Vieja appears as a low mound on the slope, with scattered volcanic boulders and a gray-brown matrix of volcanic mud. Excavations have revealed rows of postholes, hearths, cassava-processing griddles, and broken pottery frozen in place beneath the flow.

### What the site was
Guajicutea Vieja was probably not a single village but a **ceremonial and residential center** that grew over centuries:

- **Residential area**: clusters of bohíos around a central plaza.
- **Ceremonial plaza**: a flat, packed-earth space where ball games and tobacco rituals took place; some high-status rituals may have involved sulfur brought down from the summit caves.
- **Storage pits**: underground pits for cassava and seed crops.
- **Workshop area**: flaked volcanic glass and stone adze production.
- **Burial ground**: crouched burials with shell ornaments and pottery offerings.

The presence of imported shell beads and non-local stone suggests that Guajicutea Vieja was also a **trade and ritual hub**, connecting the southern mountain communities with the northern coastal villages.

### Cultural significance
For the Guaji, Guajicutea Vieja was more than a village. It was the place where the **mountain met the people**. The volcano was both giver and destroyer:

- It provided fertile soil and fresh water.
- It erupted without warning, sending yukubara down the slopes.
- It was the doorway to the underworld and the home of ancestral spirits.

The buried houses of Guajicutea Vieja are therefore understood not just as ruins, but as **spirit houses** — places where the living once slept and where the dead may still dwell beneath the mud. Some later Guaji groups refused to farm directly over the mound, leaving it as a sacred ground.

### The old story
Guaji oral tradition remembers Guajicutea Vieja as the end of one age and the beginning of another. The story says that after the first ancestors arrived by boat, the people multiplied along the rivers and the island grew rich. Two ways of life emerged: the **Yukurí** of the western foothills and high valleys, who looked to the mountain, and the **Caracurí** of the eastern and northern coasts, who looked to the sea.

For a time the Yukurí were dominant. Their chiefs controlled the volcanic trade routes and demanded tribute from river villages. They grew proud and forgot the old obligations to the sea and the sky. The gods sent no immediate punishment. The volcano had been quiet for generations, and the Yukurí chiefs believed it had fallen asleep.

Then, without the warning smoke or tremors that usually came before an eruption, a yukubara poured down the slope and buried Guajicutea Vieja in a single day.

The story is not a literal record, but it captures something real: the mudflow was **unexpected**. There may have been no major eruption beforehand — just a collapse of a water-saturated slope after heavy rain, or a small vent clearing that no one recognized as dangerous. To the survivors, the event looked like divine judgment.

The yukubara did more than destroy a village. It broke the power of the Yukurí. Their ceremonial center was gone, their fields buried, and their claim to speak for the mountain was shaken. The **Caracurí**, who had suffered less, absorbed the surviving Yukurí clans through marriage, alliance, and perhaps coercion. Out of this came the unified Guaji people — neither purely eastern nor western, but a new society that claimed descent from both the sea and the mountain.

Later Guaji used the story to teach that greed offends all three powers: the sea, which provides trade and rain; the mountain, which provides soil; and the sky, which carries the breath of life. The Yukurí had taken from the sea and the mountain without giving back, and the mountain answered. Guajicutea Vieja remained the place where the old division was punished and preserved.

## Age and dating

There are no written records, and radiocarbon dates from Guajicutea Vieja are scarce because the tropical climate destroyed most organic material before the mudflow sealed it. The current estimate is approximate:

- **First Guaji arrival on the island**: roughly **500–250 BCE**, based on the earliest pottery styles and the depth of coastal shell middens.
- **Founding of Guajicutea Vieja**: possibly several centuries after arrival, perhaps around **200 BCE–200 CE**.
- **Burial by yukubara**: **516 CE**, the year the Guaji calendar begins.

This makes the preserved settlement about **1,500 years old** and the first human presence on Guajicutea roughly **2,000–2,500 years old**. The dates are provisional; much of the early history is inferred from oral tradition, volcanic stratigraphy, and a small number of excavated artifacts.

## Highland cave sites

High in the central volcano, above the cloud forest, are three lava-tube caves that preserve a different side of Guaji life. Unlike the lowland villages, which were farms and fishing camps, these caves were connected to **trade, ritual, and war**. They were used for centuries and then deliberately abandoned before colonization.

The caves formed when fluid lava drained away from ancient tube systems, leaving long tunnels and chambers in the andesite and dacite. The cold, dry air inside preserves wood, fiber, and organic materials far better than the humid lowlands.

### Cueva Guanani — the treasure cave
**Site 2** lies at roughly **1,500 meters elevation** on a steep slope of andesitic stratovolcano rock, only about **486 meters from a river**. Its name comes from *guanín*, the Taino alloy of gold and copper, because this cave appears to have been a **trade cache**.

Inside, archaeologists have found:
- **Guanín ornaments** — small pendants and disks, some unfinished.
- **Shell beads** — not local, probably traded from islands to the west.
- **Polished stone axes** — made from greenstone or jadeite, sourced far from Guajicutea.
- **Pottery sherds** — styles linking the cave to both the northern coast and the southern mainland.
- **Wooden boxes and baskets** — preserved by the cold cave air, some still containing residue from trade goods.

Cueva Guanani was probably a **waystation** for traders crossing the island. Goods moved between the north coast, the southern volcano settlements, and the western islands, and this cave was a secure place to store valuables overnight or between trading seasons.

### Cueva Zemí — the spirit cave
**Site 3** is the highest of the three, at roughly **1,638 meters elevation** in the **dacite-rhyolite caldera complex**, about **916 meters from a river**. It is the most sacred of the caves.

The chamber contains:
- **Carved zemís** — wooden and stone spirit idols placed in niches, many showing the mountain and the sea combined in a single figure.
- **Tobacco paraphernalia** — small bowls, pipes, and cigar holders used in rituals.
- **Sulfur residue and breathing tubes** — evidence that bohíques used the cave's volcanic gases for vision rituals.
- **Offerings** — shell, carved bone, and rare feathers.
- **Burials** — crouched skeletons of important individuals, wrapped in fiber mats.
- **Paintings** — red and black pictographs on the cave walls: spirals for the mountain, wave lines for the sea, canoe shapes, and stick-figure ancestors with raised arms. These are among the clearest surviving Guaji images because the cave protected them from rain and sun.

Cueva Zemí was likely used by bohíques for ceremonies connected to the volcano. Its high position put it close to the sky world, and the cold breath of the cave was interpreted as the voice of ancestors.

### Cueva Cacibay — the refuge cave
**Site 4** sits at roughly **1,406 meters elevation** on very steep andesitic terrain, about **1,382 meters from the nearest river**. Its name means roughly "chief's cave" or "refuge cave."

This cave shows signs of **defensive use**:
- A narrow entrance that could be blocked with stones.
- Ash layers from cooking fires, suggesting long occupation.
- Broken weapons and tools — stone blades and wooden spear foreshafts.
- Storage niches for dried food and water gourds.
- Human remains with trauma injuries, suggesting a last stand or siege.

Oral tradition says Cueva Cacibay was the final hideout of a southern Guaji faction during a war with northern rivals or with raiders from another island. The people who used it were eventually forced to abandon the highlands, leaving their goods behind.

### Trade and conflict
These caves show that the early Guaji were not isolated. They were part of a wider island trade network that moved:
- **Guanín and greenstone** from the south and west.
- **Shell and feathers** from the coasts.
- **Pottery styles** between islands.
- **Obsidian and volcanic glass** from Guajicutea's own slopes.

But they were also vulnerable. The same canoes that brought trade brought raiders. The highland caves were both **treasury and fortress** — places to hide wealth and people when coastal villages were attacked. By the time European contact began, the caves had been abandoned for generations, their entrances overgrown and their contents forgotten except in fragmentary oral memory.

## Catacumbas del Abismo

Deep in the central volcano, far above the valleys and below the summit, lies a network of lava tubes and chambers known in later records as **Catacumbas del Abismo** — the Catacombs of the Abyss. The Guaji had their own name for it, now lost, which meant something like **"the door that opens downward."**

The catacombs were not created all at once. The natural lava tubes existed long before people arrived, but the carved chambers, the burial niches, and the calendar corridor were made after **516 CE** — after the yukubara destroyed Guajicutea Vieja and the Caracurí absorbed the Yukurí to form the unified Guaji. The catacombs were, in a sense, the first monument of the new society.

### Location and setting
The catacombs sit at roughly **1,287 meters elevation** on a very steep slope of andesitic stratovolcano rock, in the zone of **rock outcrops and sparse vegetation**. They are about **60 kilometers from the coast** and roughly **558 meters from the nearest river**, deep inside the mountain and far from any village. Their location, on the **northern and eastern side** of the central highlands, places them much farther from the southern volcano than the three southern caves and closer to the old Caracurí territory than to the Yukurí lands of the west.

This northern placement was probably deliberate. The yukubara that destroyed Guajicutea Vieja had flowed down the southern and western slopes. By placing their sacred dead in the northern highlands, the Caracurí — and later the unified Guaji — kept the catacombs away from the paths the mountain had already used to punish the old western center.

The entrance is a narrow fissure in a cliff face, easy to miss unless one knows where to look. Inside, the lava tubes descend in stages, growing colder and darker as they go. Some chambers are tall enough to stand in; others narrow to crawlways. In places, the tubes open into larger rooms where the roof has partially collapsed, letting in thin beams of light and patches of moss.

### Ownership and use
The catacombs were almost certainly a **Caracurí** institution that became shared after the yukubara. Several clues point eastward:

- The entrance faces the rising sun and the coastal trade routes the Caracurí dominated.
- The earliest calendar carvings use sea-bird motifs more often than mountain symbols.
- The storm ledger pays close attention to coastal destruction, suggesting record-keepers who cared deeply about the sea.
- The calendar begins in 516 CE, the year the Caracurí rose to dominance by absorbing the Yukurí.

If this is true, then the first Yukurí dead placed in the catacombs were probably buried there as a political and spiritual act — a way for the Caracurí to claim the ancestors of the old western chiefs and bind the two peoples together. After the merger, the catacombs became a shared sacred place, but the language of the carvings — the names, the month order, the emphasis on storms and sea — preserves an eastern point of view.

### The realm of the dead
For the Guaji, depth was sacred direction. The sky world was up, the living world was the surface, and the underworld was down. The deeper a cave went, the closer it came to the ancestors and the gods of volcanic fire.

Catacumbas del Abismo was therefore the island's most sacred religious space. Burial was only one of its functions. The deeper chambers were places of **prayer, vision, and communion with the underworld**, where bohíques descended to speak with ancestors and the mountain itself. Not everyone was interred there — only:

- **Caciques and their families** — rulers who needed to enter the underworld with status.
- **Bohíques** — shamans who had spent their lives walking between worlds.
- **Warriors killed in battle** — whose violent deaths required special rites.
- **Offerings** — pottery, shell, feathers, prayer objects, and tools placed with the dead or left as tribute.

The bodies were not simply laid in the cave. They were placed in **niches carved into the tube walls**, in crouched positions facing the mountain's heart. Some chambers hold dozens of skeletons, stacked in rows that follow the curve of the lava tube.

### What survives
Because the catacombs are cold, dry, and sealed from the surface, preservation is better than in the lowlands:

- **Human remains** — articulated skeletons, some with healed injuries and dental wear.
- **Funerary pottery** — bowls, jars, and figurines placed as offerings.
- **Shell ornaments and beads** — necklaces, bracelets, and ear spools; also whole shells left as prayer offerings.
- **Prayer objects** — small carved stones, feather bundles, and clay tablets with pictographic petitions.
- **Wooden implements** — staffs, bowls, and ritual objects rarely preserved elsewhere.
- **Carving tools** — basalt hammerstones, obsidian chisels, and abrasive grinding stones left behind by the workers who shaped the niches.
- **Pictographs deep in the tubes** — red and black figures of ancestors, mountains, and sea spirits, painted where torchlight barely reached.

The deepest paintings are the simplest: single vertical lines, hand stencils, and spiral shapes that may represent the descent into the underworld itself.

### Carving the chambers
The niches and wider chambers were not natural. They were enlarged by Guaji workers using a combination of techniques:

- **Fire-setting**: building fires against the lava-rock walls, then quenching the hot stone with water or urine to make it crack along natural fractures.
- **Stone hammers and chisels**: basalt and andesite hammerstones for breaking rock; obsidian flakes for trimming and shaping.
- **Abrasive grinding**: sandstone or pumice rubbers for smoothing niche floors and walls.
- **Wooden levers and wedges**: for prying loose cracked blocks.
- **Baskets and rope**: for hauling debris out of the tubes.

The work was slow and dangerous. Many of the tools were left behind as offerings once a chamber was finished — a way of dedicating the labor to the mountain. Broken hammerstones and worn obsidian blades still lie scattered on the floors of the deepest rooms.

### Mountain clothing
Because the catacombs and summit caves were cold, those who entered them needed insulation. The Guaji had no native animal that produced warm pelts, so they relied on other materials:

- **Feather cloaks** — quilted layers of down and body feathers from highland birds. These were the warmest garments and were almost exclusively worn by bohíques and caciques. Because each cloak required the feathers of many birds — collected over seasons or taken through ritual hunting — they were too precious for ordinary use.
- **Layered cotton** — multiple thin cotton garments worn together, sometimes stuffed with loose cotton batting for insulation. Cotton was imported, so this was the practical clothing of guides, warriors, and workers who had to enter the high mountains.
- **Bark-cloth wraps** — beaten inner bark from certain trees, layered and rubbed with oil to resist wind.
- **Trade goods** — wool or camelid fiber from the mainland near Tapuya, brought in by canoe. Such imported cloth was rare and valuable, often used as ceremonial capes rather than everyday wear.

### Feather garments as regalia
Feather clothing was never just practical. It was **religious attire**. Birds moved between earth and sky, so a cloak of feathers allowed the wearer to move between worlds as well.

- **Bohíques** wore feather cloaks and headdresses when descending into the catacombs or entering sulfur caves, signaling that they traveled with the permission of the sky spirits.
- **Caciques** received feather mantles as part of their installation, marking their role as intermediaries between the people and the gods.
- **Warriors** sometimes wore smaller feather sashes or armbands earned through deeds, but full cloaks remained the privilege of priests and chiefs.
- **Specific birds** carried meaning:
  - **Highland owls** — soft, dense down provided the warmest insulation; their nocturnal nature linked them to the underworld and ancestor spirits.
  - **Quetzal relatives and trogons** — brilliant green, red, and blue feathers were used on the outer surface of cloaks as symbols of the sky world and sacred authority.
  - **Large parrots and aracaris** — strong wing and tail feathers added structure and bright color; their feathers represented the living world and speech.
  - **Coastal boobies and pelicans** — abundant body down, used as practical filler in less prestigious cloaks and sleeping wraps.

The work of making a single cloak fell to specialists who collected feathers during molting seasons, preserved them with smoke, and stitched them onto an imported cotton backing in overlapping rows. A great cloak might take years to complete and would be inherited, repaired, and eventually buried with its owner.

A bohíque preparing to descend into the catacombs would put on a feather cloak over a padded cotton tunic, wrap bark cloth around the legs, and pull up a feathered headdress that doubled as a hood. Dressed this way, he was no longer merely a man entering a cave; he was a being who could walk in the world of the dead.

### Danger and taboo
The catacombs were not entered lightly. The air in the lower tubes is thin and sometimes carries sulfur fumes from small vents. Several chambers have low ceilings where carbon dioxide can pool. The Guaji understood these dangers and treated them as proof that the place belonged to the dead and the gods, not the living.

Only certain bohíques were permitted to descend to the deepest chambers. They went with torches and breathing tubes, performed rites for the dead, prayed at the painted shrines, and returned as quickly as possible. Commoners were forbidden from entering; even caciques were buried by specialists who left immediately afterward.

### The storm ledger
Deep in the catacombs, in the same long corridor where the calendar was carved, the Guaji kept a **storm ledger** — a pictographic record of bad storms and hurricanes. It was not writing in the literal sense, but a carefully maintained notation system that allowed the bohíques to track the island's weather across generations.

The ledger used a horizontal baseline representing the passing years. Above and below this line were symbols:

- **Small wave marks** — ordinary storms, heavy rains, or dangerous swells.
- **Large spiral marks** — major hurricanes.
- **Crossed human figures** — storms that caused deaths.
- **Broken canoe symbols** — storms that destroyed fleets or coastal villages.
- **Mountain symbols beside storm marks** — storms that triggered landslides or yukubara.
- **Red handprints** — the bohíque who made the entry, placed beside each event.

The bohíques added new marks after each bad season, comparing recent storms to older ones to look for patterns. They believed that clusters of severe storms warned of imbalance among the three powers: too much rain meant the sea was restless, landslides meant the mountain was unsettled, and unusual winds meant the sky was disturbed.

The ledger made the catacombs more than a burial place. They were also an **archive** — the only place where the Guaji tried to hold time still. The great hurricane of **Year 627, Month of the Great Waters** is recorded there as one of the largest spirals, with many crossed figures and broken canoes. Some later scholars think the ledger helped the merged Guaji predict the timing of the hurricane season and decide when to move villages inland.

### Abandonment
Like the highland caves, the catacombs were abandoned before colonization. The reasons were probably a mix of war, population disruption, and changing beliefs. Once the living stopped returning, the entrances were forgotten and overgrown. But the dead remained, undisturbed in the dark and cold, until much later explorers forced their way inside.

## The Guaji calendar

The Guaji tracked time by the moon. Their calendar was lunar, with each month beginning at the first visible crescent and lasting until the next new moon. A year had **thirteen months**, matching the cycles of the moon more closely than the shorter twelve-month count used by some mainland peoples. The bohíques occasionally dropped the thirteenth month — which they called the "hidden month" or "the moon that waits" — to keep the calendar aligned with the seasons and the return of the flying fish schools.

The moon was also the key to reading the sea. The Guaji knew that the highest and lowest tides came with the new and full moons, and that the strongest currents ran between those points. Fishermen planned their outings around the lunar cycle, and canoe traders timed departures to leave with the outgoing tide and arrive with the incoming one. A cacique planning a long voyage would consult the bohíque not only for omens but for the moon.

The calendar counted years from **516 CE**, the year of the yukubara that buried Guajicutea Vieja. That year became **Year 1**. All later events were dated from it:

- The yukubara = Year 1
- The abandonment of the highland caves = roughly Years 400–500
- The great hurricane = Year 627
- Later European contact = well over a thousand years after Year 1

The months were not named after gods or heroes. They were named after what happened on the island:

1. **Month of the First Rains** — the wet season begins.
2. **Month of Sprouting** — root crops push through the soil.
3. **Month of the Flying Fish** — schools move close to shore.
4. **Month of the Dry Wind** — the trades blow strongest.
5. **Month of the First Storms** — the hurricane season opens.
6. **Month of the Great Waters** — the peak of the storm season.
7. **Month of the Still Sea** — the ocean calms between storm cycles.
8. **Month of the Second Storms** — late-season hurricanes.
9. **Month of Harvest** — yams and cassava are dug.
10. **Month of Smoke** — fields are burned and cleared.
11. **Month of Planting** — new crops go into the conuco mounds.
12. **Month of the Long Nights** — the shortest days of the year.

The bohíques kept the calendar in the catacombs, carved into a long corridor wall. The corridor begins with the largest and most ornate carving: a circle divided by three lines representing the sea, the mountain, and the sky, with the number **516** rendered in a stylized tally. This marks the year the yukubara buried Guajicutea Vieja, the Yukurí were absorbed into the Caracurí, and the unified Guaji began to count time.

From that starting point, the wall stretches for many meters, each year marked by a small carved circle. Every fifth year is joined by a horizontal line; every tenth year by a larger ring. Major events interrupt the pattern:

- **Wars** — crossed spears or broken canoes carved beside the year.
- **Deaths of great caciques** — small human figures with feathered headdresses.
- **Eruptions and yukubara** — spirals and flame-like shapes.
- **The great hurricane of Year 627** — the largest spiral on the wall, surrounded by broken canoes and fallen figures.
- **The merger of the Yukurí and Caracurí** — two hands clasped, carved after Year 627.

The storm ledger and the calendar are really the same document: a visual history of the island written in stone and pigment. By reading the corridor, a trained bohíque could see not only how many years had passed since the yukubara, but how the Guaji had lived, suffered, and survived through them.

## The great hurricane

By Year 627 of the Guaji calendar — roughly **1143 CE** — the unified Guaji had been living on the island for over six hundred years. They had expanded their villages along rivers and coasts, rebuilt trade networks, and established the catacombs as a shared sacred center. Then, in the **Month of the Great Waters**, a **great hurricane** struck.

Oral histories describe it as unlike any storm before or since: weeks of wind and rain, rivers bursting their banks, coastal villages swept away, and landslides pouring down the saturated western slopes. The eastern coasts suffered the worst damage because the Caracurí had settled them most densely. The western valleys were flooded and buried in mud. The storm killed hundreds and scattered communities across the island.

### Aftermath
The hurricane was remembered as a judgment from the three powers acting together: the sea raised the waves, the mountain sent the landslides, and the sky drove the wind. It tested the unified Guaji in the same way the yukubara had tested the old Yukurí. But this time, the people did not fracture. Survivors gathered in the central river valleys, shared food and land, and rebuilt together.

The event was added to the catacomb calendar as one of the largest spirals ever carved. Afterward, the Guaji became more cautious about coastal settlement and more deliberate about maintaining grain reserves. The highland cave sites — Cueva Guanani, Cueva Zemí, and Cueva Cacibay — were still visited after the hurricane, but less often. The catacombs, closer to the center of the island, became the main sacred place of the unified people.

## Gold and copper ornamentation

The mineral-rich volcanic heart of Guajicutea provided small amounts of **native copper** and **gold**, mostly from stream gravels and weathered veins in the highlands. The Guaji did not mine metal in any organized way, but they collected nuggets and flakes from riverbeds and exposed rock.

They worked these metals cold, without smelting:

- **Native copper** — hammered into small disks, beads, and ear spools. Copper was associated with the mountain and with endurance.
- **Native gold** — rarer, saved for the most important ornaments: pendants for caciques, small plaques sewn onto feather cloaks, and offerings placed in caves.
- **Gold-copper mixtures** — accidental or deliberate combinations found in some river gravels, prized for their reddish color.

Metal ornaments were signs of high status and spiritual power. A cacique might wear a gold pendant shaped like the sun or the mountain, while a bohíque could carry a copper disk engraved with pictographic prayers. Most of these objects were later looted from the caves, but a few survived buried with their owners in the catacombs.

## The mountains and the mine

### Sacred and profane geography

Guajicutea has two mountain systems, and the Guaji treated them very differently.

The **southern volcano** — **Monte Yukumá**, rising to roughly **2,955 meters** — was the sacred mountain. This was the living power that had created the island, buried Guajicutea Vieja, and given the soil its fertility. The Guaji did not mine gold or copper from Yukumá's slopes. To cut into the sacred mountain for metal would have been an act of greed — the same kind of greed that, in oral tradition, had provoked the yukubara. Gold found in Yukumá's stream gravels was collected casually, as a gift already given, but no one dug into the mountain itself.

The **northern mountain range** was older, lower, and geologically distinct — an andesitic stratovolcano complex that had been eroded into a gentler, more dissected landscape with multiple peaks and broad river valleys. Because it was not the active sacred volcano, the Guaji treated it as **profane ground**: a place where the mountain's mineral wealth could be taken without offending the living power. This is where they mined gold.

### The peaks

The northern range has four major peaks:

- **Cerro Guanarí** (~1,460m) — the highest of the northern peaks, and the one closest to the gold mine. Its name may derive from *guani* (gold) and *arí* (place of), meaning roughly "golden hill." The rivers flowing from Guanarí carried gold-bearing sediments down to the mine site.
- **Cerro Caracurí** (~1,220m) — the easternmost peak, associated with the Caracurí sea people who controlled the northern coasts and trade routes. Lower and more weathered than the others, with subtropical forest covering its lower slopes.
- **Cerro Trogoní** (~1,380m) — a central peak named for the trogons and quetzal-relatives that lived in its montane rainforest. The bright feathers of these birds were collected here for feather cloaks.
- **Cerro Vienturí** (~1,410m) — the southernmost of the northern peaks, exposed to the northeast trade winds. Its name comes from *vientu* (wind). Cloud forest clings to its windward slopes.

Together with Monte Yukumá in the south, these five peaks defined the mountainous spine of Guajicutea. The northern peaks were working mountains — sources of stone, feathers, and gold. Yukumá was the mountain itself, the one that spoke through eruption and mudflow.

### Mina Yaruma

The ancient gold mine — **Mina Yaruma** — sits at about **940 meters elevation** on the lower eastern slopes of the northern mountain, where streams descending from Cerro Guanarí had carved into gold-bearing veins and concentrated the metal in their gravels. The name may come from *yaru* (to dig or extract) and *ma* (place), meaning "the digging place" or "the place of extraction."

Unlike the casual collection of gold nuggets from Yukumá's stream gravels, Mina Yaruma was an **organized mining site**. The Guaji worked exposed veins of gold-bearing quartz and andesite by:

- **Panning** in the streams below the mine, where gold particles had weathered out of the rock and settled in gravel bars.
- **Shallow pits** dug into decomposed vein material, following the gold-bearing zones downward where the rock was soft enough to break with stone tools.
- **Hammerstones** — dense volcanic rock used to crush quartz and release gold flakes. Broken hammerstones still litter the mine floors.
- **Grinding stones** — flat mortars where crushed ore was ground to powder and then washed to separate the heavy gold.

The work was hard and slow. The Guaji had no metal tools, no smelting, and no wheel. All extraction was done with stone, wood, and muscle. A single season's work might produce only enough gold for a few small ornaments.

Mina Yaruma was a **Caracurí operation**. The eastern sea people controlled the northern coasts and the trade routes, and the northern mountain fell within their territory. After unification in 516 CE, the mine continued under the unified Guaji but remained associated with the Caracurí tradition. The gold from Yaruma was traded south to the central valleys and eventually reached the caciques and bohíques who wore it as regalia.

The mine was not a large industrial site. It was a seasonal working place — visited during dry months when the streams were low enough for panning and the trails were passable. During the wet season, the montane forest closed over it, and the mine slept.

## Later history

After the yukubara period and the great hurricane, the volcano entered a long phase of dormancy. The merged Guaji continued to live on Guajicutea, expanding their villages along rivers and coasts. Over time, their culture changed through contact with other island groups, internal conflict, and adaptation to the island's varied environments. But the memory of Guajicutea Vieja persisted in oral tradition as the first sacred place — the village that the mountain swallowed and preserved.
