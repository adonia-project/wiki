# History of Zong (1538-1606) — Internal Notes

## Key names
- **Emperor Tiancheng** (天成) — r. 1521–1560, Tamsui founding, colonial split
- **Emperor Tianhao** (天浩) — r. 1560–c. 1585, deposed by the usurper
- **Emperor Foyuan** (佛元, "Buddhist Origin") — r. c. 1585–1589 (only 4 years), Baiduist usurper
  - Personal name: Canglan Rui (蒼蘭瑞)
  - House of Canglan (蒼蘭, "Azure Orchid") — eastern coast noble/merchant family
  - Broke the Tian- naming convention with Fo- (Buddha)
  - Scrapped Tianism entirely, provoked immediate elite resistance
- **Emperor Tianren** (天仁, "Heavenly Benevolence") — r. 1589–c. 1605, Tianhao's elder son
  - Led the Yuanlong Restoration, pursued Baiduist integration policy
  - Assassinated during the Canglan Rebellion by Baiduist conspirators
- **Emperor Tianxiao** (天孝) — r. c. 1605–1643, Tianren's younger brother
  - Inherited throne after Tianren's assassination, reversed integration policy
  - Issued 1606 Baiduist expulsion decree

## Timeline
- 1538: Tamsui founded
- 1543: Tamsui Insurgency, colonial split (Sinchew/Louyang)
- 1538–1560s: Colonial consolidation, indigenous wars
- 1560: Tianhao accedes
- 1564: Great Disappointment — Volisanians discover Zong colonies
- 1560s–1580s: Surplus crisis — colonial goods flood Zong, domestic production slumps
- 1572–1585: Great Peasant Revolt — combined economic (surplus crisis) + religious (Baiduist oppression, Baiduism as liberation)
  - Baiduist oppression: temple closures, restrictions on ordination, special taxation, surveillance, violence from Tianist militias
  - Baiduism as liberation: Ocean Crossing teaching, monasteries provided charity/education/mutual aid, spiritual equality
  - Baiduist militias became dominant military force in several provinces, parallel administration
  - Ended with the Baiduist usurpation (c. 1585)
- c. 1585: Baiduist usurpation — Canglan Rui (Foyuan) seizes throne, deposes Tianhao
- c. 1585–1589: Foyuan's 4-year reign — scrapped Tianism, religious reforms, economic mandates (halted colonial goods imports, no tariffs), Secret Trade
  - De facto split kingdom: Baiduist-controlled west and south (Foyuan from Zhongyuan), Tianist loyalist east and north
  - Tianhao fled to Louyang colony, established court-in-exile at Anping, coordinated resistance from abroad
  - Tianren (elder son) sent to eastern/northern provinces to rally mainland loyalist forces
  - Surface unity maintained in international/colonial matters
  - Colonial trade mandates alienated Louyang and Sinchew colonies → colonies lent support to reclamation army (coordinated through Tianhao's court-in-exile)
  - Normalized relations with Daras (Baiduist heartland, end of 200-year continental isolation)
  - Alienated Bingzhou: Baiduist ruler but Tianist political loyalty, supported Yuanlong loyalists, deprived Foyuan of continental ally
- 1587–1589: Yuanlong Restoration — 2-year military campaign, Tianren (Tianhao's elder son) overthrows Foyuan, who is executed
  - Restoration forced concessions to Societal Assembly: expanded legislative authority, decree ratification, self-convening, expenditure audit
  - Not full constitutional monarchy — emperor retained executive power, military, foreign policy, Tianist church
  - Assembly composition expanded: Baiduist representatives (integration policy), colonial representatives
- 1589–c. 1605: Tianren's reign — pursued integration/reform of Baiduists
  - Tianren wanted to integrate Baiduists, aware of merchant house power
  - Daaiho War (1594-1608) in Shansi/Sansei states: Wong State + Leung State merged via marriage → smaller states wanted to leave Jyunhap → merged state demanded greater tax share for defense → war
  - Collapse of Jyunhap → millions of Shansi Baiduist refugees into southern Zong
  - Factionalism: Jyunhap Baiduists (radical, militant) vs Zong Baiduists (established, cautious)
  - Canglan Rebellion (c. 1605): surviving Canglan remnants + Baiduist merchant houses + Lau Kingdom (breakaway from Jyunhap, 1605)
  - Rebel army formed in Lau Kingdom but split between loyalty to Canglan and Lau King
  - Jyunhap vs Zong Baiduist factionalism → failure of rebellion (assassination succeeded but military campaign failed)
  - Tianren assassinated by the conspirators
- c. 1605–1606: Tianxiao's accession — reversed integration policy, led to 1606 expulsion
- 1605–1606: An-Ming Understanding with Sultanate of Maros
- 1606: Baiduist Expulsion decree

## Key themes
- First Lurandian Native Wars (1538-1560s): indigenous resistance in Sinchew and Louyang; Volisanian smuggling of firearms to indigenous Lurandians via Balboa colony after Great Disappointment
- Surplus crisis: colonial goods → price crash → domestic producers ruined → agricultural contraction → artisanal depression (needs own article)
- Monetary dimension: gold/silver inflows → inflation in land/luxury, deflation in agriculture/artisanal goods
- Great Peasant Revolt: combined economic grievances (surplus crisis) + religious (Baiduist vs Tianist)
  - Firearms: Baiduist militias acquired Darasian matchlock muskets through smuggling networks via Daras; pivotal in defeating imperial army
  - Before this, Zong gunpowder use was siege weapons + hand cannons for naval operations (adopted from Darasian ships), used in colonial attempts
  - Darasian matchlocks outperformed Zong hand cannons in field combat
- Baiduist usurpation: House of Canglan, eastern coast merchant-noble family, Baiduist
- Foyuan's reforms: dismantled Tianist establishment, elevated Baiduism, land redistribution
- Secret Trade: Ong Sam, enslaved Dagit/Balausian peoples to Sinchew (1580s onward)
- Yuanlong restoration: Tianren (Tianhao's elder son) led western aristocratic + Tianist resistance
- Road to expulsion: Tianren pursued integration, assassinated in Canglan Rebellion; Tianxiao (younger brother) reversed policy
- An-Ming Understanding (1605-1606): Zong-Maros partition of New World
- Global context: Great Disappointment, Sengoku Export/Sea Wolves, War of the Mesogean, Darasian colonial expansion, Sultanate of Maros
