### The defining combination
**Tropical savanna, not rainforest.** Chidoha is the driest territory of the nine on median rainfall (1,467 mm against the Karawã–Apyãwa's 4,601 mm) and sits almost entirely in the Aw savanna class. But its rainfall is highly seasonal — the 90th percentile reaches 4,271 mm, so the country receives abundant water in a wet season and much less in a dry one.

The territory is also **unusually river-rich**: the highest mapped river density of any family. Whatever the unnavigable, lake-less quality of the Karawã–Apyãwa's country, Chidoha has real streams.

It is very low-lying (median 38 m) but contains a genuine peak of 1,578 m — the only substantial high ground in the western interior.

The coast faces the **Gulf of Louyang** — the sheltered body of water, not the open Shendan.

## 2. What that implies

Inferences from the environment, offered as a starting point. Each should be confirmed or rejected.

### Agriculture — the decisive difference

**A tropical savanna with good rivers can be farmed.** This is the essential contrast with the Karawã–Apyãwa, whose flat waterlogged forest could not. Chidoha's soils are not leached by year-round saturation, its rainfall arrives in a defined wet season suited to a planting cycle, and it has the streams to water crops through the dry months.

The likely consequence is a **grain-based agricultural economy** with a genuine agricultural calendar: planting at the onset of the rains, harvest at their end, and a dry-season period of storage, processing, and non-farm activity. That in turn supports **food surpluses**, which are what allow permanent large settlements, specialist crafts, and social hierarchy.

**This is the most consequential inference in the file.** If Chidoha are farmers, they are structurally different from the Karawã–Apyãwa in almost every downstream respect — settlement size, social complexity, storage, warfare, and religion.

### Settlement and social complexity

Surplus agriculture supports **larger and more permanent settlements** than foraging can, and the Caddoan inspiration — mound-building, with ceremonial centres and ranked lineages — fits a society of this kind. Mound centres would be expected at river junctions and on the higher ground near the 1,578 m peak, where they would not flood.

The smaller territory (87,577 km², the smallest of the nine) combined with high agricultural potential suggests **higher population density** than the Karawã–Apyãwa: a smaller area supporting more people.

### Rivers and transport

With the highest river density of any territory, rivers are likely to be **roads as well as water sources**. Unlike the Karawã–Apyãwa, whose interior had no navigable water at all, Chidoha can plausibly move goods and people by water — which supports trade, and makes river junctions the natural sites for the largest settlements.

### The coast

Gulf of Louyang frontage, ~280 km. The Gulf is sheltered water, shallow and less exposed than the open Shendan. That suggests **estuarine and near-shore fishing**, and a coastal trade role — but with easier conditions than the Karawã–Apyãwa's surf-pounded ocean shore.

### Seasonality and the year

A pronounced wet/dry cycle structures everything: the agricultural calendar, the season for warfare or raiding, the season for building, and the ritual year. The dry season is when a farming society has labour free — and, in many real-world analogues, when it fights or builds.

## 4. Open questions

### Agriculture and economy
1. **Is agriculture the basis, or mixed?** Proposed here is grain-based farming with surpluses. Alternative: mixed farming and foraging, or farming concentrated only along rivers.
2. **What crops?** The real analogue (Caddo) grew maize, beans and squash. In-world equivalents need naming — and this connects to the vocabulary work.
3. **How much did the dry season shape the economy** — seasonal migration, dry-season hunting, craft production?
4. **Irrigation, or rain-fed?** With strong seasonality and good rivers, some water management is plausible.

### Society
5. **Mound centres — yes or no?** If yes, how many, and where? River junctions and the eastern high ground are the natural sites.
6. **Social organisation** — ranked lineages, chiefdoms, a confederacy of towns? The Caddo were a confederacy of politically independent but linked communities.
7. **How does Chidoha differ from Kawesa?** Both are mound-builders in the current notes; they need distinct identities.
8. **Population density** relative to the Karawã–Apyãwa — assumed higher, but how much?

### The two languages
9. **Relationship between Chidoha and Nakoha** — dialects of one language, or two related languages? Are they the two halves of a divided population (e.g. river valley vs coast)?

### The colonial era (entirely unresolved)
10. **What happened to Chidoha under Zong colonisation?** They are inland and riverine, not coastal-forest — so the Karawã–Apyãwa pattern does not transfer automatically. Were they traders, intermediaries, or conquered?
11. **Did the Great Pestilence reach them** as it did the Karawã–Apyãwa? A river-and-trade society would transmit disease quickly — possibly worse than the isolated forest peoples.
12. **Do they appear anywhere in existing lore?** Currently they do not. Any Chidoha role in the colonial period, the Sinchew War, or the Free Sinchew confederation is unwritten.
