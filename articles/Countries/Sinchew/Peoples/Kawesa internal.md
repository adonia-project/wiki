## 2. The two groups — and a third element

### The north–south split is real and sharp

| | **northern Kawesa** | **southern Kawesa** |

| latitudes | 2.3–4.0°S | 4.0–9.1°S |
| area | ~62,000 km² | ~352,000 km² |
| median elevation | **79–158 m** | **292–327 m** |
| rainfall | **2,266–2,547 mm** | **1,055–2,374 mm** |
| country | low, wet, forest and monsoon | higher, drier, savanna |
| neighbours | **Timara** | **Hachina**, **Nahola**, Chidoha |
| principal war | with the **Timara** | with the **Hachina** |

The two halves of one people differ by **1,500 mm of annual rainfall** and 150 m of elevation. That is the widest internal environmental range of any family, and it maps directly onto the two rivalries the user identifies.

**Northern Kawesa fought the Timara. Southern Kawesa fought the Hachina and the Nahola.**

### The hills in the middle

**30,352 km² of land above 600 m**, centred at (−108.14, −5.89) and running from 3.3°S to 8.4°S. This is the ridge country that splits the Kawesa world, and it is probably the physical reason there is no single Kawesa state: the terrain divides the people into basins that face different directions, fight different enemies, and have little reason to combine.

The highlands are also, on the evidence of the Hachina notes, the source of the gold that the Hachina were fighting through Kawesa to reach.

## 3. "Kawesa" is an exonym, not a people

**User, 2026-09-21:** *"I think Kawesa probably was not a genuine people, we can probably split it up, but I think from the hachina and the zong perspective they were the same people because of the language and the lack of diplomacy from all of them, I think maybe 7 clans that spoke similar languages and probably had a root but at this point they were disparate, kinda like 'barbarians' in europe they were not the same people but to the zong and hachina they were."*

This reframes the entire entry, and it is a better solution than treating Kawesa as a nation.

### The term
**"Kawesa" is an umbrella label applied from outside.** It denotes a set of politically separate clans whose languages were recognisably related and none of whom conducted diplomacy that would have distinguished them from one another. To the Zong and the Hachina — literate, courtly, treaty-making peoples — that combination read as a single people, and they named it accordingly.

The analogy is the classical one: "barbarian", "Scythian", "Celtic". Not a people but a category, covering groups that were related in language and comparable in culture, and which outsiders had neither the means nor the motive to tell apart.

### The name is a modern coinage (user, 2026-09-21, corrected)

User: *"kawesa is a modern name as in it was given to them from records/histories to give them a proper name, can we not use kawesha, probably would be different word entirely like akwesa was likely the name of one clan that had their history preserved to modernity."*

**Three distinct things, not one chain:**

| | what it is | status |
|---|---|---|
| **the Hachina name** | what the Hachina called these peoples, adopted by the Zong | a **separate word entirely** — not yet determined |
| **Akwesa** | the name of **one chiefdom** — the one whose history was preserved into the modern era | a genuine chiefdom name |
| **"Kawesa"** | a **modern coinage**, derived from *Akwesa*, applied to the whole group | modern historiographical / administrative term |

### What this means
**"Kawesa" is not a historical name at all.** It is a modern term, given to these peoples from surviving records in order to give them a proper name. The records did not preserve a name for the group — because there never was one — so the modern state took the name of the single clan whose history survived and applied it to everyone.

**Akwesa is that clan.** Its history was preserved to modernity, which is what made it available to be borrowed. The others left no name that survived.

**This is the archaeology of the exonym.** "Kawesa" looks like an old term and is not one. It is a modern label retro-projected onto a group that never had a collective name, and it carries within it the accident of which clan happened to be documented.

### Akwesa was the Tamsui clan — confirmed
User, 2026-09-21: *"akwesa were the people who sold the land to tamsui so they were able to survive as a minoirty group within the new sinchew colony."*

Confirmed. **Akwesa was the chiefdom whose bay the colony acquired by purchase**, and that is why its history survived and why its name became the modern one.

### The Kawesa War was genocidal
User, 2026-09-21: *"I think the kawesa war was really a very genocidal war that had killed and enslaved most fo the kawesa."*

The Kawesa War (11 August 1538 – 7 April 1542) **destroyed most of the Kawesa population.** The campaigns of those four years killed and enslaved the greater part of the seven chiefdoms, and what survived did so as scattered remnants rather than as peoples.

**The war was not a conquest with an administration to follow.** The Zong had no intention of governing the Kawesa, per the territorial-island doctrine — they wanted a harbour. Hachina wanted the southern lands and the gold approaches. Neither party needed the Kawesa alive. What followed the war was therefore not subjection but **removal**: land taken, populations killed or enslaved, survivors dispersed.

### Who survived, and why
**Akwesa survived because Akwesa had already made its deal.** The chiefdom whose bay had passed to the colony before the war was, by 1542, a client of the Zong rather than a target of them. It survived **as a minority group within the new Sinchew colony**, on land it had not had to defend because it had sold the rest.

**This is the crux of the modern name.** "Kawesa" descends from **Akwesa** — the one chiefdom that survived to be documented, because it was the one chiefdom that sided with the victors. The name of a people that was largely annihilated is taken from the single chiefdom that profited from the transaction which destroyed them.

### How history gets written
On the question of whether the modern country acknowledges any of this — the user's answer is the correct one: *"thats how history is written right, by those who actually survived."*

The Sinchewese record of "the Kawesa" is the record of **the survivors of a genocide, written by a people that committed it, and named after the collaborators.** There is no Kawesa account of the Kawesa War, because the Kawesa who could have written one did not survive it. The only continuous thread is Akwesa's, and Akwesa's thread runs through the colonial administration.

**Whether modern Sinchew knows any of this** — that "Kawesa" is a 20th-century coinage, that it names a genocide, that it names the victims after a chiefdom that enabled it — is an open question, and a live one. It would be entirely realistic for the accepted national history to present the Kawesa War as the necessary clearing of a dangerous frontier, and for the name's origin to be a matter for specialists rather than schoolbooks.


Akwesa was the chiefdom the Zong dealt with directly in the Tamsui Plan of 1535, whose land was bought, and whose affairs therefore appear in the colonial record. **Documentation is what preserved it** — and documentation is exactly what the other six chiefdoms lacked, first because nobody recorded them, and then because most of them did not survive.

### The Hachina term for them — "barbarian"
User, 2026-09-21: *"maybe we can figure out a word for 'barbarian' that the hachina used for them, it was also used to describe Chidoha and Salowi as well, but they probably mainly used it to refer to the Kawesa people."*

**The Hachina had a category, not a name.** They called the stateless peoples to their north and west ***mahik*** — "kingless", from *ma-* (negative) + *hik* "king".

The term was applied to the **Kawesa**, and also to **Chidoha** and **Salowi** — the three families with no kings. It was used **principally of the Kawesa**, who were the largest and nearest of the three.

**The term is pejorative.** User, 2026-09-21: *"he was a 'king' to his own people, even if it was a small politie, the Hahkina called them kingless but it was pejorative."*

**Do not treat *mahik* as a neutral description.** I previously wrote that it was "precisely accurate" because none of the three families had a monarchy, and framed it as an observation that hardened into a category. **That was wrong.** It is an exonym of disparagement applied by a centralised neighbour to peoples it judged deficient, and it says what the Hahkina thought these peoples lacked, not what they were.

**The Kawesa had kings.** They were small polities, but their rulers were kings to their own people. The Hahkina's "kingless" means only that they had no ruler of the *Hahkina* kind — a monarch of a centralised state with a capital. Reading that as a statement about their actual political organisation mistakes the Hahkina's standard for a universal one.

**This is close to a name but is not one.** It says what the Hachina thought these peoples lacked, not what they were called. It is the nearest thing to a period term for the Kawesa that survives, and it is why the Hachina and Zong could speak of them as a single kind of thing.

### The Hachina name is still open
The Hachina gave these peoples a name, and the Zong adopted it. That word is **not** "Kawesa" and **not** "Akwesa" — it is a separate term, and it is what the Hachina and Zong actually called them in the period. It remains to be determined, and it is likely to be a Hachina-language word.

### What was actually there
**Roughly seven chiefdoms**, speaking **similar but distinct languages** sharing a common root, and by the 1400s politically **disparate**. They shared a linguistic ancestry and a broad cultural family. They did not share a government, a diplomacy, or — probably — a sense of themselves as one people.

### Consequences for the established history

| event | how it reads under this model |
|---|---|
| **Tamsui Plan (1535)** | The Zong "allied with the chiefdom around the modern Tamsui location" — they dealt at *chiefdom* level when it suited them, while thinking in "Kawesa" terms. The exonym was a convenience, not an error. |
| **Tamsui founded (1538)** | Acquired from the **one chiefdom** holding it, on land the other six had no say over. No Kawesa authority existed to object. |
| **Kawesa War (1538–1542)** | Fought against *chiefdoms*, not a state. The Zong and Hachina beat a set of separate forces that could not combine. |
| **"Partition of Kawesa territory"** | **Partly a fiction imposed by the victors.** There was no Kawesa to partition. Two powers drew a line across a region inhabited by seven unrelated polities and called the halves of it after their own colonies. |
| **Why they were never destroyed** | There was no capital, no throne, no single authority whose submission could end resistance — because there was no single people. |

**The deepest consequence:** the modern map of Sinchew shows "Kawesa" as a single territory, because the Zong treated it as one and the colonial state inherited that assumption. Whether a **Kawesa identity** exists in the modern country — as something people actually claim — is a separate question, and probably a post-colonial one.

## 4. Political structure — a decentralised chiefdom league

**Kawesa is not a state.** In the 1400s it is a **set of roughly seven politically separate chiefdoms** — each with its own leadership, its own wars, and its own grudges, sharing a language family and little else.

### Why the Muskogean mirror fits
The framework assigns Kawesa the **Muskogean** mirror (Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw). Applied to politics rather than phonology, this is an unusually good fit: the real **Creek Confederacy** was precisely a **loose league of autonomous towns**, bound by common language and custom, capable of joint action against outsiders, and structurally incapable of acting as a unitary state.

The divergence is that even the Creek had a league. **The Kawesa chiefdoms may have had less than that** — a shared language and a common enemy, and no council at all.

### What the structure explains

- **Why Tamsui fell so easily.** No chiefdom can mount the kind of concentrated defence a kingdom can, and the Zong dealt with one chiefdom and bought it.
- **Why the Zong targeted them.** The Zong knew the Kawesa were militant — and knew the chiefdoms fought each other constantly.
- **Why the Kawesa War (1538–1542) succeeded.** Hachina and the Zong together outmatched a set of separate forces that could not combine against them.
- **Why they were never fully conquered.** Equally, a set of separate chiefdoms is not easy to destroy outright: there is no capital to take and no throne to occupy.

User, 2026-09-21: *"I think in the 1400s is a decentralized alliance of a few kawesa speaking clans."*

**Kawesa is not a state.** In the 1400s it is a **decentralised alliance of several Kawesa-speaking chiefdoms** — a league rather than a kingdom, holding the largest territory in Sinchew with the weakest central authority over it.

### Why the Muskogean mirror fits
The framework assigns Kawesa the **Muskogean** mirror (Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw). Applied to politics rather than phonology, this is an unusually good fit: the real **Creek Confederacy** was precisely a **loose league of autonomous towns**, bound by common language and custom, capable of joint action against outsiders, and structurally incapable of acting as a unitary state. That is exactly the Kawesa the user describes — and it is also why the Zong found them easy to exploit and hard to defeat.

**Note:** per the resolution agreed for Hachina (2026-09-21), the mirror supplies **phonology**, and culture is invented freely. But in this case the mirror's political template happens to match what the user has independently specified, so it can be used as a model with confidence.

### What the structure explains

- **Why Kawesa lost Tamsui so easily.** A league of chiefdoms cannot mount the kind of concentrated defence a single kingdom can. The Zong dealt with *the chiefdom holding the Tamsui site*, and bought it.
- **Why the Tamsui Plan targeted them.** The Zong knew the Kawesa were militant — and knew the chiefdoms fought each other constantly. Fragmented politics are exploitable politics.
- **Why the Kawesa War (1538–1542) succeeded.** Hachina and the Zong together outmatched a coalition of chiefdoms that could not combine against them.
- **Why they were never fully conquered.** Equally, no chiefdom league is easy to destroy outright: there is no capital to take, no throne to occupy, no single authority whose submission ends resistance.

## 6. Open questions

### The chiefdoms and the exonym
1. **Confirm the chiefdom count** — the user suggests **seven**. Should the linguistic framework's five Kawesa-family languages be reconciled with seven chiefdoms (some languages spanning two chiefdoms), or should the family expand to seven languages?
2. **Clan names** — none yet. The framework's five language names (Kawesa, Chinapa, Talowa, Hokchita, Yakona) could serve, plus two more. But if "Kawesa" is an exonym for the whole group, it is odd for one *chiefdom* to bear it — unless one chiefdom's name became the umbrella term, which is a common origin for exonyms and worth considering.
3. **What did the chiefdoms call themselves?** Each presumably had its own name, none of which survives in the colonial record — because the Zong used the Hachina term instead.
3b. **The Zong rendering** — was *kawesha* transliterated into Hokkien characters or translated? A translation ("the many peoples" → something like 諸邦) would be a different kind of borrowing from a transliteration, and would suggest the Zong understood the meaning when they took it.
4. **Which chiefdom held Tamsui**, and is it remembered as the one that sold the coast?
5. **Did any two chiefdoms act jointly** against the Zong or Hachina, or was every response single-chiefdom?
6. **Is there a Kawesa identity in the modern country** — do people claim it — and is that a post-colonial development? This is the question that decides whether the exonym eventually became real.
7. **Do the northern and southern Kawesa know they are different** from each other, or does the language family mask the distinction to them as much as to outsiders?
8. **How did the Zong decide** which chiefdom was "the Tamsui chiefdom"? Did they identify a leader, or simply buy the nearest one?
9. **Is there a Kawesa council of any kind** — a meeting, a seasonal assembly, a shared ritual — or literally nothing holding the groups together but language?

### Older questions

3. **What binds them** when they fight each other constantly? Shared tongue, marriage, ritual, a common enemy, or seasonal assembly?
4. **Which chiefdom held Tamsui**, and is it remembered as the one that sold the coast?

### The wars
6. **How old is the Timara rivalry** in the north, and what is it over — land, fishing, the long lake?
7. **How old is the Hachina war** in the south, and is it primarily about gold?
8. **Is Nahola an ally of Hachina** (as the user indicates) or a tributary, or both at different times?
9. **Did the Kawesa ever beat either enemy decisively?** A league that only ever loses is less interesting than one that wins sometimes.
10. **What did the Kawesa do after 1542** — did the southern chiefdoms become Hachina subjects, and the northern ones Zong subjects? Or did the league persist?

### Culture and economy
11. **Are the northern Kawesa farmers or fishers?** Low, wet, 2,500 mm — poor for grain, perhaps good for wet-ground crops and riverine fishing. Compare Karawã–Apyãwa, whose environment is similar.
12. **Are the southern Kawesa grain farmers?** Higher, drier, savanna — much better for cereals. If so, the southern chiefdoms may be closer to the Hachina in economy than to their own northern kin, and the two halves of Kawesa may have less in common than the shared language implies.
13. **What does the Gulf coast give them** — ports, fishing, trade? They held the best harbours in Sinchew.
14. **Do they work the gold themselves**, or trade it?
15. **Where is the league's meeting place**, if it has one?

### Language
16. **Do the northern and southern dialects diverge** as much as their environments? If the Kawesa family has five languages, the two halves may not speak mutually intelligible forms — which would make "a Kawesa-speaking alliance" a looser thing than it sounds.
17. **Build the Kawesa lexicon** on Muskogean phonology, as with the Hachina lexicon.

### The two corrections
**`chiefdom` → `chiefdom`** throughout all Sinchew and Zong colonial material (10 files). The Akwesa were a **chiefdom**, and so were the other six; the collective term is **the Kawesa chiefdoms**. Do not call them chiefdoms.

**Hokta is a KING, not a chief.** The Hahkina's *mahik* ("kingless") is a **pejorative exonym** — it describes the absence of a ruler *of the centralised Hahkina kind*, not the absence of a ruler. Do not treat it as a neutral ethnographic category. I did, twice, and was corrected both times.

### Emic vs etic — hold both at once
- **Chiefdom** = the analytical term for the political unit
- **King** = the Akwesa's own term for Hokta ("a king to his own people")

**Chiefdom** describes the polity type; **king** describes how its ruler was regarded internally. The Hahkina's "kingless" measures the Akwesa against a standard they did not hold.

### Patriarchal succession and the marriage of 1521
**The Akwesa practised male descent.** Hokta had **no sons and two daughters — Chinowa and Oktisa**. The absence of a son left the kingship without a direct heir, and this is the context of the 1521 marriage: **uniting Chinowa with Chiu Khéng-gio̍k supplied the chiefdom with a male figure of standing connected to Hokta's line, at the same time as it secured the alliance with the Sūn-heng Hâng.** The alliance and the succession crisis were the same problem.

**Still open:** how the succession was actually determined in 1527, and to whom the kingship passed. If it ran through a daughter's husband, **CKG may have been king of the Akwesa from 1527** — which reframes his entire later career (governor of his own chiefdom from 1535, removed in 1543, ambassador to his own people thereafter).
