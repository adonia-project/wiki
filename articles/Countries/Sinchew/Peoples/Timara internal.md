Timara is second only to Karawã–Apyãwa in rainfall, and has the longest coastline of any family. It is the wettest and most maritime of the northern families.

### The two halves — as the user described

The territory divides sharply along latitude.

| latitude band | area | median elevation | lakes | dominant climate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5–2.0°S | 84,744 km² | **18 m** | none | **Af 99%** |
| 2.0–3.0°S | 125,648 km² | 189 m | none | Af 95% |
| **3.0–4.0°S** | 41,101 km² | 208 m | adjoins the long lake | **Am 74%** |

**The northern half** is flat, low and continuously wet — 99% tropical rainforest at a median elevation of 18 m, with a long exposed coastline. Environmentally this is nearly identical to Karawã–Apyãwa territory: saturated ground, no lakes, water everywhere and gathered nowhere.

**The southern portion** is different. It rises to a median of 208 m, shifts from rainforest to tropical monsoon (Am 74%), and adjoins the long lake. This is the part the user identified as a **lake culture**.

## 2. The lake — and the Kawesa

The long lake (14,705 km², 477 × 144 km, east–west elongated) lies at 3.64°S, 107.4°W, in the shallow trough described in the geography notes — higher ground on both its northern and southern sides. Its shores are divided:

| family | share of lake shore |
|---|---|
| **Kawesa** | **54%** |
| **Timara** | **46%** |

**The lake is divided between exactly two peoples, and the division is east–west.** Surveying the family sequence across the lake's latitude (3.64°S) gives, west to east: **Kawesa → Timara → Nahola → Uwisa → Karawã–Apyãwa**. Kawesa holds the western shore, Timara the eastern shore. **They face each other directly across the water, dividing it almost exactly in half.**

That is the structural basis for the conflict the user proposes. Two peoples on opposite sides of a single large lake, in a region where standing water is otherwise almost absent — the lake would be the only such resource for hundreds of kilometres in any direction.

### Resolved: the earlier Yelani share was a map artifact
An earlier version of the map gave Yelani 35% of the lake shore. That was spurious — the rule forcing all land above 780 m to Yelani was grabbing isolated high points around the lake basin, far from Yelani's actual mountain territory, producing disconnected slivers. The user spotted it immediately ("what yelani is in the mountains probably a bug") and estimated the true division as an even 50/50.

**Fixed 2026-09-19.** The map rebuild now prunes disconnected fragments from every family and reassigns them by cost. Yelani retains only its contiguous mountain belt, and the lake shore falls to Timara and Kawesa alone.

## 3. What the user has proposed

User's framing, 2026-09-19: *"they have a similar climate for half of the group in the north, but the southern ones who live in the valley with that long lake probably were a lake culture that fought probably a lot with the Kawesa people, who came from the south, probably those two cultures are linked because of that lake, even though I think the Kawesa is probably split into a few different groups."*

Four distinct claims, all of which the data supports:

1. **Half the group shares the northern climate.** Confirmed — the northern bands are 95–99% Af rainforest, effectively the same environment as Karawã–Apyãwa.
2. **The southerners are a lake culture.** Confirmed — the southern band is climatically distinct (Am), sits higher, and adjoins the lake.
3. **They fought the Kawesa, who came from the south.** Structurally supported by the shared lake, and the direction of Kawesa expansion is a separate question (see below).
4. **Kawesa is probably not one people but several.** This is very likely correct and is flagged for when Kawesa is developed — it is the largest family at 21% of the country, and a group that size occupying the central plateau would be a set of related peoples, not a single polity.

The reading these claims point to — that Timara is not one culture but two — was then confirmed by the user and is developed in section 4 below.

## 4. Cultural history — the two Timara (user, 2026-09-19)

User's framing: *"I think the timara is two groups, I think they share a language family but I think the northern ones are coastal similar to the karawa, but because they were not connected to the biru and closer to tamsui they were unaware but also because of their less desireable land the northern ones were not as messed with, they were not afraid when the pestilience came so it came as a surprise, they also did not go into the jungles because they probably had disputes with the southern peoples who they had fought, the southern peoples were probably always fighting, I think the zong government used this to use them to help them defeat the northern people and wage war against the kawesa, which was on the territory that was where tamsui and more desirable area was."*

**Timara is not one people but two**, sharing a language family and a name. This is the resolution of the central question in this file.

### The geography that drives it

The two halves occupy opposite kinds of coast and opposite kinds of country.

**Northern Timara** hold the **northern coast** — the **Shendan** side. This is open ocean, deep and unsheltered, with no natural harbours. Inland, the land is flat, saturated and low: 99% tropical rainforest at a median elevation of 18 m. Environmentally this is nearly identical to Karawã–Apyãwa territory. Their land is **wet, flat and hard to exploit** — and their coast offers ships nothing.

**Southern Timara** face the **long lake**, in monsoon country at higher ground. And beyond them, the **Kawesa** hold the **western coast** — the **Gulf of Louyang** side, which is **sheltered water with usable harbours**.

### Tamsui sits on Kawesa land — confirmed
The Zong colonial capital, founded **1538**, is at **(−111.59, −4.01)**, elevation 2 m, directly on the coast. **It lies within Kawesa territory.** The Zong built their colony on the sheltered gulf coast, in the most desirable harbour land in the region — not on the exposed northern shore.

That is the material basis of everything that follows.

### Why the northerners were caught unprepared

Three factors left northern Timara exposed:

1. **They were not connected to the Birú.** The Karawã–Apyãwa hold the Balboa border and watched the Birú destroyed — they had the object lesson. The northern Timara had no such warning, and no route by which news of the isthmus catastrophe would reach them.
2. **Their land was not worth taking.** The Zong took what they wanted on the Gulf coast. The northern Shendan shore, with no harbours and swamp behind it, offered little, so **the northerners were left largely alone** — not protected, simply uninteresting.
3. **So when the [[Great Pestilence]] came, it came as a surprise.** The Karawã–Apyãwa had reason to fear what was crossing the ocean. The northern Timara had none.

### Why they did not flee inland

The Karawã–Apyãwa retreated into the forest when catastrophe came. **The northern Timara could not do the same — because the forest was held by their enemies.**

The southern Timara were inland, lake-facing, and had long been in conflict with the north. Retreating into the interior would have meant walking into hostile territory. **The coast, however bad, was theirs.** So they stayed, and were conquered where they stood.

This is the tragedy of the northerners in one line: they had nowhere to go that was not already an enemy's.

### The Zong use the division

The Zong colonial administration exploited the Timara split. According to the user's framing, they **used the southern Timara against the northern**, and directed them also against the **Kawesa** — whose territory held Tamsui and the desirable gulf coast.

This is textbook colonial practice: find an existing enmity, arm one side, and let it do work that would otherwise cost you troops. The southern Timara gained Zong favour and a free hand against old enemies; the Zong gained local manpower against both the northerners and the Kawesa, at no cost to themselves.

It also left a poisoned inheritance. The southern Timara were made into instruments of colonial conquest against their own linguistic kin and their neighbours — which is a different and heavier legacy than simple defeat.

### What this makes of the Timara
Two peoples, one language family, divided by geography and then deliberately set against each other. The northerners were the ones who lost everything: unprepared, unable to flee, conquered with their own cousins' help. The southerners survived but became collaborators in the destruction of the northern half of their own people.

**The Kawesa, meanwhile, lost the most valuable land in the region** — Tamsui and the gulf coast — to a foreign power, having been attacked from two directions at once.

## 5. Open questions

### The lake and the conflict
1. **Is the lake the reason for Timara–Kawesa hostility**, or one arena among several? What else do they contend over?
2. **What is the lake called** by each side? If they fought over it for generations, they may have different names for it — which would be revealing.
3. **Did they fight over the water itself** (fisheries, navigation) or over the surrounding land?
4. **Was there ever peace** — intermarriage, trade, shared ritual? A permanent war across a shared lake is difficult to sustain; is it punctuated conflict or continuous hostility?
5. **Which side expanded?** The user says Kawesa "came from the south." If they pushed north into the lake basin, the conflict is a story of Timara defending. If Timara pushed south, it reverses.

### Northern vs southern Timara
6. **Are they one people or two?** This is the central question. Shared language family, shared name — but different environments.
7. **If one people, what binds them** — kinship, trade, ritual, a common origin story?
8. **If two, do they acknowledge the kinship?** Related peoples who fight each other is a different story from a single nation with an internal rift.
9. **Does the coast connect them**, or is the north's maritime orientation separate from the south's lake orientation?

### Subsistence
10. **Does northern Timara fish the sea like the Karawã–Apyãwa?** Same environment, same coastline exposure, so probably yes.
11. **Is southern Timara's economy built on lake fishing** — and does that make them richer or more settled than the northerners?
12. **Agriculture** — the southern lakeshore is the best agricultural ground in Timara, at higher elevation with seasonal monsoon. Could this be a cultivating society, unlike the northern swamp?

### Other
13. **What does "Timara" mean**, and is it an endonym or an exonym? If it is what the *Kawesa* call them, that would be telling.
14. **Is the coastline densely settled**, given it is the longest of any family and the north is flat and wet?
15. **Relationship to Karawã–Apyãwa** — the two northern families share a climate and a coastline. Are they neighbours in contact, or separated by the width of the Timara territory?
