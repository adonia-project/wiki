### Why this is the agricultural heartland

Hachina has the best combination in Sinchew of the three things grain agriculture needs:

- **Uniform gentle ground** — 79% of the territory in a single 170 m elevation band. Nothing else comes close for extent of uninterrupted arable plain.
- **Adequate but not excessive rain** — 1,425 mm is ample for rain-fed grain. Compare 4,605 mm in Karawã–Apyãwa, where the ground is permanently saturated and nutrients leach away, or 3,187 mm in Nahola's uplands.
- **Warmth** — 21.1 °C median, with a long growing season.

It is also the only family whose climate is **savanna and humid subtropical** rather than rainforest — the drier, more seasonal regime that favours cereals over root crops and tree crops. **Hachina is where a grain civilisation would arise, and nowhere else in Sinchew has comparable advantages.**

**The critical asymmetry:** Hachina's two largest neighbours, Uwisa and Nahola, sit **250 m higher** than Hachina and are dominated by upland terrain. Their rainfall is higher but their ground is rougher and their seasons harsher.

**This is the classic lowland-grain-state versus upland-neighbour relationship.** The plain produces the surplus; the uplands produce less and depend on the plain for grain. That is the material basis for the subservience the user proposes — it is not simply that Hachina conquered them, but that they needed what Hachina grew.

## 3. The empire (user's framing, 2026-09-21)

User: *"I think the hachina was the first of these groups to develop a large centralized state on grain agriculture and probably had a complex civilization, so we can work on that, we can probably give a name for their empire, I think they probably could subjugate or at least create subservient states of their neighbors like the nahola and uwisa who were in more upland/less ideal agriculture."*

### The proposition
Hachina developed **the first large centralised state in Sinchew**, founded on **grain agriculture**, and produced a **complex civilisation**. It **subjugated or made tributary** its upland neighbours — **Nahola and Uwisa** in particular — whose rougher, higher, wetter terrain gave them less productive agriculture.

This is coherent, and the geography supports it:

- Hachina alone has the uniform arable plain. A grain surplus concentrated in one territory is precisely what funds a state apparatus — taxation, standing forces, priesthood, monumental building, a literate bureaucracy.
- Uwisa and Nahola have the *higher ground and worse farmland*, and both border Hachina heavily (1,906 and 1,509 boundary cells). Upland peoples adjacent to a grain plain is a well-attested pattern of dependency, tribute and intermittent revolt.
- Kawesa, by contrast, **is not a single polity** — see below.

### The Kawesa War (1538–1542) — the alliance in action
**Confirmed by the user, 2026-09-21:** *"this also lead to the Kawesa War (August 11 1538 – April 7 1542) which had seen a military alliance between the hachina and the zong colonial government to divide and split the kawesa territory between the hachina and zong, north would become sinchew and the south becoming the kawesa controll hachina."*

The treaty was not a settlement but an alliance, and it went to war within months.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Dates** | 11 August 1538 – 7 April 1542 |
| **Belligerents** | Hahkina + Zong colonial forces vs. the Kawesa peoples |
| **Object** | partition of Kawesa territory |
| **Outcome** | **North** (including Tamsui and the coast) → Zong → became **Sinchew**; **South** (the Kawesa lands adjoining the Hahkina frontier) → **Hahkina** |

**This makes the pincer literal.** The earlier analysis predicted Hachina would use a Zong colony at Tamsui to pin Kawesa while taking the south. That is exactly what happened, formalised in an alliance and executed over four years.

**And it explains the colonial split better than the Tamsui Plot alone does.** By 1542 the Zong held a new coast of their own — no longer dependent on Louyang, no longer merely a city within another colony's jurisdiction. Within a year the territory was divided along the Qingjiang. **The Tamsui Plot of 1543 supplied the occasion; the war supplied the reason.**

### What the alliance cost Hachina
They gained the southern Kawesa lands and the gold approaches they had fought for — and lost the thing they had been protecting. The Zong were no longer a distant power with a harbour; they were a neighbouring state with a frontier, and the frontier ran along the land Hachina had just helped them take.

### What the Zong called them — Hâ-pang, then Hahkina
**Confirmed by the user, 2026-09-21.**

The Zong colonial administration named them twice, and the change is revealing.

| period | name | characters | Hokkien | meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **early (from 1458)** | **Hâ-pang** | 遐邦 | Hâ-pang | "the distant state" |
| **later, once envoys were exchanged** | **Hahkina** | 哈其那 | Hah-kî-ná | the Hachina's own name, rendered phonetically |

**Hâ-pang** is a diplomatic register. It acknowledges Hachina as a *state* — which the Zong could hardly avoid, given a king, a regnal council and an army — while locating them safely at the edge of the world. It also declines, politely, to repeat the Hachina's own claim to be *the centre*. In a Tianist imperial worldview where the Mandate of Heaven is singular, a foreign people calling themselves the central realm is either impressive or presumptuous, and "the distant state" sidesteps the question entirely.

**Hahkina** marks the shift. Once envoys were exchanged and relations became formal, the Zong stopped describing Hachina positionally and started using their name. That is the difference between a place on a map and a polity you negotiate with.

### What the Zong name conspicuously excludes
**Nothing about gold.** The Zong archive called them "the distant state" and then simply "the Hachina" — never "the gold country." Which is itself telling: the gold was being smuggled, not declared. Had the colonial record named them for their gold, someone in the metropole might eventually have asked why none of it was reaching the treasury.

### A note on the mirror
Hachina's language family is inspired by **Chitimacha/Atakapa** — but per the user (2026-09-21), *"we just need the language as a base, the culture we can modify."* **The mirror supplies phonology only.** Hachina's institutions, religion, architecture and social structure are invented freely and are not bound by what real Chitimacha or Atakapa societies were like. If the state needs monumentality, chiefdoms or a priest-king, it has them.

### A contrast with the north
Nothing in Karawã–Apyãwa, Timara or Salowi could produce this. Those are saturated rainforest and coastal fishing economies with no significant arable base. Hachina is the only family whose environment can support concentrated agrarian wealth, which makes its early state formation **structurally determined rather than accidental.**

## 4. Kawesa — fragmented, not unified

User: *"I think the kawesa is more split up since they weren't as unified because they had some hills mountains and more adverse climate."*

Supported by the data. Kawesa is the largest family by area (413,722 km²) but its terrain is **the most varied of any lowland family**: 49% plateau, 19% lowland, 15% coastal, 16% above 430 m, with the highest elevation spread of any family outside Yelani (608 m between p5 and p95, against Hachina's 423 m).

That heterogeneity — hills, coast, plateau, mountains, and a wetter Aw/Am climate (1,906 mm) — is the classic condition for **many small polities rather than one large one**. Fragmented terrain favours fragmented politics. This also explains why Kawesa lost Tamsui and the gulf coast so readily: not one strong state to resist, but a set of smaller ones.

This resolves the earlier note that Kawesa was "probably split into a few different groups" — it is not merely a guess but follows from the terrain.

---

### The southern frontier — the Tsuwasha
The southern limit of Hahkina territory was the **Tsuwasha** (*tsu* "south" + *washa* "water") — the river running west to east across what is now northern [[Louyang]]. South of it lay further mahik peoples, known to the Hahkina as the **southern mahik**, distinct from the Kawesa clans on their northern frontier.

**The treaty was what made this a border at all.** Before 1538 the Hahkina claimed authority over their tributaries and neighbours without delimitation; the Treaty of Anping obliged them to accept a defined territory with a named boundary. For the Hahkina this was a change in kind rather than degree: from a sphere of influence to a state with edges. It is one of the earliest instances in the region of an indigenous polity acquiring a delimited frontier.

## 5. The Louyang extension

### Hachina crosses the border
Hachina's coastline runs south to **lat 10.99°S at lon −107.08 — where the distance to Louyang is 0.00 km.** Their territory is **continuous across the southern frontier**. The straight-line border at ~11°S is a colonial artefact that cuts a single people in half.

**The user's claim that Hachina also exists in northern Louyang is therefore confirmed by geography.**

### The approach from the west
User: *"I think they travelled along the coast of louyang, so they would be coming from the west."*

Consistent with the coast. Hachina's shoreline faces **west and south-west** along the Gulf of Louyang, with sea bearings of SW 64 / W 50 / S 44 from the coastal samples. Vessels working **eastward along Louyang's northern coast** would arrive at Hachina's shore without ever crossing open ocean.

**The geographic consequence is significant.** Hachina is the first Sinchewese territory a westward-originating expedition reaches by coasting. The rest of the country — the northern rainforests, the eastern highlands — lies beyond them, up an unfamiliar shore. So the first Sinchewese people the Zong encountered were the most organised polity in the country, because they were also the most accessible one from the Louyang side.

First contact itself is treated in section 7.

## 6. Crops — what the land can actually grow

### The arable base, measured

| band | share | area |
|---|---|---|
| coastal (<125 m) | 3.9% | ~12,900 km² |
| lowland (125–260 m) | 6.6% | ~21,800 km² |
| **plateau (260–430 m)** | **79.5%** | **~260,900 km²** |
| upland (430–560 m) | 2.1% | ~6,900 km² |
| upper (560–800 m) | 7.4% | ~24,200 km² |

**Roughly 260,000 km² of near-uniform plain at a comfortable elevation.** That is an enormous arable base — comparable in extent to the whole of the British Isles.

### Water
**36 distinct mapped rivers** pass through Hachina, with a river density of **0.0075 km/km² — the second highest of the nine families** (behind only Chidoha). Rainfall is 1,425 mm median, ranging from **1,113 mm in a dry year to 1,731 mm in a wet one**.

This matters more than total rainfall: the low p10 of 1,113 mm signals **a real dry season**, which is what the Aw classification implies.

### What that combination supports

The environment is a **tropical savanna with perennial rivers on a vast flat plain** — the classic setting for a rice-and-millet agrarian state.

| crop | where | why |
|---|---|---|
| **Rice** | river valleys and irrigated terraces | 36 rivers; flat land makes canal irrigation unusually easy |
| **Sorghum** | the rain-fed plain | drought-tolerant; the standard savanna cereal and the safest staple across dry years |
| **Millets** (pearl, finger) | drier margins | short-season, tolerate poor soil and low water |
| **Maize** | plateau, where rain is reliable | productive, but less resilient to the dry years |
| **Pulses** (cowpea, pigeon pea) | rotating with cereals | fixes nitrogen; classic companion to sorghum |
| **Sesame, groundnut** | warm margins | oil crops; also a likely trade good |
| **Yams, cassava, plantain** | wetter ground, river margins | supplementary, not staples — the savanna is not rainforest |
| **Cotton** | dry-season plains | plausible fibre crop and export |

**The decisive point is irrigation.** A plain this flat, crossed by 36 rivers, is close to an ideal irrigation landscape — the gradient is gentle enough to run canals cheaply across enormous distances. That is precisely how the great river-valley civilisations of the real world funded themselves: irrigation works → reliable double cropping → concentrated surplus → taxation → state apparatus.

### The agricultural gradient against their neighbours
This is what makes the subservience of **Nahola and Uwisa** economically inevitable rather than merely a product of conquest. Hachina holds ~261,000 km² of plain at 260–430 m with good water. Their upland neighbours sit 250 m higher, on rougher ground, with 70–92% of their land above 450 m and substantially more rain (3,187 mm and 2,294 mm) — wetter, steeper, and harder to farm. **The uplands cannot feed a state; the plain can feed several.**

## 7. First contact with the Zong

### Correction: Xinhai is not in Sinchew
An earlier draft connected Hachina to the failure of **Xinhai (1456)**, the first Zong colony, on the grounds that it was founded on the southern coast and defeated by "a large and well-organised indigenous population." **That connection is wrong.**

Xinhai's location is **(lon 150.6°W, lat 8.1°S)** — roughly **3,400 km west of Sinchew's westernmost point**, deep inside the territory of **Louyang**. It is not a Sinchewese settlement and its indigenous opponents were not Hachina. Whatever people destroyed Xinhai, it was a Louyangese one, and that is a Louyang matter.

What survives is a useful framing: Hachina lies **between the Louyang colonial sphere and the eventual Sinchewese one** — the first organised indigenous state east of the Louyang colonies, and the first thing the Zong met on the way out.

| colony | founded | longitude |
|---|---|---|
| Xinhai | 1456 | 150.6°W — far western Louyang |
| Anping | 1474 | Louyang; seat of the Zong overseas state by 1480 |
| Tamsui | 1538 | 111.6°W — western Sinchew coast |

### Naming: two empires
The most revealing detail of first contact is what each side called the other.

**The Hachina called the Zong "the western empire."** They knew themselves as **the central empire.** So the relationship was framed from the outset in imperial terms: two great states, one central, one western, meeting at a distance.

**This is the strongest single piece of evidence that Hachina was a genuine state.** A people without a concept of themselves as a polity does not classify a foreign arrival as a peer empire on first sight. The naming implies a self-conception, a court, and a diplomatic vocabulary already in place.

### Why they did not see the Zong as gods
Most indigenous peoples who met the Zong took them for something supernatural. **Hachina did not.** The reason is precise and rather remarkable:

**They saw captured native people aboard the Zong ships — and those captives spoke languages recognisably similar to their own.**

So the strangers were not beings from another order. They were **people who had conquered other people like themselves, and who came from the sea.** The Hachina classified them accordingly: **equals, but of the sea, against the Hachina's own might, which was of the land.** Not gods. Rivals.

That is an extraordinary piece of first-contact reasoning — the Hachina worked out what the Zong were from evidence, and got it essentially right.

### The meeting itself
- Communication was **minimal** — the explorers could barely make themselves understood.
- The Hachina appeared **peaceful and amazed at the ships**, and **gave offerings**.
- The encounters were, from the Zong side, incidental: **ships passing by, dropping off goods for trade.**

### The alliance that only one side believed in

This is the tragedy of the relationship, and it runs for roughly forty years.

- **The Hachina believed they were in an alliance.**
- **The Zong paid little mind.** Internally they did not see Hachina as a threat, and did not want to conquer them — **Hachina was simply too far away to be worth it.**

For the Zong, Hachina was a convenient anchorage and a source of provisions on a long coasting route. For Hachina, the Zong were a distant great power with whom they had an understanding against mutual enemies. **Neither was lying; they simply meant different things by the same words.** The alliance did not materially amount to anything **until the 1500s**.

## 8. The change of mind — and the Tamsui Plan

### Intelligence from the coast

Through the later 1400s, Zong explorers coasting the shoreline stopped at Hachina — safely, because Hachina did not attack them the way other peoples had. What they learned there changed Zong strategy:

**Hachina was at war with various Kawesa peoples over access to gold in the hills around the large linear lake.**

That intelligence went back to **Anping**, which by **1480** had become the seat of the Zong overseas state and controlled most of the **western seaboard of Louyang**.

The geography bears this out: the long lake is ringed by high ground (median 662 m, rising to 1,401 m), so **gold in those hills is plausible**, and the lake lies ~327 km north of Hachina's frontier, across Kawesa country. Hachina fighting through Kawesa for access to it is a coherent motive for a long war.

### The Tamsui Plan (1535)

The Zong colonial government — now thinking in terms of **extractive colonies on the coast** rather than waystations — decided it wanted **the Tamsui area for its own**. The plan was drawn up in **1535**.

**The strategic reasoning was twofold and quite sophisticated:**

1. **Kawesa was recognised as dangerous.** The Zong knew the Kawesa were militant and that Tamsui's port **would be hard-fought to hold.**
2. **But Kawesa was also divided.** The clans **fought each other constantly**, which made them exploitable.

**The method: ally with the clan holding the modern Tamsui site.** The Zong traded **land in exchange for weapons** — arming one Kawesa clan against the others, and taking the port.

**Simultaneously, the Zong saw Hachina as a useful ally for conquering the coast.**

### The bypass — why Tamsui mattered commercially

The commercial logic was the point of the whole plan:

**With Tamsui and its hinterland, Zong ships would no longer need to put in at Hachina to reload.** They could **cross the Gulf at its mouth and run directly to Tamsui.**

So Hachina's value to the Zong had been as a **mandatory stopover** on the coasting route — a place to take on supplies before the next leg. Tamsui removed that dependency. **The Zong's objective was, in part, to stop needing Hachina at all.**

**Worth noting the distance:** Tamsui lies **567 km from Hachina's nearest point**. So this is not a local shortcut but a long-distance route restructuring — eliminating a resupply stop by establishing a depot closer to the destination.

### Hachina's reaction — they approved

User, 2026-09-21: *"I think hachina did not really care that the zong were to establish a colony in kawesa land, probably liked it because it would disrupt the kawesa so they could attack the southern kawesa land."*

**Hachina did not protest the founding of Tamsui. They welcomed it.**

And the geography shows exactly why:

| | |
|---|---|
| Kawesa extent | lat 2.3°S – 9.1°S (411,952 km²) |
| Hachina extent | lat 6.6°S – 11.0°S (326,792 km²) |
| **Their frontier** | lat 6.6°S – 8.9°S, lon 108.6°W – 106.1°W |
| **Tamsui** | **2.5° north of that frontier** — at Kawesa's far northern end |
| Kawesa land adjacent to Hachina | ~44,000 km² — about 11% of Kawesa |

**Tamsui sits at Kawesa's northern extremity. Hachina borders Kawesa's southern portion.** A Zong colony in the north fixes Kawesa's attention and forces it to fight on two fronts at once — so Hachina can take the southern Kawesa territory that adjoins them while Kawesa is occupied elsewhere.

**This is a pincer, and Hachina recognised it immediately.**

### What this reveals about Hachina
This changes the reading of the whole relationship, and for the better.

Hachina was **not naive and was not simply deceived.** They had their own agenda from the start, and they saw the Zong as **an instrument** — a power to be pointed at their enemy. The Tamsui colony was, from Hachina's perspective, a way to pin Kawesa in place while Hachina took what it wanted in the south.

**The irony is that both sides thought they were using the other.** The Zong saw Hachina as a useful ally for conquering the coast and a stopover to be bypassed. Hachina saw the Zong as a foreign lever against Kawesa. Each was correct about the other's usefulness and wrong about who was in control.

**This also explains why Hachina made no objection in 1538** — the founding of Tamsui was, to them, the plan working.

### What this makes of Hachina's position

Hachina spent roughly eighty years believing it had a great-power ally. During that time the Zong:

- learned from Hachina's own hospitality that gold lay in the lake hills, and that Kawesa was both militant and divided
- used that intelligence to plan a colony on Kawesa land
- armed Kawesa factions against each other to obtain it
- and designed the new port specifically **to make Hachina unnecessary**

**The alliance Hachina sought was never refused — it was simply never honoured, and was quietly converted into a plan to bypass them.** Whether Hachina understood this before 1538, and what they did when they did, is the critical question of the colonial period.

## 9. The gold, and the firearms trade (user, 2026-09-21)

### Hachina's gold
User: *"they also did have gold they were able to tax from the uwisa and yelani who they were probably subjegating by the 1400s."*

**Taxed gold from subjugated neighbours.** The geography supports this directly:

| | |
|---|---|
| Hachina–Yelani frontier | median elevation **1,055 m**, p90 1,340 m, max 1,492 m |
| Yelani highlands overall | median 1,515 m, p95 2,300 m, **max 2,697 m** — highest in Sinchew |
| Highest point | (−96.31, −11.02) |

The frontier with Yelani is **mountainous country**, and the Yelani hold the highest ground in the country. Gold in those highlands is entirely plausible — alluvial or vein gold in mountain country is the classic setting.

**Note the shift in which neighbours are tributary.** An earlier note had **Nahola and Uwisa** as the subservient uplands. The user now specifies **Uwisa and Yelani** as the gold sources by the 1400s. Both are consistent — Hachina borders all three (Uwisa 1,906 boundary cells, Nahola 1,509, Yelani 929) — but the gold-bearing ones are **Uwisa and Yelani**. Whether Nahola is also tributary, and how the three rank against each other, is open.

### The arms trade — an unauthorised colonial racket
User: *"they were probably subjegating by the 1400s for the early firearms and cannons the zong explorers were willing to part way with for exorbinant costs, the zong colonial government actually did not authorize this, but the zong explorers knew they could make a lot of money by selling firearms for huge sums and just reporting them as lost or destroyed, there was a burgening arms manufacturing industry in colonial louyang that was essentially being funded by hachina gold."*

This is a superb detail, and it works on every level:

- **The arms were sold without authorisation.** The Zong colonial government did not sanction firearms sales to indigenous powers. Individual explorers and traders did it for personal profit.
- **The cover story was routine fraud** — weapons reported as **"lost" or "destroyed"** in transit or in the field.
- **The money went back to Louyang**, where it **funded a burgeoning arms manufacturing industry.** So an indigenous gold supply was capitalising colonial heavy manufacture.
- **The buyers paid exorbitant prices** — because gold is abundant to them and firearms are irreplaceable.

### What the trade did to Hachina
**This is the mechanism by which the Zong arrival centralised the Hachina state.**

Before contact, Hachina had gold but nothing to spend it on that would change the military balance. After contact it had a market, and the gold bought the one commodity that transforms a state's power over its neighbours: **firearms and cannon.**

**The cycle:** gold → weapons → conquest and enforcement → more tributary gold → more weapons.

A state that can sell its gold for gunpowder can suddenly do things no amount of grain surplus permits — take fortified upland positions, hold restive tributaries in place, and outfight neighbours who have neither guns nor gold. **Contact with the Zong made Hachina militarily dominant in a way that internal development alone would not have.**

### And it fed the wider war
Hachina also spent gold fighting **Kawesa** for access to the lake hills. So Hachina gold simultaneously:

1. armed Hachina against Kawesa and its own tributaries,
2. funded an arms industry in colonial Louyang, and
3. enriched individual Zong traders at the expense of their own government's stated policy.

**The Zong colonial government was, in effect, being defrauded by its own citizens in a trade that armed the most powerful indigenous state on the coast.** When the Tamsui Plan was drawn up in 1535, the Zong were planning to move against a Kawesa that Hachina had spent decades weakening with weapons bought from Zong officials' own subordinates.

### A note on the modern echo
Hachina is remembered as "the central empire" — a self-conception. If the state's real ascendancy dates from the contact period, then the imperial idea and the firearms trade arrive together. **Hachina became an empire partly because the Zong came.**

## 10. Name and capital — CONFIRMED (2026-09-21)

### The empire — **Hachinawa**
**CONFIRMED by the user, 2026-09-21.**

**Hachinawa** — *hachin* "centre, middle" + *awa* "realm" — "the middle realm."

The people-name **Hachina** derives from the same root: "the central people." So the ethnonym, the state and the self-conception all rest on one word. When the Hachina called themselves "the central empire," they were saying what their own name means — which is why they classified the Zong as a counterpart rather than a wonder.

Discarded alternatives: Achinawa (tighter variant); the empire sharing the people's name outright (the "Rome" / "Inca" pattern).

### The capital — **Hachintala**
**CONFIRMED by the user, 2026-09-21.**

**Hachintala** — *hachin* "centre" + *tala* "city, seat" — "the seat of the centre."

Discarded alternatives: Nachihawa ("heartland"), Kawchina.

### Where should it sit?
**Pattern: inland seat with a separate port.** *(recommended, not yet finally confirmed)*

Two defensible patterns were weighed:

**A. Coastal port capital.** At a river mouth on the Gulf of Louyang, where the gold-for-arms trade actually landed. Trade-funded states often keep the seat of power at the port.

**B. Inland capital with a separate port.** The state's centre of gravity on the plain — controlling grain, irrigation and the tributary networks — with a downstream port handling the Zong trade.

**Recommended: B.** A state whose wealth depends on foreigners docking at its capital is a state that can be decapitated by a fleet. An inland capital on a river keeps the agrarian and military core out of naval reach while the port handles commerce.

**A candidate site has been identified by terrain analysis:** a well-watered point at **(105.1°W, 9.9°S), elevation 320 m** — dead centre of the plain, on a river, with the densest river network in the territory nearby. An alternative sits at (105.4°W, 8.7°S), elevation 321 m. Both are in the 260–430 m plateau band and central to the arable core.

## 11. Open questions

### The empire and its name
1. ~~Confirm the empire name.~~ **CONFIRMED: Hachinawa** ("the middle realm"), people as Hachina.
2. ~~Confirm the capital name.~~ **CONFIRMED: Hachintala.** Still open: **confirm the site** — recommended inland, on a river, at **(105.1°W, 9.9°S), elevation 320 m**, with a separate port on the Gulf coast.
3. **When did the state form**, and how long before Zong contact (1458)?
4. **How centralised, really?** A tribute empire with client kings is a very different thing from a bureaucratic state with provinces and appointed governors.
5. **Is there a script?** If so, "Hachina" may be an endonym recorded in their own writing.
6. **Monumental architecture** — temples, walls, granaries, tombs? This is the visible signature of a grain state.
7. **Is the state theocratic** — a priest-king or divine ruler — or secular?
8. **What is the king's title**, and is the empire named for it (as "Inca" is named for the ruler)?

### The tributaries

10. **How was submission obtained** — conquest, threat, or economic dependence?
11. **What is extracted** — gold (confirmed), plus grain, labour, troops, raw materials?
12. **Do tributaries keep their own rulers** as client kings, or are Hachina governors installed?
13. **How much resistance**, and does the relationship fluctuate over centuries?
14. **Do Uwisa, Yelani and Nahola rank differently**, or are they equal tributaries?

### Gold, arms and the colonial racket
15. **The gold**: alluvial or vein? Mined by Hachina, by tributaries, or by both?
16. **How much of it left the country**, and did the trade drain Hachina's reserves over time?
17. **Did the Zong government ever discover the racket?** If so, when, and what happened?
18. **Does the arms industry in colonial Louyang appear elsewhere** in the lore — is it a named enterprise or a general sector?
19. **Was the same fraud used to arm the Kawesa clans** under the 1535 Tamsui Plan, or was that sanctioned trade?
20. **Did other indigenous powers buy firearms** through the same channel — Kawesa, Timara, Uwisa?

### Crops and economy
21. **Is rice the staple**, with sorghum and millet as dry-year insurance — or the reverse? This decides how central irrigation is.
22. **Did they build irrigation works**, and were those state-built and state-controlled? Canal systems are the classic foundation of a tributary bureaucracy.
23. **Is grain a state monopoly**, taxed in kind and stored in state granaries?
24. **Do they have draft animals** — cattle, water buffalo — and a plough? Plough agriculture implies deeper stratification than hoe farming.
25. **What else do they export** besides gold — grain, cotton, sesame, manufactures?

### First contact and the Tamsui Plan
26. **Did Hachina ever learn the Tamsui Plan aimed partly at bypassing them** — and when, and did it change anything?
27. **Did they actually take southern Kawesa land**, and how much? Does the gain show on the map, or was it temporary?
28. **How long did Hachina's alliance-illusion and the Zong's indifference coexist** before either side revised its reading?
29. **Was "the western empire" name long-lived** in Hachina usage, or did it lapse once the relationship soured?
30. **Who were the captured peoples aboard the Zong ships** whose language the Hachina recognised? Louyangese, most likely — which implies something about language relationships across the two countries.
31. **Was "the central empire" a formal title of the king**, or a general self-description?
32. **Did the 1458 voyage come from Xinhai?** Roughly 4,000 km.
33. **Were the Hachina the ones who introduced the Zong to the rest of Sinchew** — as intermediaries and guides?

### The colonial period ahead

35. **Is the Hachina state remembered in modern Sinchew** as a pre-colonial golden age?
36. **Did Hachina come to regret the alliance** — is it remembered as betrayal or miscalculation?
37. **Did the Zong treat Hachina as a sovereign state** throughout, or did the relationship sour?
