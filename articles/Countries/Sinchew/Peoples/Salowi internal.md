## 1. The land they live in — measured

The territory is Sinchew's **westernmost lobe**, wrapped between the open **Shendan Ocean** to the north and the enclosed **Gulf of Louyang** to the south.

| measure | value |
|---|---|
| Area | 114,639 km² |
| Extent | lat 3.8°S–6.8°S (≈333 km), lon 119.4°W–113.3°W (≈680 km) |
| Elevation | min 2 m, p25 **6 m**, **median 44 m**, p75 162 m, p95 298 m, max **1,632 m** |
| Land above 200 m | 13.2% |
| Land above 400 m | 1.3% |
| Köppen | **Am 59.5%, Aw 38.4%**, Af 1.7% |
| Rainfall | p10 **1,470 mm**, median 2,563 mm, p90 **4,977 mm** |
| Temperature | median 24.5 °C |
| **Coastline** | **≈2,001 km — longest of any Sinchew family** |
| Direction the sea lies | **north, west, south and east** — see below: Shendan to the north, Gulf of Louyang to the south |
| Lakes | **zero** |
| River density | 0.0066 km/km² — third highest of the nine |

### The defining combination
Three things set Salowi apart from every other family, and especially from their neighbours the Karawã–Apyãwa.

**First, the coastline.** 2,001 km on a territory of 114,639 km². The coast-to-area ratio is 0.0175 km/km², roughly fifty per cent more crenulated than the Karawã–Apyãwa's 0.012. The sea lies in all four directions, which means the territory is not a coastal strip but a **lobe wrapped by water** — a peninsula or a cluster of peninsulas with deep bays between them.

**Second, seasonality.** Köppen Am and Aw dominate; Af is negligible. Unlike the Karawã–Apyãwa, who live under essentially unbroken rain, the Salowi have a **real dry season**. Rainfall still varies hugely — 1,470 mm at the dry end to 4,977 mm at the wet — so some part of the territory is markedly drier than the rest. This is a monsoon-and-savanna coast, not a rainforest one.

**Third, flatness.** Median elevation 44 m; a quarter of the land is below 6 m. This is mangrove-grade terrain. The single exception is a mountain reaching 1,632 m, which stands out sharply against everything around it and would be a landmark of obvious significance.

### Real-world analogue
**Southwest Florida** — the Calusa homeland, the Ten Thousand Islands, and the Everglades' gulf coast. A low, drowned, estuary-riddled limestone coast where the land is poor and the water is rich.

## 2. What that implies

Offered as a starting point, to be confirmed or rejected.

### Subsistence — maritime, and probably non-agricultural
This is the central inference, and the environment supports it strongly.

The land is **flat and low**, much of it below 6 m, which means poor drainage and salt intrusion near the coast. It is not good farmland, and the seasonal rainfall would make rain-fed cultivation risky at the dry end of the range. But the coast is **long, intricate and shallow-shelved** — precisely the configuration that produces abundant estuarine life: fish, shellfish, crustaceans, turtles, waterfowl.

So the likely pattern is a **marine and estuarine economy**, with fishing and shellfish gathering as the staple and the land used for hunting and gathering rather than farming. This is exactly the Calusa pattern and it is a genuine anomaly worth keeping: **a dense, complex society with no agriculture**.

### Shell as the monumental material
A rainforested country has timber; a mountainous one has stone. This coast has neither, but it has **shell in extraordinary quantity** — the by-product of a shellfish-eating economy accumulated over generations. Shell mounds are therefore the natural monumental form, and they arise incidentally before they arise deliberately.

This is worth developing as the Salowi visual signature: **mounds, causeways and platform works built of shell**, not stone.

### Water, and its absence inland
The territory has **no lakes at all** and moderate rivers. So fresh water is a practical problem. On a low, flat, salt-penetrated coast, drinking water would come from small streams, springs and shallow wells — and its availability would constrain where settlements can be.

This may be the single most important practical fact about the territory. A maritime people with rich fishing grounds still needs fresh water, and on this coast that is not automatic.

### Settlement and the sea as the road
As with the Karawã–Apyãwa, the interior offers no navigable route — but the Salowi coast is far longer and far better sheltered. **Canoe travel along this coast would be easy and would connect the whole territory**, which makes the sea their highway even more decisively than it is for the Karawã–Apyãwa.

Sheltered bays also mean calm water and safe anchorages, which points to larger watercraft than a surf-beaten coast would allow.

### Two seas, not one — a correction

**Corrected 2026-09-19 (user):** *"isnt north the ocean and south the gulf for the salowi?"* — yes. My first draft wrongly described them as facing the gulf only.

The Salowi face **two different marine environments**:

| direction | water | character |
|---|---|---|
| **North** | **Shendan Ocean** | open ocean, deep, exposed, swell |
| **South** | **Gulf of Louyang** | enclosed, sheltered, shallow, estuarine |

This is a considerably richer setup than a single-water orientation, and it may explain the extraordinary 2,001 km of coastline: **they occupy a lobe wrapped between two seas**, with the gulf on one side and the open ocean on the other.

It implies two distinct marine economies rather than one:

- **Gulf side (south)** — mangrove and estuarine shellfish, calm canoe water, the classic Calusa pattern. This is where the sheltered wealth is.
- **Ocean side (north)** — open-water fishing, larger swell, less shelter. Different craft, different species, different risk.

The Gulf is closed on its far side by the landmass of Louyang, so the gulf-facing coast looks *across* enclosed water; the ocean-facing coast looks *out*.

### Settled (user, 2026-09-19)

**One people, not two.** The two coasts are not two peoples: the Salowi are a single people sharing a language and identity. *"I think its the same people, though I think there was many disparate villages."*

**Population concentrated on the gulf side.** *"I think the gulf side was where more people lived since it was more calm."* The reasoning is straightforward and sound: the Gulf of Louyang is sheltered, so its water is calmer, its shellfish beds richer, its anchorages safer and its canoe travel easier. The Shendan coast, by contrast, is open ocean with real swell.

The consequence is a people whose **centre of gravity is the southern, gulf-facing shore**, with the northern ocean coast held as a secondary, more sparsely used margin — probably for seasonal and special-purpose activity rather than permanent settlement.

**Many disparate villages.** The Salowi are politically **fragmented**: numerous separate villages rather than a single polity. Whether they had any overarching structure above the village — a council, a paramount office, ceremonial confederacy — is undetermined (see section 4).

### What this means for the shell mounds
If villages are autonomous and no paramount authority exists, then **shell mounds are better read as communal and ceremonial works than as chiefly monuments** — the product of many villages gathering, or of a village's own accumulated ritual practice, rather than the display of a ruling lineage. That is a meaningfully different tradition from the mound-building Kawesa on the plateau, whose earthworks are associated with an agricultural society.

### Where the ocean coast fits
Not a homeland but a resource margin. Plausible uses: seasonal fishing camps, turtle and marine-mammal hunting, shell gathering, and access to open-water species unavailable in the gulf. It may also be where specialist parties went — and where reputations were made, given the greater risk.

Whether they had contact with Louyang across the gulf remains an open question with obvious significance (see section 4).

## 3. Cultural character — what to develop

The Calusa inspiration gives a strong and unusual template. The following are **proposals** keyed to the environment.

### The non-agricultural complex
The most valuable thing about the Calusa model is that it **breaks the standard assumption** that complexity requires farming. A society here could be dense, stratified and monumental while living entirely off the water — which makes the Salowi structurally unlike every inland family in Sinchew, and unlike the mound-building Kawesa on the plateau.

Worth deciding deliberately: **non-agricultural, or minimally agricultural?** The stronger version — no farming at all — is more distinctive and better supported by the terrain.

### Seasonality as a structuring rhythm
With a real dry season, the year would have two phases. Dry-season concentration of fish in shrinking water bodies is a classic fishing windfall; wet-season flooding spreads them out again. So the annual cycle is likely built around the dry season, with the wet season a time of stored food, repair and inland work.

This is a good structural difference from the Karawã–Apyãwa, whose year is undifferentiated rain.

### Chiefs, and how authority works without land
If there is no farmland, then wealth and status cannot rest on land tenure. It would rest instead on **fishing grounds, shell, canoe access, and the control of labour** — and on the redistribution of a perishable surplus, which is a very different basis for power than owning fields.

The Calusa had an inherited chiefly elite with a paramount chief. A comparable structure here would need its legitimacy to come from somewhere else: ritual authority, control of the sea, or kinship. **This is the most interesting open question about Salowi society.**

### The mountain
One mountain rises to 1,632 m out of a territory whose median elevation is 44 m. A landmark of that contrast would almost certainly carry religious or ceremonial significance — and it is the only high ground they have.

## 4. Open questions

### Subsistence
1. **Agriculture: none at all, or minimal?** The strong version (none) fits the terrain best and is the most distinctive choice available.
2. **What does the marine economy actually consist of** — fish, shellfish, turtles, waterfowl, marine mammals? Anything beyond that?
3. **Is there shellfish in quantity?** Shell mounds depend on it, and so does the monumental tradition. Worth deciding explicitly.
4. **Fresh water** — where does it come from on a flat, salt-exposed coast, and does it constrain settlement?

### Society
5. **How is authority organised, and legitimised** without land as the basis of wealth? Settled that villages are numerous and disparate; undetermined is whether anything exists *above* the village — a council of elders, a paramount office, a ceremonial confederacy, or nothing at all. This is the key remaining political question.
6. **Monumental architecture** — shell mounds and platforms, yes? Causeways between keys? How far does it go?
7. **How dense** is the population? Maritime economies can support surprising density — does Salowi?
8. **Canoes and watercraft** — how large, given sheltered water?

### Relations
9. **Do they have contact with Louyang across the Gulf of Louyang?** The gulf is closed on the far side by Louyang's landmass, so this is geographically plausible and would matter a great deal.
10. **Relationship with the Timara to the north and Chidoha to the south** — trade, rivalry, or isolation?
11. **Are the Salowi a single people** or several communities sharing a language? Their territory is large and peninsular, which often means fragmentation.
12. ~~Do the two seas produce two peoples?~~ **RESOLVED: one people.** ~~Which coast holds the population?~~ **RESOLVED: the gulf side, being calmer.** Still open: **what is the ocean coast actually used for** — seasonal camps, specialist hunting, or a genuinely separate sphere of activity?
13. **Do the villages have names, and how many are there?** "Many disparate villages" needs a figure for the pre-colonial period.
14. **Do the villages compete?** Autonomous villages in a rich environment could be rivals, or linked by marriage and ceremony. Which?

### The name and the language
12. **What does "Salowi" mean** — self-designation or exonym? A single-language family needs a name for itself.
13. **Vocabulary** — for a maritime people the core words would be sea, tide, estuary, shell, fish, canoe, island, and the dry and wet seasons. These are the terms that will generate place names along this coast.

## 5. Sequencing note

Culture first, history after — as with the Karawã–Apyãwa. The two coastal families make a useful pair: both maritime, both lake-less, both oriented to canoe travel, and both facing colonial pressure from the sea — but differentiated by **rainforest versus monsoon-savanna**, **open ocean versus gulf**, and **one mountain versus many**. Working them together may sharpen both.
