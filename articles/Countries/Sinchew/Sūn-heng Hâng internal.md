## 2. The scale of it — 500 years

**Founded before 1474 and dissolved in 1993.** The house existed for something like five centuries, through:
- the Zong colonial period and the founding of Anping
- the Oktalwa grant, Okheng, and the sale of 1535
- the [[Tamsui Plot]] and the colony's division (1543)
- the [[Great Disappointment]] (1564) and the whole early colonial era
- the [[First Mahik War]] and the destruction of the Kawesa chiefdoms
- the Baiduist expulsion and influx (1606 onward)
- Sinchew's revolutionary and modern eras

**It is the longest-lived thing in the entire scenario**, and it outlasted every institution built around it: the Akwesa chiefdom, Okheng, Colonial Zong, the Sinchew Colony, and the colonial administration itself.

**The house the family built outlived the country they sold.** That is the fact worth protecting.

## 3. New points this creates
- **[[Chiu Khéng-gio̍k]] ran the company for 55 years** (1543–1598), the same span he served as ambassador. **So his two longest-held positions were the ambassadorship and the family firm** — while the two he lost (miko, king of Okheng) were the ones with substance. Worth adding to his article.
- **The family's wealth after 1535 came from the house, not the gold.** The 4,000 taels were a one-off; the company was the inheritance. It explains how the Chiu family remained notable for centuries.
- **A modern Sinchewese thread is now available**: the company survived into living memory, so there are people whose family firm was bought by a state enterprise in 1993 — and whose ancestor ran a private kingdom in the 16th century.

**What "proper Zong" meant, and how I read it:** the user said *"Liánhé Huàxué Jítuán should be renamed to be proper zong too."* My reading, which the user accepted: **a name coined in Zong, not a translation of a foreign-style name.** 聯合化學集團 was simply "United Chemical Group" rendered in characters — nobody founds a company by translating an English name.

**How *Chóng-hòa Chi̍p-thoân* fixes that:** it is **abbreviated from a longer formal title** (總 + 化工 = "Zong Chemical Works"), which is the actual pattern of Chinese industrial conglomerates — nobody says 中國石油化工集團 in ordinary use, they say 中石化. It is short, reads as a state-scale enterprise, and pairs legibly against the family firm: **Chóng Sok-ka Kong-si bought by Chóng-hòa Chi̍p-thoân.**

**A petrochemicals trading house bought by a chemicals manufacturer.** Not an arbitrary sale — the buyer is the natural one.

## The settlement campaign (2026-09-21)
User: *"actually first we should bring up in the History of Sinchew (1474-1538) the population numbers, the advertising push by Sūn-heng Hâng to bring people to Okheng, basically selling plots of land to landowners in zong, then also providing shipment of serfs from the landowners land in zong to sinchew, the company acted as a management agency, this helped raise funds for okheng."*

**Written into BOTH `History of Sinchew (1474-1538)` (new "The settlement of Okheng" section + a population table) and `Sūn-heng Hâng`.**

### What the scheme was
1. **Advertising campaign in Zong** from the 1520s
2. **Plots sold to Zong landowners** who remained on the mainland
3. **Company ships the labour** — serfs from the purchasers' own Zong estates to the territory
4. **Company acts as managing agent** for holdings whose owners might never see them
5. **Proceeds fund Okheng** — depot, works and wars paid for out of the settlement campaign

**This changes what the company was.** It was not merely a trading house at a bay; it was a **colonial development company** running a land-and-labour scheme — selling land in one country, moving people to it from another, and administering the result. That is precisely the model of the Virginia Company or a chartered colonial venture.

### Population table added
| year | Akwesa | Zong | total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1511 (grant) | c. 12,000 | c. 60 | c. 12,000 |
| 1521 (marriage) | c. 11,000 | c. 200 | c. 11,000 |
| 1527 (epidemic) | c. 6,000 | c. 400 | c. 6,400 |
| 1535 (purchase) | c. 5,000 | c. 1,000 | c. 6,000 |

**The critical observation the table makes:** the settlement campaign could **not offset the plague losses**. The Zong population grew from 60 to about 1,000 over 24 years, while the Akwesa fell from 12,000 to about 5,000. **The territory was emptied faster than it was filled**, and the immigrants were a small fraction of those who died. Worth protecting — it is the demographic fact that explains everything else.

### CONNECTION WORTH DEVELOPING — the Edict of 1500
**The [[Zong Edict of 1500]] banned slavery on the Zong *mainland* but was deliberately ambiguous for the colonies** ("a legal grey area in which slavery was banned on the mainland but tolerated, or at least not actively prohibited, in the overseas colonies"). Shipping bound labour out of Zong estates to Okheng sits **exactly in that gap** — the labour left the mainland, where the ban applied, and arrived in a colony, where it did not. **This is very likely how the scheme worked, and it prefigures the [[Secret Trade]] of the later 16th century.** Not yet cross-referenced in either article; worth doing.

## The transported labour was of MIXED status (2026-09-21)
User: *"I think it was a mix of slaves and serfs owned by landlowners, whoever they wanted to ship over for thsi scheme."*

**Correction applied.** I had written it as serfs only. The estates of Zong held **both serfs (bound to the land) and slaves (held as property)**, and **the landowners chose whom to send**. The company's part was **carriage and management, not ownership** — "it did not enquire into the condition of those it carried."

### The Edict of 1500 loophole is now PRECISE, not vague
I had described the edict's colonial application as "a legal grey area". **Reading its actual terms, it is sharper than that:**

| the Edict of 1500 prohibited | it did not address |
|---|---|
| the **enslavement of any person within the territory of the Kingdom of Zong** | the **movement of the unfree OUT of the kingdom** |
| the **transport of enslaved persons INTO the kingdom** | — |

**So bound labour leaving a Zong mainland estate for an overseas territory fell outside the prohibition entirely** — not by ambiguity, but by the edict's silence. The outbound traffic was never covered.

**This is the legal mechanism the whole scheme rested on**, and it is now written into the History of Sinchew article.

### And it makes the scheme a precursor of the Secret Trade
The article now says the traffic of this period **"is regarded as an early form of the [[Secret Trade]]"** — the later system by which enslaved peoples from [[Dagit]] and the [[Balausia Peninsula]] were carried to the Sinchew plantations. So the company's 1520s settlement scheme is the ancestor of the later slave traffic, using the same legal gap.

**Worth protecting:** the scheme was legal, the landowners chose, and the company carried — three parties each able to say the condition of the transported was not their concern.
