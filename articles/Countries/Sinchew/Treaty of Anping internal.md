**The key point:** the established Zong doctrine of "military demonstration and diplomatic accommodation" is *exactly* the policy the Treaty of Anping embodies. The treaty is not a departure — it is the doctrine applied to the largest polity in the region.

**And the timing is tight and useful:** in 1538 there was **one** colonial administration, based at Anping, and Tamsui was part of the Louyang colony. The treaty therefore predates the split by five years. So the Zong party to the treaty is the **single colonial government at Anping** — not "the Sinchew colony," which did not yet exist.

## 3. "Needed an island" — UNRESOLVED

The user's phrase is: *"the colonial government realized it would not be possible to rule the inland, as it was still fighting wars inland in louyang and needed an island."*

**I checked for islands. There are none near Tamsui.** Sinchew's land is 99.99% one contiguous mass; the only three separate components are **8.6, 12.3 and 12.3 km²**, all in the far north-east around (−92.8, −2.3) — roughly **2,000 km from Tamsui**.

**Possible readings, none confirmed:**
1. **Metaphorical** — the Zong needed a *secure base* rather than territory; "island" as a figure for an isolated defensible position.
2. **A specific island elsewhere** — perhaps near Anping in Louyang. I have no Louyang DEM, so cannot check.
3. **Confused with the coastal-enclave doctrine** — i.e. the Zong resolved to hold *coastal stations* only, which are conceptually "islands" of Zong authority in an indigenous sea. **This is the reading I adopted in the article**, since it matches the established doctrine.
4. **A different location entirely** — an island the Zong wanted for a harbour or a penal station.

**Action: ask the user.** The article currently uses reading 3 implicitly without asserting any island.

## 4. What the article argues

**Structure:** infobox → lead → Background (overextension / failure of inland rule / the Hahkina) → The Hahkina embassy → Terms (Recognition / Limits / Trade) → Significance → Aftermath → See also.

**The central argument:** the treaty is the application of established Zong doctrine to the largest polity in the region. The colony conceded nothing it could hold — recognition of the Hahkina cost it no territory it was capable of governing — and in exchange gained a harbour on sustainable terms. Both sides got what they wanted, and both understood the arrangement differently.

**The three-part terms** are my construction, and worth checking against the user's intent:
- **Recognition** — Hahkina sovereignty over the interior; no Zong inland claim asserted
- **Limits** — Zong fixed at coastal stations (principally Tamsui); mutual non-aggression
- **Trade** — formal terms, bringing the previously unauthorised gold-and-arms commerce under colonial supervision

**The trade clause is the interesting one.** It is where the treaty's stated intent and the colony's actual practice diverge — the smugglers were to be regularised, but the arms continued to flow. The article notes this in Aftermath.

## 6. Open questions

1. **What does "needed an island" mean?** See section 3. The one genuinely unresolved element of the brief.
2. **Who signed for the Zong?** A colonial governor of the single pre-split administration. Name and office undetermined.
3. **Who was the king's son**, and did he become king? If he later reigned, the embassy reads as an apprenticeship in statecraft — which is a good story.
4. **Was the treaty ratified in Zhongyuan**, or concluded by the colonial government on its own authority? A colonial administration signing a recognition treaty with a foreign state is a significant act; the metropolitan court's response matters.
5. **Could the Zong government have repudiated it?** If Anping acted beyond its authority, and the court later objected, that is a live thread.
6. **What did the Hahkina believe they had signed?** They had spent eighty years believing they had an alliance the Zong did not acknowledge. Did they read this treaty as the alliance finally formalised? If so, the 1538 document means something different on each side — and that is a repeat of the original misunderstanding at a higher level.
7. **How long did the treaty hold?** The indigenous wars continued through the century. When was it first broken, and by whom?
8. **Does the treaty survive in modern Sinchewese memory** as a founding document, or is it a colonial artefact?
9. **Where is the Hook of Tianhe?** Named as where the Qingjiang empties into the sea, and therefore a location on the 1543 boundary. Not yet placed on the map.
10. **Does the treaty mention the Kawesa** — the Hahkina's enemy and the power whose land Tamsui occupied? If the Zong took Tamsui on Kawesa land with Hahkina approval, the treaty is where that arrangement would be recorded.

## The boundary was fixed by a posthumous title (2026-09-21)
User: *"this fiction was created sometime in the Treaty of Anping, because they needed to define the tamsui/sinchew borders with the hachina, they had said the land ceeded by the 'king' of the akwesa was to be the zong, and shared with the hachina."*

**The Zong–Hahkina boundary of 1538 follows the extent of what the Zong claimed to have acquired from Hokta as "King of the Northern Mahiks."** Hokta was the *miko* of the Akwesa and nothing more; the royal title was conferred posthumously during these negotiations, and its scope determined how much coast the Zong could claim.

**The Hahkina took what lay beyond the claimed lands** — not as territory they had won, but as land shared out of the colony's own. That framing is worth preserving: it flatters the Zong and conceals that they had settled one harbour and fought for none of the rest.
