## 1. Who he is

**周慶玉 — *Khèng-gio̍k*, "auspicious jade". Kawesa name: Kengohka** (user's choice, 2026-09-21, from the options offered).

Born about **1505** in [[Anping]]. Son of **[[Chiu Bûn-hái]]**, the merchant who founded the [[Sūn-heng Hâng]] and wanted a kingdom of his own.

### The career — four offices, two of them nominal
| office | period | age | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Miko of Akwesa** | 1527 – his death | ~22 | **kept for life, after the chiefdom ceased to exist** |
| **King of Okheng** | 1527 – 1535 | ~22–30 | ruler of the merged territory |
| **Provisional administrator** | 1535 – 1543 | ~30–38 | appointed by the colony that bought it |
| **Ambassador to the Akwesa** | 1543 – his death | ~38– | appointed by the colony that removed him |

**He held the miko title after the chiefdom had ended, and the last two offices were both conferred by the power that dispossessed him.**

### The chain he is the hinge of
- **1521** married in at ~17, as an alliance pawn arranged between his father and Hokta
- **1527** plague kills Hokta; the Akwesa are much reduced; **he becomes miko and king at ~22**
- **1535** sells the territory for 4,000 taels under threat of invasion and execution for treason
- **1535–1543** governs it for the colony
- **1543** removed for siding with Tamsui in the [[Tamsui Plot]]
- **1543–** ambassador to his own former people

**The man whose family built the territory signed it away, governed it for the colony that took it, sided with the settlement against the capital, and finished his life as the colony's intermediary with the people he had once ruled.**

## 2. The Kawesa name — decisions and open points
**Kengohka**, formed from the first element of *Khèng* (慶). Chosen by the user from three options (Khengiokwa, **Giokwa**, **Kengohka**) — **Kengohka** preferred for having the heavier clusters, making it more distinctly Kawesa in shape than the smoother *Giokwa*.

**The article states plainly that the colonial papers do not record when the name was given**, and does not assert whether it came at the marriage (1521) or the accession (1527). **This is deliberate** — but the user may want it decided, since the timing says when the Akwesa began treating him as one of their own.

**A useful detail now in the article:** the Akwesa called him *Kengohka* and the administration called him *Chiu Khéng-gio̍k* — **the two usages corresponding to the two offices he held.** One name for the people he ruled, one for the power that employed him.

---

## 3. What the article deliberately does not do
- **No motive attributed.** It does not say he sold the territory reluctantly, or that he had no choice, or that he profited. It records the price, the threat, and the fact that no charge was laid.
- **No claim about his feelings** on being removed in 1543 or on being made ambassador.
- **The record section says the evidence "consists of the administration's papers, which were written by the party to the transactions that dispossessed him."** That is the honest position and it is the strongest thing that can be said without inventing testimony.

---

## 4. Open questions
1. **Date of death.** Currently shown as "his death" in four infobox fields, which is accurate but unsatisfying. He was ~38 on his removal in 1543. **If he lived to 65–70 he died c. 1570–75** — which would mean he lived to see the [[Great Disappointment]] of 1564, when Volisanian explorers reached the Zong colonies from the east. That is a significant thing for him to have witnessed and it probably wants deciding.
2. **Did he have children with Chinowa?** Unrecorded. If he did, they would be the heirs to a lapsed chieftainship and would have a claim on a territory their grandfather sold.
3. **When did he take the name Kengohka** — marriage or accession?
4. **What happened to Chinowa?** She appears in 1535 and 1543 and then is not mentioned.
5. **What did his father Chiu Bûn-hái make of the sale?** He does not appear in the record after the 1520s. **If he was alive in 1535** he watched his son sell the kingdom he had spent twenty-four years building — which is the single most powerful unwritten scene in the whole scenario.
6. **Are there descendants?** The Chiu family received 4,000 taels of gold in 1535 and presumably remained in the colony. **A merchant family descending from the last miko of the Akwesa would be a good modern Sinchewese thread.**
7. **The later literature** mentioned in the record section — what does it argue? The sale of 1535 as collaboration or coercion is the obvious historiographical fight.

Unnamed, which is realistic and useful — but note what they are:
- **Grandchildren of Hokta** through Chinowa — heirs of the last miko
- **Grandchildren of Chiu Bûn-hái** through CKG — heirs of the company's founder

I had him dying in 1572 at 67 with an unrecorded death place. **Both were wrong.** The corrected lifespan is **twenty-six years longer** and changes his significance considerably.

### What the 93 years put him through
| year | age | event |
|---|---|---|
| 1505 | — | **born at Huwei, the Zong capital** — an elite's son, not a colonial |
| 1511 | 6 | his father takes the grant at the bay; he is brought up at the settlement |
| 1521 | 16 | married to Chinowa |
| 1527 | 22 | plague; Hokta dies; he becomes miko and King of Okheng |
| 1535 | 30 | sells the territory |
| 1543 | 38 | removed; appointed ambassador |
| **1564** | **59** | **the [[Great Disappointment]]** — Volisanian explorers reach the western coast |
| 1572–85 | 67–80 | the [[Great Peasant Revolt]] in Zong |
| 1585 | 80 | Foyuan's Baiduist usurpation |
| 1587–89 | 82–84 | the [[Yuanlong Restoration]] |
| 1594 | 89 | the [[Daaiho War]] begins in the Jyunhap |
| **11 Apr 1598** | **93** | **dies at Tamsui** |

### The two things that matter most
**1. He was miko for 71 years and ambassador for 55.** The offices he held longest were precisely the two that had stopped meaning anything — a chieftainship over a people no longer governed as a chiefdom, and an ambassadorship to them after the colony had taken their land. **He held the titles for three quarters of his life, long after their substance had gone.**

**2. He died just eight years before the Baiduist influx of 1606.** Zong expelled its Baiduists to Sinchew from 1606, which transformed the colony into a major population centre. **He missed it.** He died in the Tamsui he had grown up in — the small frontier settlement — before the events that made Sinchew into what it became.

### He never went home
**Born in the capital, died in the colony, aged 93, having last seen Huwei as a small child.** He lived in the settlement for eighty-seven of his ninety-three years. Whatever the Chiu family had been in Zong, he was a colonial for the whole of his adult life.

### New open questions
1. **How long did Chinowa live?** If she died before him he was a widower for decades; if she lived as long, she died in the colony as an old woman. **Their two children would have been born into this marriage across the middle of the sixteenth century.**
2. **Did he ever revisit Zong?** A colonial official of his standing might have; it would be the only journey of his life that ran in the direction of his birth.
3. **His 60s–90s are unrecorded.** Thirty-five years of ambassadorship, including the Great Disappointment and the whole of the Great Peasant Revolt, and the papers say nothing. **That gap is the largest unwritten space in the scenario.**

## The provisional administratorship was dissolved, not handed on (2026-09-21)
User: *"Provisional administrator of the Tamsui territory was dissolved in 1543 and it was replaced with Tân Bûn-khéng who became the governor of sinchew colony."*

**Corrected.** I had written that Tân Bûn-khéng "succeeded him". He did not — **the office was dissolved and replaced by the governorship of the Sinchew Colony**, of which Tân Bûn-khéng was the first holder.

| office | period | ended by |
|---|---|---|
| Provisional administrator of the Tamsui territory | 1535–1543 | **dissolved** |
| Governorship of the Sinchew Colony | 1543– | *new office*, first holder [[Tân Bûn-khéng]] |

### Why this is a better fact than a simple succession
**The office existed to accommodate the former ruler of an annexed territory.** It was invented for him, and abolished the moment he left it. **His removal and the abolition of his post are a single act** — and the colony then began under a governor with no connection whatever to the people the territory contained.

So the sequence of the administration's dealings with him is:
1. **1535** — buy his territory, keep him in place because his presence eased the transfer
2. **1543** — remove him, and delete the post rather than fill it
3. **1543** — appoint a governor with no tie to the Akwesa, over a colony divided from Louyang

**Tân Bûn-khéng did not succeed him in an office. He was the first holder of the office that replaced it** — and it was the same man who had devised the legal ground for the acquisition five years earlier.
