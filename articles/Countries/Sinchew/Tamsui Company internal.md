# The Oktalwa Company and the Tamsui pseudo-state (1511–1535) — internal notes

Begun 2026-09-21. Working notes for the private enterprise that held the Tamsui site before the colonial government bought it.

Related: `History of Sinchew (1474-1538).mediawiki`, `Tamsui Plot.mediawiki`, `Hachina internal.md`. Agent memory `reference/sinchew_gis.md`.

---

## 1. The ten pigs — a grant, not a sale

**User's ruling: the Akwesa did not think they were selling land.**

In Akwesa understanding, land was not alienable property. What was granted in 1511 was **use** — a place to stand, build a depot and trade. The ten pigs were not a price; they were the customary gift that accompanied a grant of hospitality.

**This is the foundation of everything that follows.** The Zong spent twenty-four years building a settlement on land they believed they had bought and the Akwesa believed they had lent. The "purchase" of 1535 compounded it, and the Akwesa never understood the territory as sold — only as taken.

It is the same failure of translation that runs through Sinchewese history elsewhere: the Hahkina believing for eighty years that they had an alliance the Zong did not acknowledge. Same pattern, smaller scale, higher stakes.

---

## 2. Motives — a strategic marriage, not a romance

**The love story may be fake.** The user: *"the love story might also be fake, it may have been a strategic marriage organized by the clan leader and the [Chiu] commercial leader."*

The later romantic account — a merchant's son and a chief's daughter falling in love — is probably a **literary invention**, and possibly a deliberately flattering one. What actually happened was an alliance between two ambitious men:

| party | what he wanted |
|---|---|
| **Chiu Bûn-hái** | A prominent merchant who **wanted a kingdom and polity of his own** — not merely profit |
| **Hokta** | **Security.** His land lay **between hostile Kawesa clans who wanted his bay access**, and he needed a permanent armed ally |

**Chiu Khéng-gio̍k was a teenager in 1521.** The marriage was concluded on his father's initiative and for his father's purposes, and the son inherited an arrangement he had not made.

---

## 3. What Anping objected to

**Not the marriage. The sovereignty.**

The colonial government's real objection was that **Chiu Bûn-hái was establishing and dealing not on behalf of Zong**. He was conducting foreign policy, waging war, and expanding a territory in his own name — acting as a sovereign rather than as a Zong subject.

That is why the administration judged it "not worth managing" in 1522. To acknowledge the arrangement would have been to concede that a private merchant had acquired a polity — and to refuse it would have required action they did not want to take. So they did nothing, and kept the file.

**Which is precisely what made the file useful in 1535.**

---

## 4. The forced sale (1535)

The company was **paid in gold** and, simultaneously, **threatened with invasion and execution for treason** if it did not comply. The secret marriage, the independent diplomacy, the private wars and the unlicensed dealings with a foreign clan were all available as charges.

**It was not a purchase in any meaningful sense.** It was a settlement forced on a company that had no lawful standing to refuse, over land whose original grant had never been a sale.

---

## 5. The transition — Chiu Khéng-gio̍k as governor

**The user's ruling:** Chiu Khéng-gio̍k and Chinowa **remained in the territory**, and CKG was **made governor**.

The reasoning was political. **The Zong government judged that keeping the sitting merchant-administrator in place would make the transfer of power easier to sell** — to the Akwesa, to the settlers, and to the company's own employees. Zong became the **parent polity** of a territory it had just annexed, and CKG continued as **provisional administrator**, the territory remaining a sub-territory of the Zong colony.

### CKG's arc — the outline is very good
| period | role |
|---|---|
| 1511 | the founder's son, a child |
| 1521 | married to Chinowa, as a teenager, in an alliance made by his father |
| 1535 | **governor / provisional administrator** of the annexed territory |
| 1543 | **removed — he was part of the [[Tamsui Plot]]** |
| 1543– | **peacefully deposed**, then appointed **ambassador to the Akwesa**, managing relations with the clan for the remainder of his life |

**The man whose family built Tamsui sided with Tamsui against Anping.** Then, rather than being punished, he was given the one post for which he was uniquely qualified — the only Zong official who spoke Akwesa, was married into the chiefly family, and had lived there since childhood. He spent the rest of his life as the bridge between the colony and the clan his family had joined.

**His replacement became the first governor of the Sinchew Colony.**

**Note the lifespan this implies.** If CKG was a teenager in 1521, he was born around 1505. Governor in 1535 at thirty. Removed in 1543 at thirty-eight. If he lived to sixty or seventy — plausible for a man of his standing — he served as ambassador to the Akwesa for **twenty to thirty years**, into the 1560s and 1570s. He would have outlived the founding of the Sinchew colony, the Baiduist influx, and possibly the Great Disappointment.
