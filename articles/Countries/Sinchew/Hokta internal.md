## 2. The significant consequence of 1527

**He died eight years before the purchase of 1535.** He concluded the alliance, saw the territory expand under it, and was dead before the colony bought it out. He never learned — as far as the record shows — that the ten pigs had been treated as a sale.

That also means **his successor, not Hokta, was chief when the territory was sold.** The successor's name is not preserved; the article says so explicitly.

## 5. Open questions

1. **Is "chief" the right title?** Or should Hokta hold something more elevated within Akwesa practice?
2. **Who succeeded him in 1527?** The article says the name is not preserved — is that right, or should the successor be named?
3. ~~Was the successor Chinowa's brother?~~ **RESOLVED — no sons.** User, 2026-09-21: *"Hokta had no sons only two daughters."* So there was no brother to inherit. **The kingship passed through a daughter, or to some other kinsman, and the record does not say how.**

   **This is now the most significant open question in the whole scenario.** With no male heir, the succession in 1527 must have run through one of the two daughters — and Chinowa was the one married to [[Chiu Khéng-gio̍k]]. **If Akwesa kingship passed to a daughter's husband, then Chiu Khéng-gio̍k may have become king of the Akwesa in 1527.**

   That would make his subsequent career extraordinary: a Zong merchant's son, married in at seventeen or so, **king of the Akwesa by twenty-two**, the man the colony had to negotiate with over the sale of 1535, **made governor of the annexed territory in the same year**, removed in 1543 for siding with Tamsui, and then **ambassador to the Akwesa for the rest of his life** — a role that reads very differently if he had been their king.

   **Alternatively** the kingship went to the second daughter's husband, or to a more distant kinsman, in which case CKG was merely the consort of a princess. **The user must decide.**

6. **Who is the second daughter?** Unnamed. **A name is needed.** Candidates in the Akwesa/Muskogean pattern: Talhina, Oktisa, Hoktina. Her marriage — if any — and its political consequence are unrecorded.
4. **Did he have a wife or wives recorded?** Chinowa's mother is unrecorded.
5. **Did the Akwesa have a burial practice** — is there a grave site? (A grave would be the only possible Akwesa-side monument.)

### What actually happened
| period | Hokta's status |
|---|---|
| life (d. 1527) | **chief of the Akwesa** — that is all. No Zong document of his lifetime gives him anything higher. |
| posthumously, by the 1530s | declared **King of the Northern Mahiks** — ruler of the *entire region* |

**The king comes from the Zong word for king.** It was not an Akwesa title, not conferred by the company in his lifetime, and not his. It was a **Zong title conferred on a dead man**.

### The three-step legal argument
1. The northern chiefdoms had **all been subject to Hokta as their king**
2. Hokta **granted his land to the Sūn-heng Hâng** → the company conveyed it to the colony (1535)
3. Therefore **all northern mahik land was the colony's already**, and chiefdoms that did not submit were **subjects in rebellion** — not foreign powers

### Why this is the right answer
**It converts a war of conquest into the suppression of a rebellion.** That is why the First Mahik War could be fought and the region partitioned without the Zong ever having to argue they were conquering anyone. The chiefdoms were not defeated enemies — they were **rebels against their own king**, and their king had given their land away.

**It also fits the colonial record exactly.** The Zong called Hokta a *chief* in his lifetime and a *king* only when they needed one — which is why "chief" and "king" both attach to him in different documents. **The gap between the two titles is the whole story.**

**And it makes the ten pigs do double duty.** The same grant — hospitality mistaken for sale — is first used to establish that the company owned the plot (1535), then expanded into a claim that the whole north was the colony's (1538). A gift of ten pigs becomes the title deed to a subcontinent.

### The Akwesa word for chief
The user: *"whatever their word was for it"* — **a word is still needed.** Muskogean, from which the Akwesa family descends: ***miko*** is the genuine Muskogean/Creek word for chief (also *mekko*). Recommended, unless the user prefers a coinage.

## SETTLED: miko, and the fiction dates to the 1538 negotiations (2026-09-21)

User: *"so he was the Miko of Akwesa"* and *"hmm probably this fiction was created sometime in the Treaty of Anping, because they needed to define the tamsui/sinchew borders with the hachina, they had said the land ceeded by the 'king' of the akwesa was to be the zong, and shared with the hachina."*

### The title in life: miko
**Hokta was the miko of the Akwesa.** *Miko* is the genuine Muskogean (Creek) word for chief, which fits the Kawesa family's Muskogean mirror. The Zong record renders it *chief*. **He held nothing higher, ever, in life.** A [[Miko]] article is now wanted.

### The fiction dates to the Treaty of Anping negotiations, 1538
**Its purpose was to fix the boundary with the Hahkina.** This is the piece that was missing, and it makes the whole thing click.

**The problem:** two allies had just agreed to partition a region, and needed a line. Nothing natural divided it, and the Zong had settled only a single harbour.

**The solution:** the line would follow *the extent of what the Zong had acquired*. So a title was needed, and its **size determined the border**:

| if Hokta was | Zong could claim | Hahkina got |
|---|---|---|
| miko of the Akwesa | the Akwesa's coast only | nearly everything |
| **King of the Northern Mahiks** | **the whole north** | only what lay beyond it |

**So the Zong claimed the entire coast on the strength of a dead man's jurisdiction — and the Hahkina were given the remainder, not as land they had won, but as land the Zong had generously shared out of its own.**

**The boundary of 1538 therefore follows a legal argument, not a military fact.** That is a very sharp piece of worldbuilding: the map of Sinchew's northern frontier exists because somebody needed a line in a negotiation and invented a king to draw it.

## Cause of death: plague carried by rats from the trading ships (2026-09-21)
User: *"hokta had died of plague, probably transmitted by rats brought to the territory from trading ships."*

**The earliest recorded outbreak at the bay**, and the disease arrived **by sea, aboard the trading vessels that called at the settlement.**

### The causal chain this completes
**The company that made Hokta a king brought the rats that killed him.** Every subsequent event runs from that:

| year | event |
|---|---|
| 1511 | the Sūn-heng Hâng found the depot; the company's money and men make Hokta a king |
| **1527** | **plague, carried from the ships, kills him** |
| 1527 | no sons; the chiefdom ends; **Chiu Khéng-gio̍k rules Okheng** |
| 1535 | CKG sells the territory to the colony |
| 1538 | the posthumous kingship is invented over his grave |

**He was killed by his own arrangement** — not by betrayal or war, but by a rat that came off a ship he had invited to his bay. And the man who benefited was his own son-in-law, whose father had put the ships there in the first place.

### What the record does and doesn't say
- **Says:** the deaths at the settlement; that he died in 1527 aged about 54
- **Doesn't say:** the course or extent of the outbreak, how many Akwesa died, **whether the epidemic was a factor in the succession**, or whether anyone at the time connected the ships to the disease
- **Nothing from the Akwesa side survives.** The epidemic that ended their chiefdom is known only from the ledger of the people who brought it.

**Worth keeping.** The article states plainly that the colony's papers "do not describe the course of the outbreak, its extent among the Akwesa, or its effect on the chiefdom."

### Open
1. **Did the outbreak kill more of the Akwesa than it did the Zong?** A population with no prior exposure would suffer far worse than the settlers — which would explain why the chiefdom could not survive his death, and would make the epidemic a demographic catastrophe rather than one man's death.
2. **Did it spread to other chiefdoms**, or stay local?
3. **Is this a distinct disease** from the [[Jade Sea Plague]] of the early 1400s (a Fosian pandemic)? It should be — that was a century earlier and on another continent.
4. **Did anyone at the time suspect the ships?** If the Zong recorded it, that is a telling entry.

## The Akwesa were devastated by the outbreaks (2026-09-21)
User: *"yeah I think the akwesa durign thsi time had many deaths from the pleague outbreaks too."*

**Plague recurred at the settlement, and the Akwesa suffered heavily.** Stated in the article as: losses "described in the colonial papers as heavy", no numbers given, and **no account of the epidemics kept by the Akwesa themselves.**

### Why this matters — 1527 is a catastrophe, not a death
**The chiefdom was hollowed out before Hokta died.** So the 1527 succession crisis was not simply "a king died without a son":

1. **Many of the Akwesa were dead** — a population with no prior exposure to a ship-borne disease suffers far worse than its carriers
2. **Hokta died of it**
3. **He left no sons** — and there were fewer kinsmen of any kind left to succeed
4. **The chiefdom was "much reduced"** — unable to hold its own against neighbouring chiefdoms
5. **A company man took the rule** — because there was no viable Akwesa candidate, and the company's men were the only power left standing

**CKG inherited a gutted chiefdom, not a functioning one.** That is why he could rule it as a merchant's son rather than as a king — **there was no one left to insist otherwise.** His governorship in 1535, and the posthumous kingship in 1538, both rest on a population that had already been destroyed by the ships his father sent.

### The double irony, worth preserving
- The **company** made Hokta a king and brought the rats that killed his people
- The **colony** bought the land in 1535 and invented his royal title in 1538 — **both transactions over a chiefdom that the trade had already emptied**

**Nobody had to conquer the Akwesa.** They were destroyed by the presence they had invited.

### Open
1. **What fraction of the Akwesa died?** A figure would sharpen this, but "heavy" and no number is realistic given the record.
2. **Did the other six chiefdoms suffer equally**, or was Tamsui's exposure uniquely severe because the ships called there? **If the others were spared, the epidemic was the reason the Akwesa alone could not hold their position.**
3. **Did the Zong settlers die at the same rate?** Adults with prior exposure would fare better; the differential is the story.
4. **Is there a name for the epidemic** in the colonial papers, or is it recorded only as mortality?
