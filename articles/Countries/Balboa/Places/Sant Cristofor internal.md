# Sant Cristòfor — Internal Notes

## History

### Pre-colonial period

The Badia de Sant Cristòfor and its surrounding coastal plain have been inhabited for thousands of years by the Birú people, the indigenous inhabitants of the Isthmus of Balboa. The bay's sheltered waters, productive fisheries, fertile coastal plain, and position at the western terminus of the trans-isthmian portage route made it one of the most densely populated and economically significant areas in pre-colonial Balboa.

#### Birú settlement

The earliest archaeological evidence of human habitation in the bay area dates to approximately 3000 BCE, with shell middens found along the eastern shore of the bay and at the base of the peninsula. These middens — composed primarily of oyster, clam, and mangrove snail shells — indicate that the bay's shallow southern end was a particularly rich shellfish harvesting ground, a pattern that continued into the colonial period.

By the first millennium CE, the Birú population around the bay had developed into a substantial settled community. The main settlement was located on the eastern shore of the bay, on the site of what is now the ciutat of Original, where the Riu Llobregat entered the bay and provided fresh water. Smaller settlements existed on the peninsula — the earliest known settlement on the peninsula was a fishing village on the bay shore, on the site of present-day Pandao, which was accessible by canoe across the bay.

The Birú of the bay area lived in permanent villages of thatched longhouses, practicing a mixed subsistence economy of fishing, agriculture, and trade. The coastal plain was cultivated with root crops, maize, and fruit trees, while the bay provided fish, shellfish, and crustaceans. The shallow southern end of the bay was also used for salt production, a practice that would continue for centuries and become a major colonial industry.

#### Trans-isthmian portage

The bay's strategic importance derived from its position at the western terminus of the principal trans-isthmian portage route. The Birú portage routes — a network of footpaths across the Isthmus of Balboa — connected the Kendriyan Ocean coast at the Badia de Sant Cristòfor to the Aethiopian Ocean coast at what is now Sant Llàtzer. The crossing took four to six days on foot, with goods carried by porters or, in some sections, by canoe along the rivers that drained toward either coast.

The Birú of the bay area controlled the western end of this route and served as intermediaries, porters, and guides for trans-isthmian trade. Goods arriving from the Aethiopian coast — spices, textiles, metals — were unloaded at the bay shore and transferred to ocean-going canoes for distribution along the Kendriyan coast, and vice versa. The bay's sheltered harbour made it a natural staging point, and the Birú community grew wealthy from the transit trade.

The portage route followed the natural corridor between the Serralada d'Estret foothills and the bay, roughly along the line of what is now the Riu Llobregat valley and the modern Camí Ral. The Birú maintained way stations and rest points along the route, and the path itself was improved over centuries of use, with stone paving in some sections and wooden causeways across the wetter areas of the coastal plain.

#### Canoe transport and the bay

The Birú were accomplished canoeists, and the bay was the primary transport route within the local area. Canoes crossed the bay regularly between the eastern shore settlements and the peninsula, a pattern that prefigured the modern ferry network by centuries. The Birú also navigated the Shendan Ocean coast in larger sailing canoes, trading along the coast to the north and south.

#### Etymology notes

- **Pandao**: From Zong word *bandao* (半島), meaning "peninsula." Given by Zong traders who used the bay as a stopping point on their Kendriyan Ocean trade routes. Adopted into Volisanian and later Catalan as "Pandao," becoming the name of the fishing village on the peninsula's bay shore.

### Naming note

Until the Second War of the Isthmus (1893–1899), the name "Sant Cristòfor" referred only to the settlement on the eastern shore of the bay — the area now known as the ciutat of **Original**. The city did not extend beyond this area; the peninsula, the coastal plain, and the surrounding districts were separate communities or undeveloped land. Under Zong administration, the old core was renamed **Sant Cristòfor Central** to distinguish it from the larger urban area that now bore the name Sant Cristòfor. The name **Original** — meaning "the original [Sant Cristòfor]" — was not given until the post-mandate territorial reorganisation of the early 1960s, when the city's ciutats were formally established and the historic core received its current name to distinguish it from the larger city.

### Volisanian colonial period

#### Discovery and establishment

Volisanian explorers reached the Badia de Sant Cristòfor by land in the early sixteenth century, following the Birú portage routes westward from the Aethiopian coast. The expedition, led by the Volisanian navigator Cristòfor de Muntaner, arrived at the bay in 1524 and recognised its strategic value immediately: a sheltered deep-water harbour on the Kendriyan coast, at the western terminus of the only viable trans-isthmian crossing. Muntaner named the bay "Sant Cristòfor" after his patron saint, and the name was subsequently applied to the settlement that the Volisanians established on the eastern shore.

The Volisanian colonial government formally established Sant Cristòfor as the official Kendriyan port of the isthmian transit corridor in 1547, alongside Sant Llàtzer on the Aethiopian coast. Between the two, the colonial government constructed the Camí Ral (Volisanian: "Royal Road"), a paved highway that replaced the Birú footpaths and became the primary commercial artery across the isthmus. The Camí Ral reduced the crossing time from four to six days to approximately two days, and could accommodate oxcart convoys and, later, stagecoach services.

The Birú population of the bay area was largely displaced from the eastern shore settlement, which became the site of the Volisanian town. The Birú were pressed into service as porters and labourers on the Camí Ral, and many were pushed to the margins of the colonial economy — the peninsula, the southern bay shore, and the hills. Salt production, which the Birú had practiced for centuries, was taken over by the colonial government and expanded into a major industry.

#### Trade hub and Fosian commerce

Sant Cristòfor's position as the Kendriyan terminus of the trans-isthmian corridor made it one of the most important commercial ports in the New World. The city grew rapidly as goods flowing between the Aethiopian and Kendriyan Oceans were unloaded, transferred to oxcart convoys, carried across the isthmus, and reloaded onto ocean-going vessels. The Volisanian colonial government levied transit fees, docking fees, and warehousing charges on all goods passing through the port, and these revenues became a major source of colonial income.

The city's trade was dominated by two flows: Aethiopian goods (spices, textiles, metals) moving west to the Kendriyan market, and Kendriyan goods (silver, hardwoods, agricultural products) moving east to the Aethiopian market. However, the most lucrative and politically significant trade was with Fosian Lurandia — particularly the Kingdom of Zong, which had established the colony of Tamsui on the western coast of Lurandia and required a reliable route to Illypnian markets.

Zong merchants had been trading at the bay since before the Volisanian arrival — the name "Pandao" (from Zong *bandao*, "peninsula") attests to this earlier presence. Under Volisanian rule, Zong trade through Sant Cristòfor grew enormously, but the Volisanian authorities viewed Fosian commerce with suspicion and sought to extract maximum revenue from it.

#### High taxes and the First War of the Isthmus

By the early eighteenth century, the Volisanian colonial government had progressively increased the transit and docking fees on Fosian goods passing through Sant Cristòfor. The fees were explicitly discriminatory — Fosian merchants paid significantly higher rates than Volisanian or Illypnian traders — and were designed both to raise revenue and to disadvantage Fosian commercial competitors. The Kingdom of Zong, whose entire Illypnian trade route depended on the isthmian crossing, objected to the fees as an impediment to its commercial interests.

Diplomatic protests failed to reduce the fees, and in 1711 Zong launched a military expedition to seize Sant Cristòfor by force. The First War of the Isthmus (1711–1716) was the first major conflict between Illypnian and Fosian colonial powers in the New World. Zong forces landed on the Kendriyan coast and attempted to take the city, but were repelled by the Volisanian garrison with the support of Birú auxiliaries who knew the terrain.

The war ended with the Treaty of Remoria (1716), which granted Zong docking and passage rights through Volisanian ports — the very access that Zong had sought. The Camí Ral remained under Volisanian control, but the treaty established the principle that the isthmian corridor was a matter of international concern, not merely a domestic colonial matter.

#### Free port status

The Treaty of Remoria and the subsequent commercial pressures led the Volisanian colonial government to restructure Sant Cristòfor's economic regime. In 1723, the city was designated a free port — goods could be imported, stored, and re-exported without paying the standard colonial transit duties. The free port status was intended to keep Sant Cristòfor competitive as a trade hub and to prevent future conflicts over access.

The free port designation transformed the city. Merchants from across Adonia — Volisanian, Zong, Nakamizu, and others — established trading houses and warehouses in the city. The volume of goods passing through the port increased dramatically, and Sant Cristòfor became one of the wealthiest cities in the New World. The free port status also attracted a diverse population: Zong merchants settled in the city (reinforcing the Pandao district on the peninsula), Nakamizu traders established a presence, and the city developed a cosmopolitan character that distinguished it from the more homogeneous Volisanian colonial towns.

The salt works at the southern end of the bay continued to operate throughout this period, and the city's economy was further diversified by shipbuilding, rope-making, and other maritime industries that served the port. The peninsula — still known as Pandao — developed as a residential area for Zong merchants and their families, and as a fishing community supplying the city with fresh fish.

### Late colonial period

#### Hub of the slave trade

By the mid-eighteenth century, Sant Cristòfor had become the principal hub of the trans-Kaftian slave trade in Balboa. Enslaved Kaftians — drawn primarily from the regions corresponding to modern Burawa, Dakare, Galwa, and Sarfone — were transported across the Aethiopian Ocean and brought ashore at Sant Cristòfor, the primary disembarkation point for the entire isthmian plantation economy. From the city's wharves, the enslaved were sold at auction and then transported inland to the sugar, coffee, and banana plantations of the Estret and Migjorn coastal plains.

The slave trade left an indelible mark on the city. Auction houses and holding pens lined the bay shore near the port, and the city's economy became deeply entwined with the trade in human beings. The Volisanian colonial government collected duties on each enslaved person brought ashore, and the city's merchant class — both Volisanian and Zong — profited from the trade as brokers, financiers, and transporters. The salt works at the southern end of the bay, which had been a Birú industry, was now worked by enslaved labourers.

The conditions of the trade were brutal. Mortality rates during the transoceanic crossing were high, and those who survived were held in crowded holding pens on the bay shore before auction. The city's free Black population — descendants of those who had purchased or been granted their freedom — was small but growing, and existed in a precarious legal position between the enslaved majority and the free colonial elite.

By the early nineteenth century, the trans-Kaftian slave trade was coming under increasing pressure from abolitionist movements in Illypnia. The Volisanian colonial government formally abolished the slave trade in 1807, though illegal trafficking continued for decades. Slavery itself was not abolished until 1848, making Balboa the second-to-last Volisanian colony to do so. The formerly enslaved population, however, continued to work the plantations under conditions scarcely distinguishable from slavery, through systems of debt peonage and contract labour.

#### Urban culture

Sant Cristòfor's position as a free port and cosmopolitan entrepôt produced a distinctive urban culture that set it apart from the rest of colonial Balboa. The city was home to Volisanian colonial officials and merchants, Zong traders and their families, Nakamizu commercial agents, a small but growing free Black commercial class, and the Birú communities that persisted on the margins of the colonial economy. This mix of peoples and cultures — Illypnian, Fosian, Kaftian, and indigenous — created a society that was more diverse, more commercially oriented, and less rigidly stratified than the plantation-dominated interior.

The city's cultural life was centred on the port and the bay shore. The waterfront was a continuous strip of wharves, warehouses, taverns, and merchant houses, buzzing with activity at all hours. The Camí Ral began at the waterfront, and the constant flow of goods and people between the two oceans gave the city a restless, mercantile energy. The Zong community around Pandao maintained its own cultural institutions — temples, tea houses, and trading companies — while the free Black community established mutual aid societies, religious brotherhoods, and informal markets.

The city was also the birthplace of [[Balboan Creole]], the creole language that emerged from the contact between Volisanian, Kaftian languages, and Fosian linguistic influences. The Creole first developed in the port districts and the plantation camps, where enslaved Kaftians, Volisanian overseers, and Zong traders needed a common tongue for commercial and social interaction. By the late colonial period, Balboan Creole was the vernacular of the city's working-class and Black populations, while Volisanian remained the language of government, commerce, and the colonial elite.

Music and religion in the city reflected its syncretic character. Catholicism, introduced by the Volisanians, was practiced alongside Zong spiritual traditions and the syncretic religious practices of the enslaved and free Black population, which blended Kaftian spiritual traditions with Catholic saints and rituals. Musical traditions from Kaftia, Illypnia, and Fosia merged in the port districts, producing the early forms of what would later become [[istmo son]], the distinctive musical genre of the isthmus.

#### Road to independence

Balboa's independence from Volisania was not the product of a domestic liberation movement but of a Volisanian policy decision. The abolition of slavery in 1848 had removed the economic foundation of the Balboan plantation system without providing any substitute. The colony's principal export — sugar — was in decline, and the costs of administration, constabulary, and the periodic suppression of unrest exceeded the revenue that the colony generated. The Volisanian liberal government, which had come to power in the early 1850s, concluded that independent states within the Volisanian economic and military orbit would serve the same strategic purpose as colonies, but without the diplomatic liability of formal empire — and would present Volisania as a progressive power granting self-determination, in contrast to the Fosian states that maintained tighter control through the commonwealth system.

The Volisanian government selected [[Jordi Rovira i Mas]], a sugar planter from the Jala highlands and a former slaveowner, as the candidate most amenable to Volisanian interests. Rovira i Mas's commercial orientation was toward the Volisanian export market, and he was a vehement racist who had opposed the abolition of slavery — qualities that Volisania regarded as assets, ensuring that the social order protecting Volisanian commercial interests would be maintained. Balboa gained independence on 15 March 1854, with Rovira i Mas as its first president.

The independence settlement had immediate implications for Sant Cristòfor. As an independent state, Balboa was no longer bound by the Treaty of Remoria (1716), which had granted Zong docking and passage rights through Volisanian ports. The Zong government, recognising the implications, entered into negotiations with the new republic and paid a sum in exchange for the continuation of Sant Cristòfor's free port status and the grant of military docking rights at the port. The arrangement preserved the city's commercial character and its role as the hub of Fosian–Illypnian trade on the Kendriyan coast.

### First Republic (1854–1899)

#### Capital city

With independence, Sant Cristòfor became the capital of the Republic of Balboa. The colonial government buildings on the bay shore were repurposed as the seat of the new republic: the Presidential Palace, the National Assembly, and the Supreme Court were all housed in the former Volisanian colonial compound. The city's choice as capital was natural — it was the largest and wealthiest city in the country, the centre of the trans-isthmian trade, and the only Balboan city with a significant commercial class independent of the plantation economy.

#### Mayors and municipal corruption

The city's municipal government during the First Republic was dominated by a succession of mayors who were, almost without exception, deeply corrupt and in the pocket of business interests. The mayor's office was effectively an extension of the city's commercial elite: merchants, railway company agents, Zong trading houses, and the owners of the salt works and shipyards all competed for influence over the municipal administration, and the mayors served as brokers between these interests and the national government.

The pattern of municipal corruption persisted throughout the period. Mayors were selected not by the electorate but by the commercial factions that dominated the city council, and the office rotated among the representatives of the city's competing business interests. Public works — roads, drainage, the expansion of the port — were consistently awarded to connected contractors at inflated prices. The city's working-class and Black populations, who had no representation in municipal government, bore the consequences: inadequate sanitation, overcrowded housing, and the periodic outbreaks of cholera and yellow fever that plagued the bay shore districts.

#### The assassination of Joan Ballester i Forteza (1886)

The most dramatic event in the city's municipal history was the assassination of Mayor **Joan Ballester i Forteza** on the steps of the Grand Train Terminal on the morning of 14 March 1886. Ballester i Forteza had been mayor since 1882 and was responsible for the Costa Nord annexation and for championing the city's bid for the canal terminus — a bid that had been lost to Portnou the previous year.

His assassin was **Marc Tristany**, a former clerk in the municipal treasury who had been dismissed two years earlier after exposing discrepancies in the city's accounts — discrepancies that pointed to Ballester i Forteza's personal enrichment from the Costa Nord annexation. Tristany's allegations had been ignored by the city council and suppressed by the mayor's office, and Tristany himself had been blacklisted from municipal employment. Destitute and embittered, Tristany shot Ballester i Forteza as the mayor arrived at the terminal for his daily meeting with the railway company officials. Tristany was immediately apprehended and later executed.

The assassination exposed the depth of the city's municipal corruption and the desperation of those excluded from its patronage system. Ballester i Forteza was widely mourned by the commercial elite — he had been effective, if corrupt — but the working-class districts received the news with indifference or quiet satisfaction. The event did nothing to reform the system; his successor, Miquel Renom i Valls, was a caretaker who made no effort to address the grievances that had driven Tristany to murder.

#### The Grand Train Terminal

The most significant municipal construction project of the First Republic was the **Estació Gran de Sant Cristòfor** (Grand Train Terminal), built between 1868 and 1872 on the bay shore, directly across the ferry plaza from the Sant Cristòfor Main ferry terminal. The terminal was the western terminus of the Isthmus Railway and the primary point of transshipment between the railway and the Kendriyan Ocean shipping that used the port.

The terminal was a grand Beaux-Arts building — a monumental stone structure with a vast vaulted train shed, ornate façades facing both the ferry plaza and the rail yards, and a central clock tower that became one of the city's most recognisable landmarks. The building was designed to impress: it was the first major public building constructed after independence, and it was intended to demonstrate that the new republic could build on a scale to rival the great railway termini of Illypnia.

The terminal's construction was, inevitably, mired in corruption. The contract was awarded to a consortium controlled by associates of Mayor Riera i Cabot, and the final cost was nearly double the original estimate. The subsequent investigation revealed that the excess had been divided among the mayor, the city council members who approved the contract, and the railway company officials who had collaborated on the project. Despite the scandal, the terminal was completed and immediately became the beating heart of the city's commercial life. The ferry plaza between the terminal and the ferry pier became the city's primary public space — a crowded, noisy, commercially vital crossroads where railway passengers, ferry passengers, dockworkers, merchants, and street vendors all converged.

The terminal also reshaped the city's geography. The rail yards and maintenance depots that sprawled southward from the terminal along the bay shore became the seed of the industrial district that would later become the ciutat of Ciutat de l'Estació. The neighbourhoods that grew up around the terminal — workers' housing, taverns, boarding houses, and small workshops — were among the city's first urban expansions beyond the old colonial core.

#### Urban growth and the five districts

The city grew substantially during the First Republic, driven by the trans-isthmian trade and the expansion of the port. The population expanded from approximately 80,000 at independence to over 200,000 by the 1880s, as migrants from the interior and immigrants from Volisania, Zong, and other countries were drawn to the city's commercial economy.

In 1866, the city was formally divided into **six districts** by municipal ordinance. The division reflected the city's racial geography, which had developed organically during the colonial period and was now codified into administrative boundaries:

* **The Coast** (La Costa) — the waterfront strip along the bay shore, dominated by the port, wharves, and commercial buildings. This was the city's economic engine and its most integrated district — Volisanian merchants, Zong traders, and Black dockworkers all worked and conducted business here, though the ownership of the wharves and warehouses was overwhelmingly white.

* **The Market** (El Mercat) — the central district, located inland from the coast. The city's main market, shops, and professional offices were concentrated here. Like the Coast, the Market was relatively integrated: it was the commercial heart of the city where all communities interacted. The newspaper *El Transístmico* was headquartered in the Market district.

* **The Northern District** (Districte Nord) — the residential area north of the Market, along the bay shore toward the bay's mouth. This was the white district, home to the city's Volisanian colonial elite, the planter families who maintained townhouses in the city, and the senior officials of the colonial and later republican government. The Northern District was the wealthiest and most spacious part of the city, with tree-lined streets and substantial houses.

* **The Eastern District** (Districte Est) — the residential area east of the Market, stretching toward the rising ground of the Serralada foothills. This was the Zong district, home to the city's Zong merchant community, Zong trading company employees, and their families. The Eastern District had its own temples, tea houses, and Zong-language institutions, and maintained a distinct cultural identity within the city.

* **The Southeastern District** (Districte Sud-est) — the residential area southeast of the Market, between the Eastern District and the Southern District. This was the mixed-race district, home to the city's mixed Balboan population — people of Volisanian and Kaftian, Zong and Kaftian, or other mixed descent. The mixed-race community occupied an intermediate position in the city's racial hierarchy: above the Black Southern District but below the white Northern District, with greater economic mobility than the former but less social acceptance than the latter.

* **The Southern District** (Districte Sud) — the residential area south of the Market, along the bay shore toward the shallow southern end of the bay. This was the Black district, home to the city's free Black population, the dockworkers and labourers who worked the port, and the descendants of formerly enslaved people who had migrated to the city from the plantation interior. The Southern District was the most overcrowded and least served part of the city — sanitation was inadequate, housing was substandard, and the municipal government invested little in its infrastructure. The Black community's mutual aid societies and religious brotherhoods provided the social services that the city did not: schools, health care, and emergency relief.

The six-district system formalised the city's racial geography but did not create it — the residential patterns had existed since the colonial period. The ordinance, however, gave the segregation an official character: municipal services, policing, and investment were allocated according to the district boundaries, and the Northern District received disproportionately more than the Southern District. The system would persist, with modifications, through the Zong Balboa period and into the mandate era.

#### Pandao and the peninsula

Pandao, on the peninsula across the bay, was not part of the city during the First Republic. It remained a separate community — a fishing village and Zong trading outpost that maintained its own local administration and its own relationship with the Zong trading companies. The ferry crossing between Pandao and the city was the primary link, and the frequency of the service increased steadily throughout the period as commercial traffic between the peninsula and the mainland grew. Pandao would not be incorporated into Sant Cristòfor until after the Second War of the Isthmus.

#### Municipal annexations

The city's rapid growth during the First Republic pushed its boundaries beyond the old colonial core for the first time. Two annexations significantly expanded the city's territory:

**Dàrsena** was annexed in 1872 to increase warehouse and dock facilities. The old city had become intensely overcrowded — the bay shore was packed with wharves, warehouses, and housing, and the commercial traffic through the port had outgrown the available space. Dàrsena, the area immediately south of the city along the bay shore, offered open land suitable for new docks, warehouses, and industrial yards. The annexation was driven by the same commercial interests that dominated the city council: the merchants and trading companies needed expansion space, and the mayor's office obliged by pushing the city boundary southward. The expansion was also a windfall for the landowners in Dàrsena — mostly associates of the municipal elite — whose property values rose sharply once incorporated into the city.

#### The city canals

To accommodate the growing volume of goods passing through the port, the city constructed a network of **canals** in the southern part of the old city and into Dàrsena during the late 1800s. The canals were cut inland from the bay, allowing boats to navigate directly to the warehouses and industrial yards rather than unloading at the bay shore wharves and transporting goods overland. The system dramatically increased the efficiency of the port — goods could be loaded and unloaded directly from warehouse doors — and it became the defining feature of the southern cityscape.

The canals transformed the geography of the southern districts. The Southern District, which had been the city's Black residential quarter, was bisected by the new waterways, and many residents were displaced to make way for the canal construction and the warehouses that lined their banks. The canals also created a new kind of urban environment — a district of waterways, loading docks, and warehouse fronts that was unlike anything else in the city, with small boats and barges navigating between the buildings. The canal district became a working waterfront, distinct from the grand bay shore port, and it employed thousands of dockworkers, boatmen, and warehouse labourers.

The canals were, like every other municipal project of the period, mired in corruption. The contracts for excavation and construction were awarded to associates of the mayors, and the routing of the canals was shaped as much by the property interests of the city council members as by commercial logic. Several canals were dug to pass directly adjacent to warehouses owned by council members, increasing those properties' values substantially.

**Costa Nord** was annexed in 1880 for a different reason: to prevent the city's wealthy residents from leaving the tax base. By the 1870s, the city's commercial elite had begun building homes along the Shendan Ocean coast north of the bay mouth, attracted by the ocean views, the sea breeze, and the distance from the crowded, disease-ridden bay shore. The exodus threatened the city's tax revenue — the wealthiest residents were moving beyond the city boundary and taking their property taxes with them. The annexation of Costa Nord brought the ocean coast within the city limits and kept the tax base intact. The move was deeply unpopular with the Costa Nord residents themselves, who had no desire to be governed by the corrupt Sant Cristòfor city council, but their objections were overruled by the national government, which was itself dominated by the same commercial interests.

#### The canal and the loss of the terminus

The construction of the Balboa Canal (1870s–1885) was a mixed blessing for the city. Sant Cristòfor had expected to be the canal's western terminus, but the project's engineers determined that Portnou, to the south, offered a clearer route and superior harbour conditions. The decision was a significant blow — the canal would bypass the city's harbour entirely, and the city would lose its role as the primary point of transshipment between the isthmian corridor and the Kendriyan Ocean. The Expansió Party government attempted to compensate by investing in road and rail connections between Sant Cristòfor and Portnou, but the canal's opening in 1885 marked the beginning of the city's relative decline as a transit hub.

#### Velásquez and the road to war

The Sant Bart's Coup of 1888 installed Rodrigo Velásquez as president, and his administration's policies had immediate consequences for Sant Cristòfor. Velásquez escalated discrimination against the Fosian community — particularly the Zong merchants and residents of the city — imposing new restrictions on Zong commercial activity, increasing docking fees, and pursuing an explicitly anti-Fosian policy that alienated the Eastern District and the city's Zong trading houses. The fee increases and anti-Fosian measures were the direct cause of the Second War of the Isthmus (1893–1899).

#### Second War of the Isthmus (1893–1899)

Zong forces invaded Sant Cristòfor in 1893, landing on the Shendan Ocean coast and advancing on the city from the north. The city's defences were minimal — the old Zong-era fort at El Fort on the peninsula had been decommissioned decades earlier, and the Balboan military presence in the city was limited to a small garrison. The city was occupied by Zong forces after brief resistance. Mayor Pere Gomila i Sastre fled ahead of the advancing troops, along with much of the city's white commercial elite.

The occupation was largely uneventful for the city's remaining population. The Zong administration, which had commercial ties to the Eastern District's Zong community, treated the civilian population with restraint. The Black and mixed-race populations of the Southern and Southeastern Districts, who had little stake in the republic's survival, continued their daily lives under occupation with minimal disruption. The war's fighting took place largely outside the city, in the interior and along the canal corridor.

The war ended with the Treaty of Sant Llàtzer (1899), which transferred Sant Cristòfor, the canal, and the surrounding territory to Zong — beginning the Zong Balboa period.

### Zong Balboa period (1899–1950)

#### Administrative reorganisation

The Zong administration fundamentally reorganised the city's governance. The elected mayoralty was abolished; in its place, the city was placed under the direct authority of the Zong governor, who appointed a **Grand Administrator** (Zong: 總行政官) to manage the city and its constituent ciutats. The Grand Administrator was unelected and answered directly to the governor, not to the city's residents. Each ciutat had its own local council, but these were advisory bodies with no executive power — they could make recommendations on local services and planning, but the Grand Administrator had final authority on all decisions.

The city was also dramatically expanded in territory. Under Zong rule, "Sant Cristòfor" referred not merely to the old city but to a vast administrative district encompassing the modern comarcas of Sant Cristòfor and Cumará, as well as parts of Baix Llevant and Els Aiguamolls. This was a far larger territory than the city had ever controlled, and the Grand Administrator's authority extended across all of it.

The old city was broken up into three ciutats, each with its own council:
* **Sant Cristòfor Central** (the former Original, renamed to reflect its new role as the central district of a larger entity)
* **Dàrsena**
* **Costa Nord**

The peninsula, which had been a separate community throughout the First Republic, was incorporated into the city for the first time. It was split into two ciutats:
* **Barcelona** — the western portion of the peninsula, facing the Shendan Ocean
* **Pandao** — the eastern portion of the peninsula, facing the bay, centred on the old fishing village and trading outpost

#### Integration of services

The Zong administration's most significant contribution to the city was the integration and modernisation of municipal services. Under the First Republic, the city's infrastructure had been neglected — roads were unpaved, sanitation was inadequate, and the working-class districts suffered regular outbreaks of disease. The Zong administration, with access to far greater resources than the corrupt republican municipality had ever commanded, invested heavily in public works.

A unified water and sewer system was constructed, serving all six ciutats for the first time. The bay shore wharves were rebuilt and expanded. Roads were paved and a system of street lighting was installed. The Zong administration also introduced a unified police force and fire service, replacing the patchwork of private security and volunteer brigades that had served the city under the republic.

The integration of services was not entirely altruistic. The Zong administration needed a functioning city to support the canal operations and the military base at Portnou, and the infrastructure investments were designed to serve the canal corridor's economic and strategic needs. The Southern District (now part of Sant Cristòfor Central) and the working-class areas of Dàrsena received significantly less investment than the commercial districts and the canal-adjacent areas, and the racial disparities of the republican period persisted, albeit in a less formalised form.

#### The Shining Star era

The Zong Balboa period was one of rapid economic growth for Sant Cristòfor. The canal, which had bypassed the city under the First Republic, now brought enormous economic activity to the region. The city served as the administrative and commercial centre of the canal corridor, and the Zong administration's investments in infrastructure and services attracted businesses and workers. The territory's population grew from 312,000 in 1900 to 2.55 million by 1934.

The period from 1910 to 1935 became known as the **Shining Star era** — a phrase attributed to contemporary observers who contrasted the territory's relative egalitarianism and prosperity with the racial repression of the Balboan interior, the authoritarianism of Volisania, and the economic stagnation of much of Lurandia. The city of Sant Cristòfor, already a historic free port, became a metropolitan centre whose wealth and openness attracted comparison with the great cities of the Jade Sea and the Mesogean.

Governor **Lîm Bûn-cheng** (1910–1925) introduced universal suffrage and anti-discrimination protections between 1912 and 1918, partly as genuine reform and partly as a strategy to attract Black Balboan workers away from the rump state. A Black professional and commercial class developed in the city during this period, and the Black community's mutual aid societies and religious institutions were supplemented by Zong-funded schools and health clinics. The Deliberative Assembly, established in 1912, provided local representation on municipal services, taxation, and commercial regulation, though the governor retained executive authority and could override its decisions.

The Zong trading community, which had been restricted and harassed under the Velásquez administration, flourished under Zong rule. The Eastern District (now part of Sant Cristòfor Central) experienced a commercial renaissance, and Zong capital flowed into the city's port, warehousing, and trading operations. Sinchewese workers also migrated to the city in large numbers, employed in the port and canal operations.

The city's cultural life flourished. A multilingual press served the territory's diverse communities: the Zong-language *Seng-kì-pò*, the Volisan-language *El Faro del Istmo*, the Sinchewese *Chương Châu Nhật Báo*, and the Black Balboan weekly *New Horizon*. The Sant Cristòfor School of painting combined Zong ink-wash techniques with Volisan colonial colour palettes and Sinchewese decorative motifs. The Sant Cristòfor Municipal Theatre (1921) hosted Zong opera, Volisan classical companies, and visiting performers. The Canal District became known for its nightlife and informal musical performances, where Black Balboan musical forms fused with Zong and Sinchewese elements.

#### The urban divide

The Shining Star era's egalitarianism was more legal than social. The population tripled between 1910 and 1934, and the pace of growth far exceeded the administration's capacity to provide formal housing. The city functioned, in practice, as two distinct settlements: a built-up commercial and administrative core of increasing density, where Zong capital funded high-rise construction, modern infrastructure, and the institutions of colonial governance; and a periphery of hastily constructed exurbs and informal settlements that housed the Sinchewese and Black Balboan working populations.

The central district — with its Zong shophouses, colonial administrative buildings, and increasingly tall commercial structures — presented the image of a prosperous, modern city. The elevated western quarter, occupied principally by the Zong population, was well-served by municipal services: paved roads, piped water, electricity, and sanitation. The harbour-front commercial district was similarly provisioned. These areas constituted the Sant Cristòfor that appeared in Zong promotional materials and that earned the territory its reputation as the shining star of Adonia.

The exurbs told a different story. The neighbourhoods that spread outward from the city along the road to the canal zone and the harbour approaches were built largely without planning permission, municipal oversight, or formal infrastructure. Structures were assembled from salvaged materials, corrugated iron, and rough timber; sanitation was rudimentary; access to clean water was inconsistent. The Zong administration tolerated these settlements as a necessary consequence of rapid population growth, and by the late 1920s the majority of the territory's Sinchewese and Black Balboan populations lived in them.

The Ong administration acknowledged the housing crisis in 1931, announcing a programme of social housing construction and slum clearance. Construction began in 1932 on several estates in the exurban districts, but the programme was interrupted by the outbreak of war in 1935, by which point only a fraction of the planned housing had been completed.

#### Pandao and the Black middle class

Pandao, the eastern ciutat on the peninsula facing the bay, underwent a transformation during the Shining Star era. Under the First Republic, Pandao had been a separate fishing village and Zong trading outpost — modest, low-rise, and peripheral. Under Zong rule, it became something unexpected: the home of the emerging Black Balboan middle class.

The reason was largely spatial. The mainland — Sant Cristòfor Central, Dàrsena, and the exurbs spreading outward — was increasingly crowded, with the working-class districts packed to overflowing and the commercial core dominated by Zong capital and Zong institutions. The peninsula, accessible only by ferry, offered something the mainland could not: room to breathe. Pandao's bay-shore location meant it was well-connected to the mainland by ferry, but its physical separation meant it was insulated from the worst of the overcrowding, the disease, and the informal settlements that plagued the exurban districts. The anti-discrimination legislation meant that Black Balboans were free to live anywhere in the territory, and Pandao — with its open land, its sea views, and its distance from the Zong-dominated western quarter — became the natural choice for those who could afford to move.

Black professionals — merchants, doctors, educators, the graduates of the Sant Cristòfor Academy — began settling in Pandao in the 1910s and 1920s, building substantial homes along the bay shore and establishing their own commercial strip of shops, offices, and community institutions. The neighbourhood retained its fishing community and its older Zong-era buildings, but the new Black middle-class development gave Pandao a distinctive character: a prosperous, self-confident Black community that existed nowhere else in Lurandia. The *New Horizon* newspaper, the voice of the Black professional class, was headquartered in Pandao by the late 1920s.

Pandao's growth was a source of quiet pride for the Black community and a visible demonstration of the Shining Star era's egalitarian promise. It was also, however, a reminder of the limits of that promise: the Black middle class that flourished in Pandao was a small fraction of the territory's Black population, the vast majority of whom remained in the overcrowded exurbs and canal zone settlements. The distance between Pandao's bay-shore villas and the corrugated-iron shacks of the exurbs was as great as any racial barrier the law had abolished.

#### Skyscraper construction

The city's rapid economic rise was expressed in a dramatic vertical transformation of the skyline. Before the Zong period, Sant Cristòfor had been a low-rise city — the colonial and republican buildings were typically two to four storeys, with the Grand Train Terminal's clock tower as the only notable exception. The Zong administration's investment in infrastructure and the influx of Zong capital created the conditions for a building boom that transformed the city's appearance.

The first wave of skyscraper construction began in the 1910s, concentrated along the bay shore in Sant Cristòfor Central. Zong trading companies, banks, and insurance firms built increasingly tall headquarters along the waterfront, competing for prominence and ocean views. The buildings were typically in the Zong commercial style — reinforced concrete frames with decorative elements drawn from both Zong and Illypnian architectural traditions. By the 1920s, the bay shore skyline was a wall of ten- and fifteen-storey buildings, and the tallest structures in the city were commercial towers rather than the church steeples and clock towers of the republican era.

A second wave in the late 1920s and early 1930s pushed higher still, with several buildings exceeding twenty storeys. The most prominent was the **Torre del Comerç** (Commerce Tower), a 24-storey Zong bank headquarters completed in 1931, which became the tallest building in Balboa and the symbol of the city's commercial ambition. The tower dominated the Sant Cristòfor Central skyline until the Great Adonian War.

The skyscraper boom was largely confined to Sant Cristòfor Central — the commercial and administrative core. The other ciutats remained low-rise: Dàrsena was industrial, Costa Nord was residential, and the peninsula ciutats of Barcelona and Pandao were still primarily fishing and trading communities. The contrast between the soaring commercial core and the low-rise working-class districts was a visible expression of the city's economic inequalities.

#### Suburban development and the outer ciutats

The territory's explosive population growth during the Shining Star era could not be contained within the old city boundaries. As the exurbs spread outward from the core, the Zong administration extended the city's rail network to serve the growing suburbs, and new ciutats began to take shape on the coastal plain east and south of the old city.

The Isthmus Railway, which had connected Sant Cristòfor to Sant Llàtzer since 1816, was supplemented by **commuter rail branches** that extended from the Grand Train Terminal into the developing suburbs. New lines were built eastward and southward from the terminal, with stations at regular intervals serving the residential neighbourhoods that sprang up along the tracks. The commuter rail made it possible for working-class families to live in the outer ciutats — where land was cheaper and housing was more spacious — while commuting to jobs in the port, the canal zone, and the industrial districts.

The ciutats that developed along these rail corridors were working-class suburbs, built largely without the planning oversight that the Zong administration applied to the central district. **Sant Martí**, on the coastal plain east of the old city, grew around a commuter rail junction and became a residential district for the port and canal workforce. **Sant Jordi**, further east, developed as a more spacious suburb for families who had graduated from the exurban shanties but could not afford Pandao or Costa Nord. **Ciutat de l'Estació**, which had grown up around the rail yards south of the Grand Train Terminal during the First Republic, expanded into a full industrial and residential district. **Sant Llorenç**, south of Dàrsena along the bay shore, developed as an industrial suburb with factories and workers' housing.

These outer ciutats were the territory's real working-class communities — the places where the majority of the Sinchewese and Black Balboan populations actually lived, as opposed to the exurban shanties that the Zong administration tolerated but did not formally recognise. The commuter rail gave them a connection to the city's economy, but the services and infrastructure in these ciutats lagged far behind the central district.

#### Streetcars

Within the closer-in ciutats, the Zong administration developed **streetcar** (tram) networks to provide local transit. The streetcars complemented the Metro and the commuter rail: the Metro served the dense central core, the commuter rail connected the outer suburbs, and the streetcars provided local circulation within the individual ciutats.

Three streetcar networks were built during the Zong period:

* **Sant Cristòfor Central** — the most extensive network, with lines radiating from the Grand Train Terminal and the ferry plaza along the main streets of the old city. The Central streetcars connected the bay shore commercial district to the inland neighbourhoods, the rail yards, and the canal district. The network was built in stages between 1905 and 1920, replacing the horse-drawn trams that had served the city since the republican era.

* **Costa Nord** — a smaller network serving the oceanfront residential district. The Costa Nord streetcars ran along the coastal boulevard and connected the residential neighbourhoods to the ferry terminal at the bay mouth, providing access to the central city without the need for a private carriage. The network was built in the 1910s as Costa Nord transitioned from a wealthy enclave of oceanfront villas to a more mixed residential district.

* **Pandao** — a network on the peninsula, connecting the Pandao bay-shore neighbourhoods to the Pandao ferry terminal and, later, to the developing areas further inland on the peninsula. The Pandao streetcars were particularly important because the peninsula had no Metro service and was entirely dependent on the ferry for access to the mainland. The streetcars made it possible to live and work anywhere on the peninsula while maintaining a connection to the city centre. The network was built in the 1920s as Pandao's Black middle-class population grew.

The streetcar networks were built and operated by separate private companies, each holding a concession from the Zong administration for its respective ciutat. The **Sant Cristòfor Tramway Company** operated the Central network, the **Costa Nord Light Railway** served the oceanfront district, and the **Pandao Tram Company** ran the peninsula system. The three companies were independent of one another and of the Metro and commuter rail operators, and there was no unified fare system — passengers transferring between networks paid separate fares. The lack of integration was a persistent grievance for commuters, particularly those who crossed between the mainland and the peninsula daily, but the Zong administration did not compel the companies to coordinate their operations.

#### Construction of the Metro

The most ambitious infrastructure project of the Zong Balboa period was the construction of the **Metro de Sant Cristòfor**, the city's underground railway system. The first two lines were built during this period, both originating underneath the Grand Train Terminal and running eastward and northeastward to connect the neighbourhoods of Sant Cristòfor Central to the growing districts beyond the old city:

* **Line 1** — ran eastward/northeastward from the Grand Train Terminal, connecting the central district to the neighbourhoods in that direction. The line served the commercial and residential areas expanding away from the old bay shore core.

* **Line 2** — also ran eastward/northeastward from the Grand Train Terminal, on a slightly different alignment, serving the industrial and working-class neighbourhoods that had grown up around the rail yards and industrial areas.

Both lines were confined to the mainland within the old city's boundaries. The peninsula — Barcelona and Pandao — had no metro service during the Zong Balboa period and remained dependent on the ferry network for transport to the mainland. The metro connections to the peninsula (Lines 5 and 6), which ran around the bay rather than under it, would not come until the post-reunification era.

* **Line 3** — a planned north–south connector line — was under construction when the Great Adonian War broke out in 1935. Construction was halted and never resumed during the Zong period; the partially built tunnels were used as air-raid shelters during the siege. Line 3 would eventually be completed and opened in the post-reunification era.

The Metro was built by Zong engineering firms using Zong capital. The Black working class provided much of the labour, but the engineering and management positions were overwhelmingly held by Zong nationals. The Metro's opening was widely celebrated — it transformed internal travel within the old city, making it possible to move between districts in minutes rather than walking or relying on horse-drawn trams.

#### The Great Adonian War

The Great Adonian War (1935–1947) devastated the city. The siege of Sant Cristòfor persisted for the duration of the war, causing widespread famine, disease, and displacement among the city's predominantly Black population. The Metro tunnels were used as air-raid shelters. The port and the bay shore suffered significant damage from the fighting, and much of the city's infrastructure was destroyed. The skyscrapers of the bay shore — the Torre del Comerç and the commercial towers — were damaged but not destroyed; their reinforced concrete frames survived the bombardment, though their façades and interiors were gutted. The cultural infrastructure of the Shining Star era was largely destroyed: the Municipal Theatre was bombed in 1938, the newspaper archives were lost, and the paintings of the Sant Cristòfor School were destroyed or dispersed.

The Zong population, which had formed the administrative and commercial elite, evacuated disproportionately during the siege — Zong naval convoys carried civilians to safety, and those with means and connections secured passage. The Sinchewese and Black Balboan populations, concentrated in the lower-lying neighbourhoods and employed in manual occupations, lacked the resources to depart and bore the brunt of the famine, bombing, and deprivation. The Zong share of the population collapsed from 15% to 2%. The territory that emerged from the siege was overwhelmingly Black and mixed Balboan.

The post-war period and the AC mandate (1950–1960) would see the city rebuilt and transformed once again. The **Second Star** — the cultural and economic flowering of the mandate period — produced a second wave of skyscraper construction as the Black capitalist class that emerged during the mandate invested in the city's commercial core, building new towers that surpassed the Zong-era heights and reshaped the skyline in a distinctly Balboan architectural idiom.

### AC mandate period (1950–1960)

#### Return to elected government

The mandate administration restored the city's elected government, replacing the Zong-era Grand Administrator with an elected **mayor** and a **city council**. The return to democratic municipal government was one of the mandate's first acts, reflecting the administration's commitment to self-governance and its break with the colonial autocracy of the Zong period. The first mayoral election was held in 1951, and the new mayor and council took office in the same year.

The restored municipal government had greater authority than the old mayoral system of the First Republic — the mandate administration devolved significant powers over planning, housing, and services to the city council, and the mayor had genuine executive authority rather than serving as a broker for commercial interests. The council's composition reflected the territory's new demographics: the majority of councillors were Black or mixed Balboan, a dramatic change from the white-dominated councils of the republican and Zong eras.

#### Rebuild strategy

The AC mandate administration inherited a devastated city. The siege had destroyed much of the bay shore infrastructure, the residential districts were in ruins, and the population was diminished and displaced. The mandate's first priority was reconstruction — restoring the basic functions of the city and enabling the return of the displaced population.

The rebuild strategy was shaped by the mandate administration's determination to create a city that would not repeat the spatial inequalities of the Shining Star era. The Zong-era city had been two cities in one — a prosperous core and a neglected periphery — and the mandate planners sought to create a more integrated urban fabric. The strategy had three principal elements: the restoration of the city's historic infrastructure, the creation of a new boulevard system, and the adoption of the "mini city" concept for residential planning.

#### Restoration of the Grand Train Terminal

The Grand Train Terminal — the city's most important building and the hub of its transport network — had survived the siege with its reinforced concrete frame intact but its façades, interior, and train shed gutted. The mandate administration made the terminal's restoration its first major project, recognising that the city's recovery depended on restoring the transport links that the terminal provided.

The restoration was completed in 1953. The mandate architects preserved the Beaux-Arts exterior — the stone façades, the clock tower, the vaulted train shed — while modernising the interior to accommodate the expanded commuter rail services and the restored Metro lines. The ferry plaza was rebuilt and enlarged, and the terminal resumed its role as the beating heart of the city's commercial life. The restoration was widely celebrated as a symbol of the city's resilience.

#### The boulevard system and the "mini city" concept

The most innovative element of the rebuild strategy was the **boulevard system** adopted in Sant Cristòfor Central (the former Original). The mandate planners, influenced by contemporary modernist urban theory, proposed a system of wide, tree-lined boulevards that would divide the old city into a series of discrete neighbourhood blocks — each block a self-contained "mini city" with its own shops, schools, community facilities, and public spaces, bounded by the boulevards on all sides.

The boulevards served multiple functions: they were transport corridors (carrying streetcars, automobiles, and pedestrians), they were commercial streets (with shops and services lining both sides), and they were social boundaries (defining the edges of each neighbourhood). The concept was that each mini city would function as a village within the city — residents could meet most of their daily needs within their own block, while the boulevards connected the blocks to one another and to the wider city.

The system was adopted in Sant Cristòfor Central, where the war damage had created an opportunity to reshape the street grid. The old city's narrow, organic streets had been largely destroyed; the mandate planners replaced them with a rational grid of boulevards enclosing neighbourhoods of mixed-use blocks. The mini city concept was less applicable to the other ciutats — the outer suburbs had developed along rail corridors and were too dispersed for the grid system — but it became the defining feature of the central city's post-war landscape.

#### Merger of Pandao and Barcelona

The peninsula's two ciutats — **Pandao** (bay side) and **Barcelona** (ocean side) — were merged into a single ciutat during the mandate period. The merger was driven by administrative efficiency: the peninsula's population had declined sharply during the war, and the mandate administration concluded that two separate ciutats with separate councils were unnecessary. The merged ciutat retained the name **Barcelona**, which was the larger of the two, though the Pandao neighbourhood continued to be known by its historic name.

The merger was controversial. Pandao's Black middle-class community, which had built a distinct identity during the Shining Star era, objected to the loss of their ciutat's name and the absorption into Barcelona, which they associated with the peninsula's older, more mixed population. The Pandao neighbourhood's character was preserved within the merged ciutat, but the loss of institutional recognition was a grievance that lingered for decades.

#### The Eastern Highway

The most significant infrastructure project of the mandate period was the construction of the **Eastern Highway** (Autopista de Llevant), a limited-access highway that ran along the eastern border of Sant Cristòfor Central. The highway was built on the line of the old city's eastern edge — the boundary between the built-up core and the expanding suburbs — and it served as both a transport corridor and a physical boundary between the central city and the outer ciutats.

The Eastern Highway was designed to move traffic quickly between the Grand Train Terminal, the port, and the suburban rail corridors without forcing it through the dense street grid of the central city. It was built as an elevated expressway on reinforced concrete columns, running above the street level and crossing the boulevards on overpasses. The highway dramatically improved the flow of goods and commuters between the city centre and the suburbs, but it also created a physical barrier between Sant Cristòfor Central and the eastern ciutats — a wall of concrete and traffic that cut off the old city from the neighbourhoods that had grown up around it.

The highway's impact on the adjacent neighbourhoods was severe. The blocks immediately to the east of the highway — predominantly working-class and Black — suffered from noise, pollution, and the loss of connectivity to the city centre. The elevated structure cast long shadows and created dead spaces underneath that attracted crime and neglect. The Eastern Highway became the most criticised element of the mandate's rebuild strategy, and its negative effects on the surrounding neighbourhoods would be a focus of urban policy for decades.

#### The banned coastal highway

The mandate administration also considered a proposal to build a **coastal highway** along the Shendan Ocean coast — a limited-access expressway that would run from Costa Nord southward through Barcelona and along the oceanfront to Mare de Déu del Mar. The proposal was advanced by the transport planners as a means of connecting the oceanfront ciutats to the city centre and relieving congestion on the existing coastal roads.

The proposal was rejected — and subsequently banned by the mandate administration — on the grounds that the highway would destroy the character of the oceanfront. The Shendan Ocean coast was the city's most valuable natural asset: the beaches, the cliffs, and the ocean views that had attracted residents to Costa Nord and Barcelona for decades. A coastal highway would require the demolition of homes, the destruction of beaches, and the permanent severing of the oceanfront from the city. The mandate administration, influenced by the growing environmental and preservation movements of the period, prohibited the construction of any limited-access highway along the Shendan Ocean coast — a prohibition that was later incorporated into the city's planning code and remains in force.

The ban forced the city to develop alternative transport solutions for the oceanfront — including the extension of the streetcar network and, later, the Metro — and it preserved the coastal character that distinguishes Sant Cristòfor from other cities that sacrificed their waterfronts to highway construction.

#### The Second Star

The mandate period's cultural and economic flowering — known as the **Second Star** — transformed the city's skyline once again. The Black capitalist class that emerged during the mandate, taking over the enterprises abandoned by the departing Zong population, invested in the city's commercial core. New skyscrapers were built in Sant Cristòfor Central, surpassing the Zong-era heights and reshaping the skyline in a distinctly Balboan architectural idiom — less ornate than the Zong commercial style, more influenced by modernist principles and by the aesthetic traditions of the Black Balboan community.

The Second Star also produced the city's most significant body of Black Balboan literature, visual art, and music. The *No Return* movement, which emerged in 1952, was the political expression of the Black Balboan community's determination to resist a return to the rump state's racial hierarchy. The movement's mass demonstrations — including a protest of 100,000 residents in 1958 — forced the Adonian Community to impose the **Twelve Guarantees** as a condition of the return of the mandate territory to Balboa in 1960.

#### The airport

The city's original airport had been located in Dàrsena, built in 1928 on reclaimed land along the bay shore. The airport was destroyed during the Great Adonian War — the runways were cratered, the terminal was bombed, and the surrounding infrastructure was devastated. The Dàrsena site was too damaged and too hemmed in by urban development to be rebuilt.

The airport was rebuilt in **Ciutat de l'Estació** in 1954, on the open land south of the Grand Train Terminal that had been occupied by the rail yards and industrial depots. The new site offered the space needed for modern runways and terminal facilities, and its position adjacent to the rail network made it accessible from the city centre. The airport was also intended to serve the **International City of Areza**, the AC-administered city in the canal zone, which had no airport of its own — the Ciutat de l'Estació airport provided the air link for the AC institutions and diplomatic community based at Areza.

### Post-mandate period and reunification (1960–1973)

#### Return to Balboan sovereignty

The mandate territory was returned to Balboa in 1960, bringing Sant Cristòfor under Balboan sovereignty for the first time since 1899. The return was conditional on the implementation of the **Twelve Guarantees**, which established full civil rights, anti-discrimination protections, and reparations for descendants of enslaved persons. The **Balboan People's Party** (BPP), which had the support of the Black majority, won the first post-Guarantees elections and began the process of integrating the former mandate territory into the Balboan state.

The transition was not seamless. The former mandate territory had developed its own institutions, its own commercial class, and its own political culture during the Zong and mandate periods — all shaped by the relative egalitarianism of the Shining Star and Second Star eras. Integrating this society into the Balboan state, which was still dominated by the planter oligarchy of the rump period, created tensions that would take years to resolve. The Twelve Guarantees provided the legal framework, but the practical reality of reunification required a fundamental restructuring of the territory's governance and administration.

#### Territorial reorganisation and the shrinkage of Sant Cristòfor

The most significant administrative change of the post-mandate period was the reorganisation of the territory's boundaries. Under Zong rule, "Sant Cristòfor" had referred to a vast administrative district encompassing the modern comarcas of Sant Cristòfor and Cumará, as well as parts of Baix Llevant and Els Aiguamolls — a territory far larger than the city itself. The mandate administration had maintained this arrangement, but the Balboan government determined that it was unsustainable: the territory was too large, too diverse, and too difficult to administer as a single unit.

Between 1961 and 1963, the Balboan government carried out a comprehensive **territorial reorganisation** that broke up the old Zong-era district into its constituent parts. The areas that had been part of the Zong territory but were not part of the city proper were detached and organised into separate comarcas: **Cumará**, **Baix Llevant**, and **Els Aiguamolls** each became independent administrative units with their own comarca governments. The city of Sant Cristòfor was reduced to its modern comarca boundaries — the area that corresponded to the actual urban footprint of the city and its immediate suburbs.

The shrinkage was controversial. The city lost control of the rural areas, the canal corridor settlements, and the agricultural zones that had been part of the Zong-era territory. The Grand Administrator — and later the mayor — had governed a territory of over two million people; the new comarca government presided over a smaller, more urban population. But the reorganisation also simplified governance and gave the city a more coherent administrative identity, focused on the urban core rather than the sprawling rural periphery.

#### Reorganisation into the modern ciutat structure

The post-mandate period also saw the formalisation of the city's current **ciutat** structure. The Zong-era division of the city into five ciutats (Sant Cristòfor Central, Dàrsena, Costa Nord, Barcelona, Pandao) had been modified during the mandate (Pandao and Barcelona merged), and the outer suburbs had developed organically along the rail corridors. The Balboan government regularised this arrangement, formally establishing the current system of ciutats, each with its own local council and defined boundaries:

* **Original** — the historic core, formerly Sant Cristòfor Central; the name "Original" was given for the first time, meaning "the original Sant Cristòfor," to distinguish the historic core from the larger city
* **Barcelona** — the peninsula, including the former Pandao neighbourhood
* **Sant Martí** — the eastern suburb, grown around the commuter rail junction
* **Dàrsena** — the southern industrial and canal district
* **Costa Nord** — the oceanfront residential district
* **Sant Llorenç** — the industrial suburb south of Dàrsena
* **Sant Jordi** — the residential suburb east of the city
* **Ciutat de l'Estació** — the rail yard and airport district
* **Sant Miquel** — the hill district, east of the city
* **Mare de Déu del Mar** — the coastal district south of Barcelona on the Shendan Ocean coast
* **Ciutat del Pujol** — the hill district, southeast of the city

Each ciutat elected its own council, which had authority over local services, planning, and community matters. The city-wide mayor and city council retained authority over cross-ciutat issues — transport, major infrastructure, and economic development.

#### Metro expansion: Lines 3, 4, and 5

The post-mandate period saw the most significant expansion of the Metro system since the Zong era. Three new lines were built, transforming the network from a central-city system into a city-wide transit network:

* **Line 3** — the north–south connector whose construction had been halted by the Great Adonian War. The partially built tunnels, which had served as air-raid shelters during the siege, were reopened and completed. Line 3 ran north–south through Original, connecting the northern neighbourhoods of Costa Nord to the southern districts of Dàrsena and Sant Llorenç via the Grand Train Terminal. The line was completed and opened in 1964, providing the first direct north–south Metro link and dramatically reducing journey times between the oceanfront and the bay shore.

* **Line 4** — a new line running east from the Grand Train Terminal through the growing suburbs of Sant Martí and Sant Jordi, serving the working-class residential districts that had developed along the commuter rail corridors. Line 4 opened in 1968, providing Metro service to the outer ciutats for the first time and reducing the dependence on the commuter rail for east–west travel.

* **Line 5** — the first Metro line to reach the peninsula. Rather than tunnelling under the bay, Line 5 ran around the northern shore of the bay from the Grand Train Terminal, crossing to the peninsula via a surface route along the bay mouth. The line connected Original to Barcelona and the Pandao neighbourhood, providing the first rail link between the mainland and the peninsula — a connection that the ferry and the streetcar network had never adequately provided. Line 5 opened in 1971 and was an immediate success, dramatically reducing travel times between the peninsula and the city centre and accelerating the development of the Barcelona ciutat.

The three new lines, combined with the extensions of Lines 1 and 2 into the outer ciutats, transformed the Metro from a Zong-era central-city system into a comprehensive transit network serving the entire city. The expansion was funded by the Balboan government with support from the Adonian Community, as part of the broader reconstruction and integration programme.

#### Merger of the streetcar networks

The three private streetcar companies that had operated since the Zong period — the Sant Cristòfor Tramway Company, the Costa Nord Light Railway, and the Pandao Tram Company — had survived the war and the mandate, but they were increasingly unsustainable. The networks were ageing, the fare structures were incompatible, and the companies had no capital to modernise their rolling stock or expand their routes. The **Costa Nord Light Railway** became insolvent in 1963, unable to maintain its tracks or pay its workers, and the other two companies were in similar financial condition.

The city government, under the BPP mayor, intervened. In 1964, the three streetcar companies were merged into the **Metro de Sant Cristòfor** system, bringing the streetcars under public ownership for the first time. The merger created a unified transport authority — the **Autoritat de Transport de Sant Cristòfor** (ATS) — which operated the Metro, the commuter rail, and the streetcar networks under a single administration. The ATS introduced a unified fare system across all three modes, ending the decades of separate fares that had been a persistent grievance for commuters. The streetcar networks were modernised with new rolling stock, and the routes were rationalised to complement the expanding Metro system rather than compete with it.

#### Heavy industry in Dàrsena

The post-mandate period saw the rapid expansion of heavy industry in Dàrsena. The ciutat's canal network — the waterways cut during the First Republic to allow boats direct access to warehouses — made it the natural location for industrial expansion. The mandate-era rebuild had restored the canals and the warehouses, but the BPP government's industrialisation programme pushed Dàrsena further: steel mills, chemical plants, and manufacturing facilities were built along the canal banks, transforming the district from a warehousing and dock district into the city's industrial heartland.

The industrial expansion brought jobs but also pollution. The canals, which had been designed for small barges and warehouse access, were now flanked by smokestacks and effluent outfalls. The water quality in the southern bay deteriorated as industrial waste was discharged into the canals and thence into the bay. The residential neighbourhoods of Dàrsena — the working-class housing that had been built for the dockworkers and warehouse labourers — were now sandwiched between the factories and the canals, and the residents suffered from air pollution, contaminated water, and the constant noise of industrial operations.

#### Depopulation of Original and suburban flight

The same period saw a significant population shift from Original to the outer ciutats. The historic core, which had been the city's only neighbourhood for centuries, was increasingly seen as overcrowded, expensive, and undesirable for family life. The boulevard system and the mini city concept had improved the urban fabric, but Original remained a dense commercial district — the skyscrapers, the port, the Grand Train Terminal, and the commercial institutions left little room for the kind of spacious residential life that families could find in the suburbs.

The expansion of the Metro and the commuter rail made it possible to live in Sant Martí, Sant Jordi, or Sant Llorenç and commute to work in Original in minutes. The outer ciutats offered cheaper housing, more space, and — for Black Balboan families, who had been crowded into the Southern District and the exurbs for generations — the opportunity to own a home with a yard for the first time. The population of Original declined steadily throughout the 1960s, as families moved outward and the commercial core expanded into the vacated residential blocks.

The depopulation was most acute in the old Southern District, which had been the Black residential quarter since the six-district ordinance of 1866. The canal construction and the industrial expansion of Dàrsena had already displaced much of the community; now the remaining residents were drawn to the suburbs by the promise of better housing and the Metro connections that made the commute feasible. The Southern District's population fell sharply, and many of its buildings were converted to commercial or industrial use or left vacant.

#### Rising crime

The depopulation of Original and the rapid, unplanned growth of the outer ciutats produced a surge in crime during the 1960s. Original's commercial core, increasingly empty of residents after dark, became a target for burglary and vandalism. The vacant buildings in the old Southern District attracted squatters, drug dealing, and informal economies that operated outside the law. The Eastern Highway, which had been built to move traffic efficiently, also created dead spaces underneath and along its edges that became havens for criminal activity.

The outer ciutats had their own problems. The rapid suburban growth outpaced the deployment of police and social services, and the new neighbourhoods — built quickly and cheaply to house the departing population of Original — lacked the community institutions that had provided informal social control in the old districts. The Black community's mutual aid societies and religious brotherhoods, which had been the backbone of the Southern District for a century, were weakened by the dispersal of their members across the suburbs.

The rising crime became a political issue. The BPP government, which had been elected on a platform of equality and opportunity, was criticised for failing to maintain public safety in the city. The government responded with increased police funding and community programmes, but the underlying causes — depopulation, industrial pollution, inadequate services in the suburbs, and the loss of community cohesion — were structural problems that could not be solved by policing alone.

#### The road to full reunification

The period between 1960 and 1973 was one of gradual integration. The Balboan government, under BPP leadership, implemented the land redistribution programme that replaced the white-owned plantations with agricultural cooperatives, and the reparations programme that provided direct payments, educational scholarships, and capital endowments to descendants of enslaved persons. The canal, however, remained under international administration — the AC Canal Commission continued to operate the waterway, and the International City of Areza remained a separate entity.

Full reunification was achieved with the **Treaty of Areza** (1973), which returned the canal to Balboan sovereignty and established the Balboa Canal Authority as the operating agency. The treaty confirmed the Twelve Guarantees and the non-discrimination provisions that governed the canal's operation, and it marked the formal end of the international administration of the isthmus. Sant Cristòfor, for the first time since 1899, was fully part of a sovereign Balboan state.

### Post-reunification era (1973–2000)

#### The suburban boom

The Treaty of Areza and the full return of the canal to Balboan sovereignty coincided with a period of sustained economic growth that fuelled a massive suburban expansion. The outer ciutats — Sant Martí, Sant Jordi, Sant Llorenç, Sant Miquel, and Ciutat del Pujol — boomed as families continued to move out of Original and Dàrsena in search of larger homes, better schools, and cleaner air. The population of the outer ciutats grew rapidly, and new residential developments — single-family homes, townhouse complexes, and garden apartment blocks — spread across the coastal plain and into the foothills.

The suburban boom was driven by the same forces that had produced the depopulation of Original in the 1960s, but on a larger scale. The Metro extensions and the commuter rail network made the suburbs accessible, and the BPP government's housing policies encouraged homeownership among the Black and mixed-race populations that had been renters for generations. The result was a city that was increasingly decentralised — the commercial core remained in Original, but the majority of the population now lived in the outer ciutats.

#### The new airport and Areza International

The Ciutat de l'Estació airport, rebuilt in 1954, had served the city well during the mandate period, but by the 1970s it was inadequate for the city's needs. The growth of commercial aviation and the introduction of larger, longer-range aircraft required longer runways and more extensive terminal facilities than the cramped Ciutat de l'Estació site could provide. The airport was also increasingly incompatible with its urban surroundings — the noise and traffic of a growing airport were a burden on the adjacent residential neighbourhoods.

In 1978, the Balboan government approved the construction of a new airport in **Sant Martí**, on the open land east of the city where the coastal plain offered space for modern runways and terminal complexes. The new **Aeroport de Sant Martí** opened in 1985. The Sant Martí site was chosen for its proximity to the city (connected by Metro Line 1, which was extended to the airport), its distance from the densely populated core, and its capacity for future expansion.

The old Ciutat de l'Estació airport, however, did not close. It was redesignated **Areza International Airport**, dedicated to serving the International City of Areza and the AC institutions in the canal zone. The Balboan government objected strenuously — it did not want an airport maintained solely for Areza, and the Ciutat de l'Estació site was prime industrial land adjacent to the Grand Train Terminal and the city's rail network, exactly the kind of location that the city needed for industrial expansion. The Balboan government wanted the airport closed and the land released for development. But the Adonian Community insisted on maintaining a dedicated air link for Areza, and the Balboan government lacked the leverage to refuse.

The compromise was that the AC would pay Sant Cristòfor to build **Metro Line 9**, a new line connecting the Aeroport de Sant Martí directly to Areza via the canal corridor. Once Line 9 was completed, passengers for Areza could fly into Sant Martí and take the Metro directly to the International City, eliminating the need for a separate airport. The AC funded the construction, and Line 9 was completed in 2003. Areza International Airport closed the same year, and the Ciutat de l'Estació site was finally redeveloped into a commercial and residential district, with the terminal building converted into a shopping centre and the runways replaced by housing and parkland.

#### Metro expansion: Line 1 extension, Line 9, and Lines 6, 7, 8

The post-reunification period saw the most significant expansion of the Metro system since the post-mandate era. The most significant completed project was the **extension of Line 1** to the new Aeroport de Sant Martí, providing a direct rail link from the city centre to the airport. The extension opened in 1986, a year after the airport itself, and became one of the system's busiest segments.

**Line 9** — the Areza connection — was the next line built. Funded by the Adonian Community, Line 9 ran from the Aeroport de Sant Martí westward through the city and then south along the canal corridor to Areza. The line was prioritised over Lines 6, 7, and 8 because of the AC funding, and it was completed in 2003. The opening of Line 9 allowed the closure of Areza International Airport and provided the first direct Metro link between the city and the canal zone.

Three other new lines were planned and begun during this period, though all three faced significant opposition and were completed after Line 9:

* **Line 6** — a second line to the peninsula, running around the southern shore of the bay from Dàrsena to Barcelona. The line was intended to provide a second rail connection to the peninsula, supplementing Line 5's northern route around the bay. Construction began in 1988, but the project faced opposition from residents of the southern bay shore neighbourhoods, who objected to the disruption of construction and the prospect of increased traffic in their communities. The line was completed and opened in 1995, seven years behind schedule.

* **Line 7** — a planned line serving the hill districts of Sant Miquel and Ciutat del Pujol. The line was designed to connect the wealthy hill communities to the city centre, but it faced opposition from two directions: hill residents who did not want the Metro in their neighbourhoods, and community activists who argued that the line served wealthy areas while the working-class suburbs of Sant Llorenç and Sant Jordi still lacked adequate transit. Construction began in 1992 but was repeatedly delayed by legal challenges and community protests. The line was not completed until the 2000s.

* **Line 8** — a planned line serving the southern industrial corridor from Dàrsena through Sant Llorenç and into the developing areas south of the city. The line was the most controversial of the three: it ran through the industrial district, where the factories and chemical plants of Dàrsena were beginning to close, and the projected ridership was uncertain. Construction began in 1995 but was slowed by the deindustrialisation of the corridor, which reduced the demand for transit service even as it created demand for new connections to the replacement employers. The line was not completed until the 2000s.

#### Deindustrialisation and the move to the canal zone

The 1980s and 1990s saw the progressive deindustrialisation of Dàrsena. The steel mills, chemical plants, and manufacturing facilities that had been built along the canal banks during the post-mandate industrialisation programme were increasingly uncompetitive — older, dirtier, and less efficient than the new industrial facilities being built in the canal zone, where the Balboa Canal Authority was developing modern industrial parks with better infrastructure and access to the canal's transport links.

One by one, the factories closed. The steel mill shut in 1982; the chemical plant followed in 1987; the last of the major manufacturing facilities closed in the early 1990s. The closures eliminated thousands of jobs and left a district of empty factories, contaminated land, and polluted canals. The deindustrialisation was a severe blow to Dàrsena's working-class population, who had lived alongside the factories for decades and who now faced unemployment in a city whose economy was increasingly oriented toward finance, services, and the canal trade.

The industrial activity did not disappear — it moved. The Balboa Canal Authority's industrial parks in the canal zone, near Portnou and Areza, attracted the manufacturing and processing operations that had once been based in Dàrsena. The canal zone offered better transport links, modern facilities, and the administrative advantages of being within the canal corridor. The shift of industry from the city to the canal zone was part of the broader economic reorientation of the isthmus after the Treaty of Areza, which concentrated the canal-related economic activity in the corridor rather than in the city.

#### Restoration of Original and the Dàrsena warehouse conversion

The deindustrialisation of Dàrsena and the depopulation of Original created an opportunity for urban renewal. Beginning in the late 1980s, the city government launched a programme of **cleanup and restoration** in Original, targeting the vacant buildings, the contaminated sites, and the deteriorated infrastructure that had accumulated during decades of neglect.

The old Southern District — the historic Black residential quarter that had been bisected by the canals and hollowed out by depopulation — was the focus of the restoration effort. The city government invested in street improvements, park creation, and the renovation of the surviving historic buildings. The canals, which had been industrial waterways for a century, were cleaned and their banks were landscaped, transforming them from industrial corridors into a waterfront amenity. The restoration was gradual — it would take two decades to complete — but it reversed the decline of the neighbourhood and began to attract residents back to Original.

In Dàrsena, the empty warehouses and factories presented a different opportunity. As the industrial tenants departed, property developers recognised the potential of the large, open-plan buildings with their canal frontage and their proximity to the city centre. Beginning in the early 1990s, the warehouses along the canals were converted into **loft apartments**, artists' studios, restaurants, and retail spaces. The warehouse conversions were initially driven by artists and young professionals who were attracted by the cheap rents and the industrial character of the buildings, but the area rapidly gentrified as the conversions proved popular with a wider market.

The Dàrsena warehouse conversion became one of the city's most celebrated urban regeneration projects, transforming a derelict industrial district into a fashionable waterfront neighbourhood. It was also controversial: the working-class families who had lived in Dàrsena for generations were increasingly priced out of the neighbourhood as property values rose, and the gentrification displaced the very community that had given the district its character.

#### Slow repopulation of Original

The restoration of Original and the Dàrsena warehouse conversions produced a slow but steady repopulation of the city centre. Young professionals, attracted by the character of the restored neighbourhoods and the convenience of living close to the commercial core, began moving back into Original in the 1990s. The population of the ciutat, which had fallen to its lowest point in the 1970s, began to recover — slowly, and with a very different demographic profile than the community that had departed.

The new residents of Original were predominantly middle-class, professional, and younger than the families who had moved to the suburbs. They were drawn by the urban lifestyle — the restaurants, the cultural institutions, the walkability — rather than by the community ties that had sustained the old Southern District. The repopulation was a sign of the city centre's revival, but it also represented a social transformation: the Black working-class community that had been the heart of Original for over a century was now a shrinking presence in a neighbourhood that was increasingly white and affluent.

### The urban renaissance (2000–2015)

#### Rapid growth

The turn of the millennium marked the beginning of a period of rapid growth for Sant Cristòfor. The city's economy, which had been reorienting toward finance and services since the deindustrialisation of Dàrsena, accelerated as the Balboa Canal Authority's expansion and the growth of the International City of Areza created demand for banking, insurance, legal, and professional services. The city's population, which had been declining for decades, began to grow again — not just in the outer ciutats but in the city centre, where the restoration of Original and the Dàrsena warehouse conversions were attracting new residents.

The growth was most visible in the skyline. The Second Star skyscrapers, which had been the tallest buildings in the city since the mandate period, were joined by a new generation of towers — glass and steel, designed by international architects, built to house the financial institutions and professional firms that were making Sant Cristòfor one of Lurandia's principal commercial centres. The city's architectural character shifted from the ornate Zong commercial style and the modernist mandate-era buildings to the international style of the global financial era.

#### Nightlife culture

The urban renaissance was also a cultural renaissance. The Dàrsena warehouse conversions of the 1990s had created a district of loft apartments, galleries, and performance spaces that attracted artists, musicians, and creative professionals. By the early 2000s, Dàrsena had become the city's nightlife centre — a district of bars, clubs, live music venues, and restaurants that operated well into the morning hours, drawing crowds from across the city and from the suburbs.

The nightlife culture was a source of both pride and tension. The clubs and bars of Dàrsena were widely celebrated as evidence of the city's vitality and its cosmopolitan character — a continuation of the Shining Star era's tradition of cultural mixing and commercial energy. The music scene, which blended istmo son with electronic, Zong, and Kaftian influences, produced a distinctive Sant Cristòfor sound that attracted international attention.

But the nightlife also generated conflict. The residents of Dàrsena — the working-class families who had not been priced out by the warehouse conversions — objected to the noise, the late hours, the public drunkenness, and the traffic that the clubs brought to their neighbourhood. The complaints were not new — the Shining Star era's Canal District had faced similar tensions — but the scale of the 2000s nightlife scene was far larger, and the conflict between the district's residential and commercial characters became a persistent political issue.

#### Government backlash

The city government's response to the nightlife culture was increasingly hostile. Under both BPP and Liberal administrations, the city imposed progressively stricter regulations on bars and clubs: earlier closing times, noise restrictions, licensing requirements, and zoning changes that restricted the areas where nightlife venues could operate. The regulations were framed as quality-of-life measures, but the nightlife operators and the creative community saw them as an attack on the city's cultural life and on the economic activity that the nightlife generated.

The backlash was driven in part by the same forces that were reshaping the city's demographics. The new residents of Dàrsena and Original — the professionals and the affluent who had moved into the warehouse conversions and the restored buildings — were less tolerant of noise and disruption than the working-class community that had lived alongside the industrial district for decades. The government's regulations reflected the preferences of the new constituency, not the old.

The artists and musicians who had been the pioneers of the Dàrsena conversion found themselves squeezed: the rising rents pushed them out of the district they had helped create, and the government's regulations restricted the informal venues and performance spaces where they had worked. The creative community that had driven the urban renaissance was, by the 2010s, increasingly dispersed to the outer ciutats or to the cheaper neighbourhoods of Sant Llorenç and Sant Jordi.

#### Municipal politics

The city's political history from the 1960s through the 2000s was shaped by the competition between the **Balboan People's Party** (BPP) and the **Liberal Party**. The BPP, which had dominated the city since the first post-Guarantees elections, held the mayoralty continuously through the 1960s and 1970s. The BPP's base was the Black working-class community and the descendants of the Second Star professional class — the constituencies that had built the city's post-war identity.

In the 1980s, the city elected its first **Liberal Party** mayor, reflecting the growing influence of the professional and commercial classes that had benefited from the city's economic transformation. The Liberals held the mayoralty through the 1980s and 1990s, a period dominated by the deindustrialisation of Dàrsena, the construction of the new airport, and the beginning of the urban restoration programmes. The Liberal administration was broadly pro-development and pro-business, and it was under the Liberals that the warehouse conversion programme and the Original restoration were initiated.

From 2000 onward, the mayoralty alternated between the BPP and the Liberals, reflecting the city's increasingly divided politics. The BPP drew support from the Black working-class communities in the outer ciutats and from the constituencies that were most affected by gentrification and displacement; the Liberals drew support from the professional classes, the financial sector, and the newer residents of the city centre. The alternation produced a stop-start pattern of policy: BPP administrations expanded social programmes and tenants' protections, while Liberal administrations promoted development and relaxed regulations.

#### The 2003 parliament bombing

On 12 March 2003, a bomb detonated underneath the **National Assembly building** in Original, where it sat above the tunnel of Metro Line 5. The explosion tore through the Metro tunnel, collapsing a section of the tunnel and the street above, and severely damaged the Assembly building's foundations. Thirty-one people were killed — mostly Metro passengers and Assembly staff — and over two hundred were injured. The Assembly was in session at the time; several assembly members were injured but none were killed, as the blast was absorbed by the building's foundations rather than reaching the chamber.

The bomber was **Adrià Vallmajor i Soler**, a lone white nationalist acting over social grievances. Vallmajor, a 34-year-old resident of Tramuntana, had been radicalised by the white nationalist fringe that opposed the Twelve Guarantees and the post-reunification political order. His manifesto, recovered from his apartment after the bombing, denounced the Guarantees, the reparations programme, and what he called the "racial dispossession" of white Balboans. He chose the Assembly building as his target as the symbol of the political order he opposed, and he chose to place the bomb in the Metro tunnel beneath it because the tunnel provided access to the building's foundations and because the explosion would be amplified by the confined space.

The bombing was the worst act of political violence in Balboa since the Great Adonian War. It prompted a massive security response — the Metro was closed for a week, the Assembly building was evacuated and closed for months, and the government deployed the Balboan Defence Forces to guard key infrastructure. The subsequent investigation led to the arrest of several associates of Vallmajor who had knowledge of his plans, though no broader conspiracy was identified.

The bombing also had lasting effects on the city's infrastructure. The damaged section of Line 5 was closed for eleven months while the tunnel was rebuilt and reinforced. The Assembly building was restored, but the Metro tunnel beneath it was reinforced with blast-resistant structures — a measure that was subsequently applied to all Metro tunnels passing under government buildings. The security measures introduced after the bombing — bag searches at Metro stations, surveillance cameras, and the deployment of transit police — became permanent features of the system.

#### Completion of Lines 6, 7, and 8

The most significant infrastructure achievement of the period was the completion of the Metro lines that had been delayed for years. Under the BPP administration elected in 2005, construction on **Lines 6, 7, and 8** was restarted with renewed funding and political commitment. The three lines had been stalled for years by opposition, legal challenges, and uncertain ridership projections; the BPP mayor made their completion a signature policy, arguing that the working-class suburbs that had been neglected by the Liberal administrations deserved the same transit access as the wealthy hill districts.

All three lines were completed within a decade:

* **Line 6** — the second peninsula line, running around the southern bay shore from Dàrsena to Barcelona — was completed in 2008, finally providing the second rail connection to the peninsula that had been planned since the 1980s.

* **Line 7** — the hill district line, serving Sant Miquel and Ciutat del Pujol — was completed in 2012, after years of opposition from hill residents and community activists. The line was less controversial by the time it opened, as the hill districts had grown significantly and the demand for transit had increased.

* **Line 8** — the southern industrial corridor, from Dàrsena through Sant Llorenç — was completed in 2015. The line's ridership had been uncertain during the deindustrialisation period, but the residential development of Sant Llorenç and the Dàrsena warehouse conversions had generated sufficient demand to justify the line by the time it opened.

The completion of Lines 6, 7, and 8, together with the earlier Lines 3, 4, 5, and 9, gave Sant Cristòfor a nine-line Metro system — one of the most extensive in Lurandia.

### Recent period (2015–present)

#### Street safety programmes

In 2015, the city launched a comprehensive **street safety programme** in response to rising pedestrian fatalities, particularly in the dense commercial core of Original and along the Eastern Highway. The programme lowered speed limits across the city, redesigned dangerous intersections, expanded pedestrian crossings, and installed traffic cameras. In Original, the boulevards that formed the boundaries of the mini city blocks were redesigned with raised crosswalks, narrowed lanes, and extended sidewalks — measures that slowed traffic but also reshaped the character of the boulevards, making them more pedestrian-friendly and less oriented toward the automobile.

The programme was controversial with drivers and commercial interests, who argued that the traffic calming measures increased congestion and delayed deliveries. But it was popular with residents of Original, who had long complained that the boulevards were hostile to pedestrians, and with the cycling community, which had been advocating for dedicated bike infrastructure for years.

#### Cycling network

The street safety programme also created the city's first **cycling network** — a system of protected bike lanes running along the avenues and boulevards of Original. The network was built incrementally, lane by lane, as the street safety redesigns created space for cycling infrastructure. The boulevards, which had been widened for automobile traffic in the post-war decades, were narrowed again to accommodate the bike lanes, and the mini city blocks were connected by a grid of cycling routes that allowed residents to traverse Original without a car.

The cycling network was extended into Dàrsena and Costa Nord in subsequent years, though the outer ciutats — with their suburban, car-oriented layouts — remained difficult to serve with cycling infrastructure. The network became a defining feature of Original's urban landscape and a symbol of the city centre's transformation from a car-dominated commercial district to a more livable, multi-modal neighbourhood.

#### The 2016 warehouse fire

On the night of 17 October 2016, a fire broke out in a converted warehouse in Dàrsena. The building — a former industrial warehouse that had been converted into loft apartments and artists' studios — was occupied by a mix of legal tenants and unlicensed squatters who had moved into the unfinished upper floors. The fire spread rapidly through the building's open-plan interior, trapping residents on the upper floors. Twelve people were killed, most of them young artists and workers who had been living in the unlicensed spaces.

The fire was the worst disaster in the city since the Great Adonian War. It exposed the dangerous conditions in the Dàrsena warehouse conversions — the inadequate fire safety, the unlicensed subdivisions, the informal occupancy arrangements that had been tolerated by the city government for years. The investigation revealed that the building had been cited for fire safety violations multiple times but that the city's inspection and enforcement system had failed to follow up.

The fire provoked **mass protests** across the city. Thousands of residents — led by the Dàrsena tenants' associations, the artists' community, and the labour unions — marched through the streets demanding accountability, stricter building safety enforcement, and protections for tenants in converted buildings. The protests were the largest in the city since the No Return demonstrations of the 1950s, and they forced the resignation of the city's building safety director and a comprehensive overhaul of the inspection and enforcement system.

The fire also intensified the debate over gentrification. The victims had been precisely the people who were being squeezed out of Dàrsena by rising rents — the artists, the young workers, the informal tenants who could not afford the legal loft apartments. The fire was seen as a consequence of the city's failure to provide affordable housing and to regulate the warehouse conversion market, and it became a rallying point for the growing movement against displacement.

#### The 2020 pandemic

The Adonian pandemic of 2020 shut the city down. The nightlife district of Dàrsena — the bars, clubs, and restaurants that had been the engine of the neighbourhood's revival — was ordered closed for months. The Metro system, which carried over a million passengers daily, was reduced to minimal service. The office towers of Original emptied as financial institutions and professional firms sent their employees to work from home.

The pandemic was devastating for the city's service economy. Thousands of workers in hospitality, retail, and personal services lost their jobs, and the small businesses that lined the boulevards of Original and the canal-front streets of Dàrsena were forced to close, many permanently. The city government provided emergency relief, but the scale of the economic damage exceeded the municipal resources.

The pandemic also accelerated trends that had been developing for years. The shift to remote work reduced the demand for office space in Original, and several commercial towers reported vacancy rates above 30% by 2022. The depopulation of the city centre, which had been reversed by the urban renaissance, threatened to resume as residents who could work from anywhere chose the space and affordability of the outer ciutats over the density and expense of Original.

#### Election of a socialist mayor (2025)

The political consequences of the pandemic, the warehouse fire, and the gentrification crisis came to a head in the 2025 mayoral election. For the first time since the post-Guarantees era, the city elected a **Socialist Front** (Front Socialista de Balboa, FSB) mayor — breaking the decades-long alternation between the BPP and the Liberals. The FSB candidate, running on a platform of tenants' rights, affordable housing, public investment, and economic justice, defeated both the BPP and Liberal candidates in a result that reflected the city's frustration with the established parties.

The FSB victory was a repudiation of the BPP, which had been the party of Black Balboan political power for over sixty years but which was increasingly seen as part of the establishment it had once opposed. The BPP's base — the Black working-class communities of the outer ciutats — had been eroded by suburbanisation, gentrification, and the party's own failure to address the housing crisis. The FSB coalition drew support from the tenants' movement, the artists' community, the labour unions, and the younger voters who had been most affected by the pandemic and the housing crisis.

The new mayor's agenda included a massive expansion of social housing, rent controls, stricter enforcement of building safety regulations, and public investment in the neighbourhoods that had been left behind by the urban renaissance. The election marked a new chapter in the city's political history — one whose outcome remained uncertain at the time of writing.

#### The Line 1 extension to Barcelona

The most persistent unresolved infrastructure question in the city was the proposed **extension of Line 1 to Barcelona** — a 7 km tunnel under the bay that would provide the first direct Metro link between the mainland and the peninsula without going around the bay. The extension would dramatically reduce travel times between the city centre and Barcelona, and it was widely supported by Barcelona residents, who relied on Line 5's circuitous route around the bay mouth and the ferry for their connections to the mainland.

The city held three referendums on the extension — in **2004**, **2008**, and **2016** — and all three passed with strong majorities. But the extension was never built. The obstacle was not the engineering or the cost — it was the **dockworkers' unions**, particularly the **Maritime Workers' Party** (PTM), which represented the ferry workers and the dockhands who operated the bay crossing. The unions argued that a cross-bay Metro tunnel would eliminate the ferry services that employed hundreds of workers, and that the jobs lost would not be replaced by the construction jobs created during the building phase. The unions organised strikes, lobbied the ATS, and pressured successive mayors to delay or cancel the project.

Each time the referendum passed, the city government commissioned a feasibility study, and each time the study confirmed the project's viability — but the political opposition from the unions was sufficient to prevent construction from beginning. The Liberal administrations were reluctant to confront the unions directly, and the BPP administrations, which depended on union support, were unwilling to advance a project that would cost their base jobs.

The Socialist mayor elected in 2025 pledged to break the impasse, declaring that the Line 1 extension would be built and that any dockworkers who lost their jobs as a result would receive **retraining and guaranteed employment** in the expanded Metro system or in other municipal services. The proposal was the most concrete commitment to the extension in two decades, but it remained to be seen whether the retraining promise would satisfy the unions or whether the opposition would intensify.
